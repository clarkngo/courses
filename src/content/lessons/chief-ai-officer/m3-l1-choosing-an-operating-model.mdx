## Who Does the Work?

Strategy says where AI should create value. The operating model says **who** builds, buys, runs, and governs AI, and how they work together. Get it wrong and you'll either have a central team that's a bottleneck or business units reinventing the same things with no guardrails.

---

## Three Models

### Centralized

A central AI team, often called a center of excellence (CoE), does most AI work for the whole organization.

- **Strengths:** consistent standards, efficient use of scarce talent, strong governance, easier reuse.
- **Weaknesses:** becomes a queue; distant from business problems; business units feel AI is "done to them."
- **Fits:** organizations early in AI maturity, or with very scarce AI skills.

### Federated

Each business unit runs its own AI teams with a light central function for policy.

- **Strengths:** close to the business, fast, strong ownership.
- **Weaknesses:** duplication, inconsistent risk practices, hard to share platforms or talent, difficult to see the whole portfolio.
- **Fits:** very large, diverse organizations with mature business units.

### Hub-and-spoke

A central hub owns platform, standards, governance, and scarce expertise. Spokes, embedded in business units, own use cases and adoption.

- **Strengths:** balances consistency with business ownership; reuse through the hub, speed in the spokes.
- **Weaknesses:** needs clear division of responsibilities and strong relationships; can drift toward either extreme.
- **Fits:** most mid-to-large organizations once AI moves beyond early pilots.

Many organizations **start centralized and move toward hub-and-spoke** as skills spread and standards mature. Plan for that evolution rather than treating the first model as permanent.

---

## What the Hub Owns vs. the Spokes

| The hub (central team) owns | The spokes (business units) own |
|---|---|
| AI strategy, portfolio view, and prioritization process | Use-case identification and business cases |
| Shared platform, model access, and approved tools | Process redesign and change in their area |
| Standards: security, evaluation, documentation | Day-to-day product ownership of their AI solutions |
| Governance, risk tiering, and review | Measuring and reporting value |
| Scarce expertise (ML engineering, evaluation, AI risk) | Domain experts and frontline adoption |
| Enterprise vendor contracts | Local champions and training |

Write this down and agree it with business-unit leaders. Ambiguity about who owns a failing AI product is a common source of conflict.

<Pitfall title="The Center That Builds Everything">

**Common mistake:** a strong central team keeps building every use case itself because it's faster and higher quality than the business units' attempts.

It works for a year. Then demand exceeds capacity, business units feel ignored, and the CAIO's team becomes the reason AI is slow. The hub's long-term job is to **make the spokes capable**: platforms, patterns, training, and review, not delivery of everything.

</Pitfall>

---

## The Core Team

Whatever the model, a CAIO typically needs these capabilities, in-house or through partners:

- **AI product management:** turning business problems into well-scoped AI products with clear metrics.
- **AI and ML engineering:** building, integrating, and operating AI systems, including retrieval, agents, and evaluation.
- **Platform and architecture:** the shared infrastructure, model access, and integration patterns.
- **Data partnership:** close links to data engineering and governance, often in the CDO's team.
- **AI risk and governance:** policy, risk assessment, model documentation, and regulatory tracking.
- **Change and enablement:** training, communications, champions networks, and adoption measurement.

Small, senior, and cross-functional beats large and specialized at the start.
