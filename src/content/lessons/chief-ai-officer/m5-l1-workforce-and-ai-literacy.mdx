## AI Changes Tasks First, Jobs Second

AI rarely replaces a whole job at once. It changes **tasks** within jobs: drafting goes faster, research takes minutes instead of hours, routine cases are handled automatically, and people spend more time on judgment, exceptions, and relationships.

That makes workforce planning a task-level exercise. For each major role family, ask:

- Which tasks can AI **automate**, with little human involvement?
- Which can it **augment**, making people faster or better?
- Which stay **human**, because they depend on judgment, accountability, trust, or physical presence?
- What **new tasks** appear: reviewing AI output, handling exceptions, managing agents, maintaining knowledge bases?

## Decide What Freed Capacity Is For

When AI frees capacity, leaders have three choices:

1. **Grow:** serve more customers, enter new markets, or take on work that was previously unaffordable, with the same team.
2. **Improve:** use time for higher-quality work: more thorough reviews, better customer conversations, faster response.
3. **Reduce:** lower headcount through attrition, redeployment, or reductions.

Most organizations will do some of each. What matters is deciding **deliberately** and saying so. Undeclared intent breeds suspicion, and suspicion kills adoption.

Some companies that announced large AI-driven reductions later reported quality problems and rehired people for customer-facing work. Plan for the human review, exception handling, and relationship work that AI doesn't remove.

<Pitfall title="Silence About Jobs">

**Common mistake:** launching AI programs while avoiding any statement about what it means for employment.

People fill the silence with the worst assumption. Employees who believe AI is coming for their jobs won't share the process knowledge that makes AI work, and won't report its errors. Be as clear as you honestly can about intent, commitments (such as redeployment or retraining), and what you don't yet know.

</Pitfall>

## Role-Based AI Literacy

AI literacy isn't one course for everyone. Different groups need different depth:

<ProcessFlow
  label="Role-based layers of an AI literacy program"
  color="#312e81"
  steps={[

    { title: "Daily users", detail: "Prompting, checking, workflows" },
    { title: "Managers", detail: "Redesigning work, measuring" },

    { title: "Executives and board", detail: "Strategy, risk, oversight" },
  ]}
/>

- **Everyone:** what AI is and isn't, approved tools, what data never goes into AI, how to spot errors, and how to report problems.
- **Daily users:** effective prompting, verifying output, and building AI into their workflow.
- **Managers:** identifying use cases, redesigning team work, setting review standards, and measuring value.
- **Builders:** evaluation, security (including prompt injection), documentation, and the organization's AI standards.
- **Executives and board:** strategy, risk appetite, regulatory exposure, and the right questions to ask.

The EU AI Act's **AI literacy duty** (Article 4) requires providers and deployers to take measures to ensure sufficient AI literacy among staff and others who operate or use AI systems on their behalf, taking into account their roles and the context. A role-based program with records of who completed what is the practical way to show you've met it.

## Building Skills That Stick

- **Learn on real work.** Short sessions using people's own tasks beat generic courses.
- **Champions networks.** Enthusiastic early adopters in each team who share tips and help colleagues.
- **Communities of practice** where people share prompts, workflows, and failures.
- **Manager modeling.** Adoption follows managers who visibly use AI well and talk about its limits.
- **Make time.** People can't learn new ways of working with zero slack. Budget time, not just licenses.

<TryIt title="Map One Role Family">
