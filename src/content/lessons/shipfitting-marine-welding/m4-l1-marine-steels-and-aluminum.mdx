## Ship Steel Is Not Just "Steel"

Hull steel must be **strong**, **weldable**, and **tough**: able to resist cracking when cold and impacted. Classification societies such as **ABS (American Bureau of Shipping)**, **DNV**, and **Lloyd's Register** set grades that steel mills certify.

### Ordinary-Strength Grades

| Grade | Toughness | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Basic | General hull structure in less critical areas |
| **B** | Better | Thicker plates or moderately critical areas |
| **D** | Tested for impact toughness at lower temperature | More critical areas, such as the sheer strake and bilge strake on many ships |
| **E** | Tested at even lower temperature | Most critical areas and cold-service ships |

### Higher-Strength Grades

**AH32, AH36, DH36, EH36** (and others) have higher **yield strength** (the "32" and "36" relate to about 315 and 355 MPa minimum yield) with the same letter-based **toughness** levels. Higher-strength steel lets designers use thinner plate, saving weight.

Grades are **stamped or stenciled** on the plate with the heat number. The **mill certificate** traces it. Using the wrong grade in a critical area is a serious nonconformance.

---

## Why Toughness Matters

Steel can become **brittle** in cold temperatures. Famous early welded ships cracked in cold seas when brittle steel met sharp corners and weld defects. Modern grades, careful **detail design** (no sharp notches), and sound welds prevent it.

---

## Preheat and Hydrogen Control

Hydrogen in a weld can cause **cold cracking** (delayed cracking) in the heat-affected zone, especially in **thick** plate, **higher-strength** steel, and **restrained** joints.

Controls:

- **Low-hydrogen** consumables, stored correctly (Lesson 3.1)
- **Preheat** to the temperature in the welding procedure; it slows cooling and lets hydrogen diffuse out
- **Dry** surfaces; remove moisture, frost, oil, and paint
- Maintain the specified **interpass temperature** range

Preheat requirements depend on the steel's **carbon equivalent**, its **thickness**, and the **hydrogen level** of the process. Measure with **temperature-indicating crayons** or contact thermometers at the specified distance from the joint.

## Marine Aluminum

Aluminum is common for **fast ferries, patrol boats, workboats, fishing vessels, and superstructures**, because it's light and resists seawater corrosion.

| Alloy | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **5083, 5086, 5456** | Plate and sheet | Marine-grade magnesium alloys; good weldability and corrosion resistance |
| **6061, 6082** | Extrusions (stiffeners, profiles) | Heat-treated; lose significant strength in the heat-affected zone when welded |

Common filler wires are **5356** and **5183**.

### Welding Aluminum

- **Clean the oxide**: aluminum forms a tough oxide film that melts at a much higher temperature than the metal underneath. Use a **dedicated stainless steel brush** (never one used on steel) and clean solvent.
- **GMAW (often pulsed spray)** with argon (or argon-helium) shielding; a **push** technique.
- Use **push-pull** or **spool guns** because soft aluminum wire buckles in long conduits.
- Watch for **porosity** (hydrogen from moisture or hydrocarbons) and **hot cracking** (wrong filler).
- No color change warning: aluminum doesn't glow before it melts, so it's easy to burn through.

## Dissimilar Metals and Galvanic Corrosion

When two different metals touch in **seawater**, they form a battery. The **less noble** metal corrodes faster. Aluminum next to steel, bronze, or stainless will corrode.

To join an aluminum superstructure to a steel deck, yards use **bimetallic transition joints**: explosion-bonded strips with steel on one side and aluminum on the other. Steel is welded to steel, and aluminum to aluminum. Keep welding heat from **overheating the bond line**, per the maker's limits.

Isolate other dissimilar contacts with **insulating gaskets, sleeves, and coatings**.

<Pitfall title="One Brush for Everything">

**Common mistake:** using the same wire brush and grinder disc on steel and aluminum.

Steel particles embedded in aluminum create **galvanic corrosion pits**, and contaminants cause **porosity**. Keep **separate, labeled tools** for aluminum, stainless, and carbon steel.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Ship steels are graded for **toughness** (**A, B, D, E**) and **strength** (**AH32/AH36** and up). Control **hydrogen** with low-hydrogen consumables, **preheat**, and dry surfaces. Marine **5xxx** aluminum plate is very weldable; **6xxx** extrusions lose HAZ strength. Clean aluminum oxide with **dedicated tools**. Use **bimetallic transition joints** to join aluminum to steel.
