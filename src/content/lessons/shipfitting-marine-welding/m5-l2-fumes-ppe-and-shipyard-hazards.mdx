## Welding Fumes

Welding fume is a mix of tiny metal oxide particles and gases. What's in it depends on the **base metal, filler, coatings, and process**.

| Source | Hazard |
|---|---|
| **Stainless steel** (and some consumables) | **Hexavalent chromium**, a lung carcinogen. OSHA has a specific chromium (VI) standard for shipyards (**1915.1026**). |
| **Manganese** (in most steel fillers) | Nervous system effects with overexposure |
| **Galvanized (zinc-coated) steel** | Zinc oxide fume: **metal fume fever** (flu-like symptoms) |
| **Old paint** on ships | **Lead** (OSHA lead standard for shipyards, **1915.1025**), and other toxic coatings |
| **Gases** | Ozone (from GMAW/GTAW arcs), carbon monoxide (especially CO₂-shielded processes in confined spaces), nitrogen oxides |
| **Shielding gases** | Argon and CO₂ displace oxygen in low or enclosed spaces |

### Controls, in Order

1. **Remove coatings** from the weld area before welding where possible (using safe methods for the coating).
2. **Local exhaust ventilation**: fume extractors and fume-extracting guns placed near the arc.
3. **General ventilation** in enclosed spaces (blowers, exhaust trunks).
4. **Respiratory protection** where ventilation isn't enough, under a respiratory protection program with fit testing.
5. **Position your head** out of the fume plume.

## Personal Protective Equipment

| Hazard | PPE |
|---|---|
| Arc radiation (UV, IR), spatter | Welding helmet with the correct **shade lens**, safety glasses underneath |
| Burns | **Flame-resistant** clothing (wool or treated cotton; avoid synthetics that melt), leather gloves, jacket or sleeves, boots |
| Cutting and grinding | Face shield over safety glasses, hearing protection |
| Noise | Earplugs or muffs; arc gouging and grinding are very loud |
| Fumes | Respirator when required |
| Falling objects | Hard hat (under or compatible with the welding helmet) |

### Lens Shade

Use the shade recommended for your process and amperage (references such as **ANSI Z49.1** and lens makers publish charts). As a rough guide, many arc welding tasks use roughly **shade 10 to 13**, with higher shades at higher currents. Arc gouging and high-amperage work need darker shades. Auto-darkening helmets must be the right shade and kept working.

Protect **bystanders** with welding screens, because arc flash ("welder's flash") burns the eyes of anyone nearby.

## Electric Shock

Ships are steel, often **wet**, and frequently **cramped**, a perfect setup for electric shock.

- Inspect leads and **electrode holders** for damaged insulation.
- Keep **dry gloves** and clothing; don't lie on wet steel without insulation.
- Make a solid **work lead connection close to the weld**, so current doesn't wander through the hull, piping, bearings, or other machinery (stray current can damage equipment).
- In tight or wet locations, use machines with **voltage-reducing devices** where required.
- Never change electrodes with bare or wet hands.

---

## Falls, Staging, and Openings

Hull work means **staging (scaffolds)**, open hatches, tank openings, and work at height on the dock floor or ship's side.

- Use **guardrails** or **fall protection** where required.
- Inspect staging before use; a competent person signs off on erection and changes.
- **Cover or guard** deck openings and manholes.
- Keep **housekeeping** tight: leads, hoses, and scrap are trip hazards.

---

## Heavy Lifts and Moving Loads

Plates, shapes, and entire blocks move overhead by crane every day. Stay out from **under suspended loads**, follow the signal person, and use **tag lines** (see the Port and Crane Operations course for rigging and signals).

<Pitfall title="Welding Galvanized Without Ventilation">

**Common mistake:** welding galvanized railings or brackets in a small compartment "just for a few minutes."

Zinc fume causes **metal fume fever**: chills, fever, and nausea a few hours later. Remove the galvanizing from the weld area where allowed, use **local exhaust**, and wear a respirator when needed. If you feel sick after welding galvanized steel, report it.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Fumes vary by metal and coating: **hexavalent chromium** from stainless, **manganese** from steel fillers, **zinc** from galvanizing, **lead** from old paint. Control them with **coating removal, local exhaust, ventilation, and respirators**. Wear **FR clothing**, the right **lens shade**, and protect bystanders. Prevent **shock** on wet steel, guard **falls and openings**, and stay clear of **suspended loads**.
