## Ships Are Built Like Building Blocks

Modern ships aren't built frame by frame on a slipway. They're built as **blocks** (also called modules or units) in shops and staging areas, then lifted into place and joined.

### Why Build This Way?

- **Better access:** welding in the shop is often **downhand** (flat), faster and higher quality than overhead work in a finished hull.
- **Parallel work:** many blocks are built at once.
- **Pre-outfitting:** installing pipe, cable trays, and equipment foundations in the open block is far easier than in a closed compartment.
- **Weather protection:** more work happens indoors.

---

## Panel Lines

For flat panels (decks, bulkheads, flat side shell), many yards use a **panel line**:

1. Plates are butt welded into a large flat panel, often by **submerged arc welding (SAW)** from one side.
2. **Stiffeners** (longitudinals) are positioned and fillet welded, often by automatic **gantry welders**.
3. Webs and other members are added.

Curved shell blocks are built on adjustable **pin jigs** that hold plates at the correct curvature.

---

## Accuracy Control

Every block must match the blocks around it within tight tolerances. If not, erection joints need **cutting, re-fitting, and extra welding**, which adds cost and distortion.

Accuracy control includes:

- **Shrinkage allowances**: parts cut slightly oversize because welding shrinks steel
- **Excess stock** (extra material) left on some block edges, trimmed at erection
- **Dimensional checks** with tapes, lasers, and **3D measurement** (total stations, laser trackers) at each stage
- Feedback of measurements to **adjust** future cutting and shrinkage factors

---

## Erection

Blocks are lifted by crane into the **building dock** or onto the **ways**, positioned to reference lines (centerline, baseline, frame marks), and supported on **blocks and shores**.

Fitters then:

- Align the block in **all three directions** (fore-aft, athwartships, height) and check **level and plumb**
- Trim excess stock and prepare **erection joints** (often welded from both sides, or one side with backing)
- Pull plates into alignment with jacks, dogs, and wedges
- Tack, then welders complete the joint following the **welding sequence** designed to control distortion

<Pitfall title="Forcing a Block into Place">

**Common mistake:** a block is out of tolerance, so the crew uses heavy jacks and come-alongs to force it to fit, then welds it.

That locks large **residual stresses** into the structure, which can cause distortion when nearby welding is done, or cracks later. Report out-of-tolerance blocks so engineering can approve a correction method, and feed the problem back to accuracy control.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Ships are built from **parts → panels → blocks → grand blocks → erection**. Blocks allow **downhand welding**, **parallel work**, and **pre-outfitting**. **Accuracy control** with shrinkage allowances, excess stock, and 3D measurement keeps blocks fitting together. At erection, align to **reference lines**, prepare joints, and follow the **weld sequence**.
