## Why Ships Are Different

Welding on a ship means working inside **steel boxes** that once held fuel, cargo, or sewage, with flammable insulation, paint, and wiring on the **other side** of the plate you're heating. Fires and explosions during shipyard hot work have killed many workers. The controls exist because of those losses.

In the U.S., shipyard work falls under **OSHA 29 CFR Part 1915**. **Subpart B** covers **confined and enclosed spaces and other dangerous atmospheres**, and **Subpart P** covers **fire protection** in shipyard employment.

---

## Testing Before Hot Work

Before hot work in or on certain spaces, especially those that held **flammable or combustible liquids or gases** (fuel tanks, cargo tanks, pump rooms) and adjacent spaces, the atmosphere must be **tested and certified** by the right person:

| Who | Role |
|---|---|
| **Marine chemist** | Certified (under NFPA's marine chemist program) to test and issue certificates for hot work in and around tanks that held flammables, among other situations required by OSHA |
| **Shipyard competent person** | Trained employee who tests and inspects spaces as allowed by Subpart B, and maintains conditions after a marine chemist's certificate |
| **Coast Guard authorized person** | In some locations where a marine chemist isn't reasonably available |

Marine chemist certificates use standard designations (from **NFPA 306**), such as:

- **Safe for Workers:** oxygen and toxic levels acceptable for entry
- **Safe for Hot Work:** flammable vapors below the required limit, and residues cleaned, so welding and cutting can proceed
- **Not Safe for Workers / Not Safe for Hot Work**

Conditions can change, so certificates carry **conditions and limits**, and the competent person re-tests as required, for example after breaks, ventilation changes, or new work.

### Fire Watch

A **fire watch** is a trained person whose only job is to watch for and put out fires during hot work. OSHA Subpart P requires fire watches in specific situations, such as when there's slag or sparks that could reach combustibles, or heat conducted through bulkheads or decks. The fire watch:

- Has **extinguishing equipment** ready and knows how to use it
- Can **communicate** with the hot work operator and sound an alarm
- Watches **both sides** of the bulkhead or deck being heated, if needed
- Stays for at least **30 minutes after hot work ends**, unless the area is surveyed and determined to have no further fire hazard

### Heat Goes Through Steel

Welding or cutting on one side of a bulkhead can heat the other side enough to ignite **insulation, paint, wiring, or stored materials**. Check the other side **before** you strike an arc.

## Oxy-Fuel Safety

- Inspect hoses, regulators, and torches; use **flashback arrestors**.
- **Remove torches and hoses** from confined spaces at breaks and at the end of a shift (or disconnect at the supply) so leaking gas can't accumulate.
- **Never use oxygen to ventilate** or "freshen" a space, or to blow off clothing. Oxygen-enriched atmospheres make clothing and materials burn violently.
- Secure cylinders upright, valves closed when not in use, caps on for transport.

---

## Confined and Enclosed Spaces

Double bottoms, tanks, voids, and cofferdams can be:

- **Oxygen deficient** (rust consumes oxygen; inert gas)
- **Toxic** (hydrogen sulfide in sewage or fish holds, solvent vapors, welding fumes, carbon monoxide)
- **Flammable** (fuel residues)

Never enter a space unless it's been **tested and labeled** safe for entry, and follow your yard's entry and rescue procedures. **Most confined space fatalities include would-be rescuers.** Don't go in after someone without proper equipment and a plan.

<Pitfall title="'It Was Safe This Morning'">

**Common mistake:** a tank was certified Safe for Hot Work at 7 a.m., and at 2 p.m. a welder starts cutting after the ventilation was moved for another job.

Conditions change. Vapors can return from residues or adjacent tanks, and ventilation changes affect fumes and oxygen. Check that the **certificate and its conditions are still valid**, and get the space **re-tested** when anything changes.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Shipyard hot work falls under **OSHA 1915**. Spaces that held flammables need **testing and certification** by a **marine chemist** or **competent person** (**Safe for Workers**, **Safe for Hot Work**). Clear **both sides** of the work, post a **fire watch** that stays at least **30 minutes** after, remove hoses from confined spaces at breaks, **never ventilate with oxygen**, and **re-test** whenever conditions change.
