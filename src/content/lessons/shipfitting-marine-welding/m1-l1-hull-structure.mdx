## A Ship Is a Beam That Floats

A ship's hull acts like a long hollow **beam**. Waves lift it in the middle (**hogging**) and then at the ends (**sagging**), bending it thousands of times a day. Cargo and water push on the sides and bottom. The hull's structure must carry these loads while staying **watertight**.

Shipfitters and welders build that structure. Knowing what each piece does tells you why fit-up, weld quality, and alignment matter.

| Member | Direction | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Keel** | Fore and aft, bottom centerline | The ship's backbone |
| **Shell plating** | Outer skin | Keeps water out; carries hull bending loads |
| **Frames** | Transverse (around the hull) | Stiffen the side shell; give the hull its shape |
| **Longitudinals** | Fore and aft | Stiffen plating and carry hull bending loads |
| **Floors** | Transverse, in the double bottom | Connect bottom shell, inner bottom, and keel |
| **Girders** | Fore and aft, deep members | Support longitudinals and decks over long spans |
| **Bulkheads** | Transverse or longitudinal walls | Divide the hull into watertight compartments; add strength |
| **Decks** | Horizontal | Close the top of the hull beam; carry loads |
| **Double bottom** | Between bottom shell and inner bottom | Protects against grounding; holds ballast, fuel, or water |

**Brackets** connect members at their ends, and **web frames** are deep, heavy frames placed at intervals to support lighter members.

## Framing Systems

### Transverse Framing

Many closely spaced **transverse frames** with fewer longitudinals. Common on **smaller vessels** such as tugs, fishing boats, and workboats. Easier to build for curved hulls.

### Longitudinal Framing

Many **longitudinal stiffeners** supported by widely spaced **web frames**. Used on **large ships** (tankers, bulk carriers, container ships) because longitudinals directly resist the **hull bending** that matters most on long vessels.

### Combined (Mixed) Framing

Longitudinal framing in the bottom and deck, where bending stresses are highest, and transverse framing on the sides. Common on many cargo ships.

---

## Watertight Integrity

**Watertight bulkheads** split the hull so one flooded compartment doesn't sink the ship. Every penetration through them (pipes, cables, doors) must keep watertight integrity. That means:

- Approved **penetration fittings** (collars, multi-cable transits)
- **Continuous, sound welds** with no gaps
- No unauthorized holes

<Pitfall title="Cutting a Hole for a Pipe">

**Common mistake:** a fitter burns a hole through a bulkhead to run a new pipe or cable without checking whether it's a watertight or structural boundary.

Unauthorized holes can compromise watertight subdivision, fire boundaries, or structural strength, and may violate classification rules. **Any** cut in structure needs an approved drawing or engineering approval, plus the proper penetration fitting and inspection.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

A hull is a floating **beam** that hogs and sags. The **keel, shell, frames, longitudinals, floors, girders, bulkheads, and decks** work together to carry loads and keep water out. **Transverse** framing suits small vessels; **longitudinal** framing suits long ships. **Watertight bulkheads** must never be cut without approval.
