## A Maritime Region

Puget Sound and the Columbia River support one of the busiest maritime economies in the country. Work for shipfitters and welders comes from several directions:

| Sector | Examples in the region |
|---|---|
| **Naval** | **Puget Sound Naval Shipyard & Intermediate Maintenance Facility** in Bremerton maintains and overhauls Navy ships and submarines, and runs a long-standing apprentice program |
| **Commercial repair and new construction** | Yards in Seattle, Tacoma, Everett, Anacortes, Portland, and on Whidbey Island build and repair tugs, barges, ferries, fishing vessels, and research ships |
| **Ferries** | **Washington State Ferries**, one of the largest ferry systems in the U.S., keeps vessels running with its own maintenance staff and contracted yard work |
| **Fishing fleet** | Seattle is home port for much of the **North Pacific fishing fleet** that works Alaskan waters; boats need steady repair between seasons |
| **Ports and terminals** | Structural repair of docks, cranes, and terminal equipment |
| **Offshore and alternative energy** | Growing work on vessels and equipment for new energy projects |

Employers change and grow, so research **current** openings, apprenticeships, and contractors in your area.

## Trades Inside a Shipyard

| Trade | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Shipfitter** | Lays out, fits, and tacks structure |
| **Welder** | Makes production welds to WPS requirements |
| **Burner / cutter** | Cuts plate and shapes; removes damaged structure |
| **Pipefitter** | Builds and installs piping systems |
| **Marine electrician** | Installs and maintains power, lighting, and controls |
| **Machinist / outside machinist** | Shafting, propellers, rudders, alignment |
| **Rigger** | Moves heavy loads and blocks with cranes |
| **Painter / blaster** | Surface prep and coatings |
| **Insulator, sheet metal worker, carpenter** | Outfitting trades |
| **QA inspector / NDT tech** | Inspects and tests the work |

Many careers move from welder or fitter into **lead, foreman, inspector, planner, or estimator** roles.

## Credentials That Help

| Credential | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **AWS Certified Welder** (or employer weld tests) | Demonstrates qualification in specific processes and positions |
| **TWIC** (Transportation Worker Identification Credential) | Required for unescorted access to secure areas of many maritime facilities and vessels |
| **OSHA 10 or 30, Maritime** | Shows basic shipyard safety training |
| **Shipyard competent person** training | For those who test and monitor spaces |
| **Forklift, rigging, or signal person** qualifications | Useful in fabrication and yard roles |
| **CWI (Certified Welding Inspector)** | For experienced welders moving into inspection |

Being able to pass a **3G and 4G** test, read **drawings and welding symbols**, and show a strong **safety record** sets candidates apart.

<Pitfall title="Only Practicing Flat Welds">

**Common mistake:** a student spends most practice time welding in the flat position because it feels good and looks good.

Shipyard work, especially at erection joints and repairs, is heavy on **vertical and overhead** welding. Employers test in those positions. Practice **3G and 4G** (and 3F/4F) early and often.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

The Pacific Northwest has **naval, commercial, ferry, fishing, and port** work for maritime trades. Shipyards employ **fitters, welders, burners, pipefitters, electricians, machinists, riggers, and inspectors**. Paths run through **college programs, pre-apprenticeship, and registered apprenticeships**. **AWS certification, TWIC, OSHA maritime training, and 3G/4G skills** help you get hired.
