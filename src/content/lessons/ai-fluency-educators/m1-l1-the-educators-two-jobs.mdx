## Two Jobs, One Framework

Most professionals only need to use AI well themselves. Educators have a second job: their learners are using AI too, often without much guidance, and they look to their teachers for how to do it well. That gives you two roles:

1. **Fluent practitioner.** You use AI to plan, create materials, give feedback, and handle administrative work, while protecting your students and your own professional judgment.
2. **Fluency teacher.** You help learners decide when AI helps their learning and when it gets in the way, describe what they need, judge what they get back, and be honest about how they used it.

The two roles reinforce each other. Students learn far more from watching how you actually use AI, including the times you decide not to, than from any policy statement.

The **4D AI Fluency Framework** gives both jobs a shared vocabulary:

| Competency | As a fluent practitioner | As a fluency teacher |
|---|---|---|
| **Delegation** | Hand off prep and admin; keep relationships and judgment of student work. | Help students tell apart AI use that supports learning from use that replaces it. |
| **Description** | Describe lessons, levels, and needs precisely enough to get usable materials. | Teach students to describe what they need, including asking AI to coach rather than answer. |
| **Discernment** | Check materials for accuracy, bias, reading level, and fit with your curriculum. | Build habits of verifying, questioning, and comparing AI output. |
| **Diligence** | Protect student data, follow policy, disclose AI use where it matters. | Set clear expectations and teach honest disclosure. |

## Why "Ban It" and "Allow Everything" Both Fail

Early responses to generative AI often swung between two extremes:

- **Ban it.** Students still use AI, just without guidance and without telling you. The students who follow the rule fall behind in a skill their future workplaces expect.
- **Allow everything.** Students outsource the very thinking assignments were designed to build, and many don't notice it happening.

Fluency is the middle path: **deliberate, assignment-by-assignment decisions** about AI's role, made visible to students and explained in terms of what they're meant to learn.

<Pitfall title="Teaching Tools Instead of Fluency">

**Common mistake:** running a session on "how to use [specific AI tool]" and calling it AI literacy.

Tools change every few months. The questions behind them don't: Should I use AI for this? How do I ask for what I need? Is this right? Am I being honest about how it was made? Teach those questions, using whatever tool is handy as the example.

</Pitfall>
