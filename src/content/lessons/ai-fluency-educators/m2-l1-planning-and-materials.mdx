## Describe the Learners, Not Just the Topic

"Make a worksheet on the water cycle" gets you a generic worksheet. AI doesn't know your students, your standards, what you taught yesterday, or what tripped students up last year. A strong teaching description covers all three types from Foundations:

| Type | What to include for teaching materials |
|---|---|
| **Product** | Grade or level, learning objective, standard or outcome, format, length, reading level, accessibility needs. |
| **Process** | "Build from what they already know about evaporation." "Order questions from recall to application." "Flag any content that might be inaccurate or outdated." |
| **Performance** | "Ask me clarifying questions before drafting." "Act as an experienced middle-school science teacher." "Push back if my objective is too broad for one lesson." |

**Example:**

```text
I teach 7th-grade science. Students already know evaporation and
condensation but confuse precipitation with condensation. Learning
objective: students can explain how energy from the sun drives the
water cycle.

Create a 15-minute practice activity: 6 questions, moving from
recall to application, one of which uses a local example (we're in
a dry inland city). Reading level around grade 6. Include an answer
key with one-sentence explanations.

Before you draft, ask me up to three questions about my students.
Flag anything you're not certain is scientifically accurate.
```

## Discern Before It Reaches Students

Everything AI produces for your classroom passes through your judgment first. Check:

1. **Accuracy.** AI makes factual errors, including in answer keys. Work through every answer yourself. Watch especially for math errors, misattributed quotes, invented historical details, and outdated science.
2. **Alignment.** Does it actually target your objective, or a nearby, easier one?
3. **Level.** Is the reading level and difficulty right? AI often overshoots or makes everything too easy.
4. **Representation and bias.** Whose names, examples, and perspectives appear? Who is missing? Are any stereotypes slipping in?
5. **Fit.** Does it match how *you* teach the concept, including your vocabulary and methods?

<Pitfall title="Trusting the Answer Key">

**Common mistake:** generating a quiz and its answer key together and only skimming the key.

AI-generated answer keys can contain confident errors, and an error in a key gets graded into dozens of students' scores. Work every problem yourself, or at minimum every one you're unsure about, before the material goes out.

</Pitfall>

## Differentiation Without Distortion

AI is especially useful for adapting one resource into several versions: simplified readings, translated summaries for multilingual families, extension problems for students ready for more. The risk is that **meaning drifts** as text is simplified. Compare each version to the original for:

- Key ideas that disappeared.
- Nuance that became a flat (or wrong) statement.
- Changes in tone that make one group's version feel "lesser."

<TryIt title="Build and Check One Real Resource">

1. Choose an upcoming lesson. Write a full description: learners, objective, product, process, and performance, like the example above.
2. Ask AI for one practice activity with an answer key.
3. Run the five checks: accuracy, alignment, level, representation, fit. Log every problem you find.
4. Give one round of specific feedback and compare the revision.
5. Ask for a version adapted for a different reading level. Compare it to the original and note anything that drifted.
6. Decide: is this ready for students? What did you have to fix?

</TryIt>
