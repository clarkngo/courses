## AI's Mistakes Are a Teaching Resource

AI's errors are, for an educator, an opportunity. Catching a confident mistake requires exactly the subject knowledge and critical thinking you're trying to build. Discernment activities let students practice both.

---

## Activity 1: Error Hunt

**Time:** 20 minutes.

1. Before class, ask AI to write a short explanation of a topic your students have studied, such as a historical event, a scientific process, or a math method. Check it yourself and note any errors. If it has none, edit in two or three subtle ones (a wrong date, a reversed cause and effect, a calculation slip).
2. Students work in pairs to find every error, using their notes and textbook as the source of truth.
3. Debrief: Which errors were hardest to spot? Why did the text still *sound* right?

Variation for older students: don't tell them how many errors there are.

## Activity 2: Check the Source

1. Ask AI a question with specific claims and ask it to include sources.
2. Students try to locate each source and confirm it says what the AI claims.
3. Tally: How many sources exist? How many actually support the claim?

Students who have done this once rarely trust an AI citation without checking again.

## Activity 3: Push Back

Students ask AI a question they know the answer to, then disagree with its correct answer: *"I don't think that's right."* Discuss what happens. Does it defend its answer with reasoning, or immediately agree? What does that mean for using AI to check your own work?

## Teaching Diligence: Honest Disclosure

Diligence for students centers on **honesty about how work was made**. Students are far more likely to disclose AI use when it's normal, expected, and not automatically punished.

Give students a simple **AI use statement** to attach to assignments where AI is permitted:

```text
AI use statement
- Tool(s) I used:
- What I used it for: (e.g. brainstorming topics, explaining a
  concept, checking grammar)
- What I did myself:
- How I checked the AI's output:
```

Grade the statement lightly, or not at all. The goal is honesty and reflection, not a confession.

Diligence also includes **privacy**: teach students not to paste personal information about themselves or others into AI tools, and to know which tools their school provides.

<Pitfall title="Making Disclosure Risky">

**Common mistake:** asking students to disclose AI use, then penalizing anything they disclose.

Students quickly learn that honesty costs points and stop disclosing. Be clear about what's permitted on each assignment (see Module 4), and treat disclosed, permitted use as completely normal.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Prepare an Error Hunt for Your Class">

1. Pick a topic your students know well enough to fact-check.
2. Ask AI for a short explanation (150 to 250 words) at their level.
3. Check it carefully yourself. Record any real errors. If needed, add two or three subtle errors of your own, and keep an answer key.
4. Write three debrief questions, including one about why the errors were hard to spot.
5. Draft an AI use statement template suited to your students' age.

</TryIt>
