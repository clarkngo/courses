## Delegation for Learners Is Different

For a professional, good delegation often means getting work done faster. For a learner, the work is usually the point. A student who has AI write their lab report has a lab report, but hasn't learned to write one.

So the delegation question for students isn't just "Can AI do this?" It's:

> **Will using AI this way help me learn what this assignment is for, or skip it?**

Students can usually answer that well once they know what an assignment is for. **Telling them the purpose of each assignment** is the single most useful thing you can do for their delegation skills.

---

## Activity 1: Help or Shortcut?

**Time:** 15 minutes. **Works for:** upper elementary through adult learners.

1. Give students a short list of AI uses on one real assignment, for example an argumentative essay:
   - Asking AI to explain what a counterargument is
   - Asking AI to write the counterargument paragraph
   - Asking AI to quiz you on your evidence
   - Asking AI to fix every grammar error
   - Asking AI to point out the weakest part of your argument without fixing it
2. In pairs, students sort each into **Helps my learning**, **Skips my learning**, or **Depends**.
3. Discuss the "Depends" cards. The best conversations happen there: grammar fixes might be fine in a history essay and a shortcut in a writing class focused on mechanics.

## Activity 2: Coach, Don't Answer

The most important description skill for learners is asking AI to **support their thinking rather than replace it**. Teach students a performance description they can reuse:

```text
I'm a student learning [topic]. Don't give me the answer. Ask me
questions and give hints one step at a time so I can work it out
myself. If I get something wrong, help me see why instead of
correcting it for me.
```

Have students try the same problem twice, once asking for the answer and once with the coaching prompt, then reflect: Which one could you do again tomorrow without AI?

## Activity 3: Prompt Makeover

1. Show a vague student prompt: *"explain the french revolution."*
2. Students improve it in rounds, adding what they already know, what confuses them, the level they need, and how they want AI to behave.
3. Compare the responses. Students see that clear description is a thinking skill: to describe what you need, you have to know what you don't understand.

<Pitfall title="Teaching Prompts Without Purpose">

**Common mistake:** teaching students to write excellent prompts that get AI to produce excellent finished work.

That trains students to delegate more effectively, but not more wisely. Pair every description lesson with a delegation question: *Should AI be producing this at all, or helping you produce it?*

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Adapt One Activity for Your Learners">

1. Choose one of the three activities above.
2. Rewrite it for a specific class you teach: their age, subject, and a real upcoming assignment.
3. Write the **purpose statement** for that assignment in one student-friendly sentence ("This essay is to practice building an argument from evidence.").
4. Optional: ask an AI assistant to critique your adapted activity from the point of view of a skeptical student, and revise based on anything useful it raises.
5. Plan when you'll run it.

</TryIt>
