## The Highest-Stakes Shortcut

Feedback is time-consuming, and AI can draft comments in seconds. That makes it tempting, and it's where educators most need diligence. Students and families reasonably assume feedback reflects *your* reading of *their* work. Grades affect students' futures. And student work is student data.

A useful line: **AI can help you prepare to give feedback, and help you write it up. It should not be the one deciding what the work deserves.**

---

## Where AI Helps Safely

| Use | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Drafting or sharpening a rubric** | You review every criterion; no student data involved. |
| **Creating exemplars** at different quality levels for norming or teaching | Invented work, so no privacy concern, and great for showing students what "proficient" looks like. |
| **Building a comment bank** for common issues | Reusable phrasing you choose from and personalize. |
| **Spotting patterns across a class** from your own notes ("12 students struggled with thesis statements") | Helps you plan reteaching. Use your summary, not student work. |
| **Polishing your own feedback** for tone and clarity | Your judgment, your content; AI only edits wording. |

## Where It Needs Guardrails or Should Stay Out

- **Grading decisions.** Assigning scores is your professional responsibility. Even "AI suggests, I approve" tends to anchor you to its number, a form of automation bias.
- **Pasting student work into unapproved tools.** Use only tools your institution approves for student data, and de-identify where you can.
- **Generic comments presented as personal.** Students notice when feedback could have been written about anyone, and it erodes trust.
- **Sensitive content.** Writing about personal experiences, trauma, identity, or family should be read and responded to by you.

The order matters. **Read and judge first**, before any AI suggestion, so your assessment isn't anchored by it.

<Pitfall title="Feedback Nobody Read">

**Common mistake:** running a stack of essays through AI and sending back its comments with light edits.

Even if the comments are reasonable, they skip the most valuable part: you learning what your students understand. And if a student later asks "What did you mean by this?", you won't know. If you use AI in feedback, it should come *after* your reading, never instead of it.

</Pitfall>

## Be Transparent With Students

If you use AI in your feedback process, tell students how, in plain terms. For example: *"I read every submission myself and decide every grade. I sometimes use an AI tool to help phrase comments clearly. Your work is never entered into a tool that isn't approved by the school."* Modeling this kind of disclosure is also the best way to teach it.

<TryIt title="Build a Feedback Toolkit Without Student Data">

1. Pick a recurring assignment. Paste in your current rubric, or describe it, and ask AI to critique it:
   ```text
   Here's my rubric for [assignment] for [grade/level] students.
   Point out any criteria that are vague, overlapping, or hard to
   score consistently. Don't rewrite it yet.
   ```
2. Revise the rubric yourself, using only the critiques you agree with.
3. Ask AI to write **three invented exemplars** at different levels (beginning, proficient, advanced). Check they actually match your rubric levels.
4. Ask for a **comment bank** of 10 comments for the most common issues, each with a blank for a specific detail from the student's work.
5. Write a two-sentence disclosure statement for students describing how you use (or don't use) AI in feedback.

</TryIt>
