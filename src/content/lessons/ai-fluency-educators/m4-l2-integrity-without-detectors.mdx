## The Problem With AI Detectors

AI detection tools promise to tell you whether text was written by AI. Applying **discernment** to the detectors themselves shows serious problems:

- **False positives.** Detectors flag human-written work as AI-generated. Research has found they disproportionately flag writing by non-native English speakers, whose simpler phrasing can resemble AI output (Liang et al., 2023, *Patterns*).
- **Easy to evade.** Light editing, paraphrasing, or asking AI to write "less like an AI" often defeats them, so the students most determined to cheat are the least likely to be caught.
- **No evidence trail.** A score from a black box can't be explained or verified, and it isn't proof of anything.
- **Unstable.** As AI models change, detector accuracy changes too. OpenAI withdrew its own AI text classifier in 2023, citing its low rate of accuracy.

A false accusation of cheating can seriously harm a student. That's a **diligence** issue: you are accountable for decisions you make based on a tool's output, just as with any other AI system.

> **Bottom line:** a detector score should never be the basis for an accusation. At most, it's a reason to look more closely using the approaches below.

## What Works Better

### 1. Make the Process Visible

Assignments that show how work developed make both learning and integrity easier to see:

- Drafts, outlines, and notes submitted along the way
- Version history in shared documents
- Short reflections: "What was the hardest decision in this draft?"
- AI use statements, where AI is permitted

### 2. Talk With the Student

If a submission doesn't match what you know of a student's work, start with a **conversation, not an accusation**:

- "Walk me through how you developed this argument."
- "What did you mean by this paragraph?"
- "What sources did you use, and how did you find them?"

Students who did the work can usually discuss it. A conversation also gives a student who misused AI a chance to be honest, and you a chance to teach.

### 3. Design for Learning That's Hard to Outsource

Assignments that connect to class discussion, personal experience, local context, or in-class work are harder to hand off to AI, and usually more meaningful. For a full approach to redesigning objectives and assessment, see [Teaching Above the Line](/courses/teaching-above-the-line/).

### 4. Follow a Fair Process

Know your institution's academic integrity process and follow it. Document what you observed, not what a tool reported. Treat a first, unclear case as a teaching moment when policy allows.

<Pitfall title="Detector Score as Evidence">

**Common mistake:** emailing a student "Our AI detector shows 87% AI-generated, so you'll receive a zero."

The score can't be verified, may be wrong, and may reflect bias against certain writing styles. Start from what you can actually observe, such as the absence of drafts or a mismatch with in-class writing, and talk with the student.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Integrity-Proof One Assignment">

1. Choose an assignment where you've worried about AI misuse.
2. Add **two process checkpoints** (for example, an outline and a draft, or a proposal and a reflection).
3. Add **one element tied to your class**: a discussion, a local example, or the student's own experience.
4. Write three conversation questions you'd use if a submission seemed inconsistent with a student's work.
5. Ask an AI assistant to try completing your redesigned assignment, then look at what it produced. Which parts could it do convincingly, and which couldn't it? Adjust if needed.

</TryIt>
