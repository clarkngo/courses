## You're the Human in the Loop

Everything in this course points to one principle: **AI assists; you're accountable.** When an AI-drafted email goes out under your name, it's your email. When an AI summary shapes a decision, it's your decision. Responsible AI use isn't a separate skill from good AI use — it's the part that makes the rest trustworthy.

Here are five habits that cover most real-world situations.

---

## Five Everyday Habits

### 1. Protect Data

- Follow your organization's AI policy. If there isn't one, ask.
- Use approved tools for work data; don't put confidential information into personal accounts.
- Strip names and identifying details when you can: "Customer A," "Employee B."
- Never paste passwords, API keys, financial account numbers, health records, or government IDs.

### 2. Verify Before You Use

Match your checking to the stakes:

| Stakes | Example | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Brainstorm list, casual rewording | Quick read for sense |
| **Medium** | Internal email, meeting summary | Read carefully; check names, dates, action items against the source |
| **High** | Client deliverable, public statement, anything legal, medical, financial, or about a person's job | Check every fact against a trusted source; have a qualified human review it |

### 3. Watch for Bias and Fairness

Be extra careful whenever AI output involves **people**: hiring, performance reviews, customer communication, descriptions of groups. Ask: Would this read differently if the person were a different gender, age, or background? Is anyone being stereotyped or left out? You can also ask the AI directly: "Review this for biased language or assumptions."

AI should never be the sole decision-maker for decisions that significantly affect people — who gets hired, promoted, approved, or disciplined.

### 4. Be Transparent

Know your organization's expectations about disclosing AI use. As a general norm, be open about substantial AI help when it matters to the reader: in academic work, in published content, and whenever someone would reasonably assume a human wrote or checked something. Never present AI-generated content — or images — as something it isn't.

### 5. Respect Others' Work

Don't use AI to pass off someone else's writing, art, or ideas as your own, and be cautious about asking AI to closely imitate a specific living creator. If AI output closely resembles a known source, don't use it as original work.

## What to Do When You Get a Bad Response

Every AI user gets bad responses: wrong, off-topic, biased, or simply unhelpful. Here's a quick playbook.

1. **Diagnose.** Was it missing context? A vague ask? A fact the AI shouldn't be expected to know? (Revisit the quick-reference table in Module 4.)
2. **Correct it directly.** "That's incorrect — our policy is 10 days, not 15. Please revise." Specific corrections work better than "that's wrong."
3. **Supply the source.** If it's getting facts wrong, give it the right material and say "only use this."
4. **Start fresh if it's stuck.** If the conversation keeps circling back to the same error, write a handoff summary with the correction included and open a new chat.
5. **Know when to stop.** Some tasks just aren't a good fit. If you've spent longer fixing the AI's output than doing it yourself would have taken, do it yourself.
6. **Report harmful outputs.** Most assistants have a thumbs-down or feedback button. Use it for harmful, biased, or badly wrong responses.

<Pitfall title="Assuming the AI Checked Itself">

**Common mistake:** asking "Is this accurate?" and treating "Yes, this is accurate" as verification.

The AI can confidently confirm its own errors. Self-review prompts are great for catching style problems and surfacing claims *to* check, but actual verification means checking against a source outside the AI — a document, a database, an expert, an official website.

</Pitfall>
