## The Vending Machine Mindset

Most people start using AI the way they use a vending machine: put in a request, take whatever comes out, walk away. "Write a thank-you email." Done.

That works for tiny tasks. But it leaves most of the value on the table, because it treats the first response as the final answer. A better model is a **collaborator** — a fast, tireless colleague who has read a lot but knows nothing about your situation until you tell them. With a colleague like that, you would:

- **Explain the situation** before asking for help.
- **React to their first draft** instead of accepting or rejecting it wholesale.
- **Ask them to push back**, not just agree with you.
- **Stay responsible** for what finally goes out the door.

| Task-completer mindset | Collaborator mindset |
|---|---|
| One prompt, one answer | A back-and-forth conversation |
| "Write X" | "Here's my situation and goal — help me write X" |
| Accept or reject the output | Give feedback: "Shorter. Warmer. Drop the second point." |
| AI does the thinking | AI helps *you* think better and faster |
| Output is the finished product | Output is a draft you shape and own |

The collaborator mindset isn't about being polite to software. It's about recognizing that **the quality of the output depends on the quality of the conversation.**

## Three Ways to Collaborate with AI

Nearly every useful thing you'll do with an AI assistant at work fits one of three roles.

### 1. AI as a Thought Partner

You bring a problem or a half-formed idea, and AI helps you explore it: brainstorming options, asking clarifying questions, poking holes, arguing the other side.

```text
I'm planning a team offsite for 12 people with a $3,000 budget.
Before suggesting anything, ask me 5 questions that would help
you give better recommendations.
```

Asking the AI to ask *you* questions is one of the most underused moves there is. It surfaces the context you forgot to mention.

### 2. AI as a First Drafter

You know what needs to exist — an email, a policy summary, a slide outline — and AI produces a first version you then edit. The blank page is gone, and your time goes into judgment and polish instead of typing.

```text
Draft a short email to a client letting them know their project
will be delivered one week late because a supplier missed a
deadline. Tone: apologetic but confident. Offer a 15-minute call.
```

### 3. AI as a Coach or Tutor

You want to *learn* or *improve*, not just get an output. AI explains unfamiliar concepts, gives feedback on your work, and quizzes you.

```text
Here's the opening paragraph of my grant proposal. Don't rewrite it.
Instead, tell me the two biggest weaknesses a reviewer would notice,
and explain why.
```

Notice the "Don't rewrite it" instruction. In coaching mode, you want feedback that makes *you* better — not a replacement that skips your learning.

## Advice for Getting Started

1. **Start with low-stakes tasks** you could easily check yourself — reformatting notes, drafting a routine email.
2. **Always read the output** before you use it. Every time.
3. **Follow up at least once.** The second response is almost always better than the first.
4. **Keep a list** of prompts that worked well so you can reuse them.
5. **Be curious, not credulous.** Enjoy what it can do, and stay skeptical of what it claims.

<TryIt title="One Task, Three Roles">

Open any free AI assistant (you'll set one up properly in Module 2 — any will do for now). Pick one real task from your week, such as "prepare for a performance review conversation." Then send three separate prompts:

1. **Thought partner:** "I have a performance review next week. Ask me 5 questions that would help me prepare."
2. **First drafter:** "Draft a one-page list of my accomplishments this quarter based on these notes: [paste a few rough notes]."
3. **Coach:** "Here's how I plan to describe my biggest challenge this quarter: [one or two sentences]. Give me feedback on how it might land with my manager. Don't rewrite it."

Compare the three responses. Which role saved you the most time? Which one improved your *thinking* the most?

</TryIt>

<Pitfall title="Letting AI Agree With Everything">

**Common mistake:** asking AI to review your plan and taking "This is a great plan!" as validation.

AI assistants tend to be agreeable. If you want real critique, ask for it directly: "What are the three strongest objections to this plan?" or "Argue against this as a skeptical CFO would." A collaborator who only flatters you isn't much help.

</Pitfall>
