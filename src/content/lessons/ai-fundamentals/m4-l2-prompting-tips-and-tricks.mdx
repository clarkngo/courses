## Eight Techniques Worth Knowing

CRAFT is the skeleton. These techniques are the muscles — use whichever ones fit the task.

### 1. Show an Example

If you want a particular style, showing beats describing. Paste a sample of what "good" looks like:

```text
Write 5 product descriptions in the same style as this example:

"Merino Trail Sock — Warm when it's cold, cool when it's not.
Cushioned where your boots rub. Built for 500 miles."
```

One or two examples often do more than a paragraph of style instructions.

### 2. Let the AI Ask *You* Questions

When you're not sure what context matters, flip the conversation:

```text
I need to write a proposal to my manager for a four-day work week
pilot. Before you draft anything, ask me the questions you need
answered to make it persuasive. Ask them one at a time.
```

### 3. Say What to Avoid

Constraints narrow the output toward what you want: "No jargon." "Don't mention pricing." "Avoid clichés like 'game-changer' and 'synergy.'" "Don't make up statistics — use a placeholder like [STAT] where a number would go."

That last one is a powerful habit: asking for **placeholders instead of invented facts** keeps hallucinations out of your drafts.

### 4. Provide the Source Material

Remember from Module 3: AI is most reliable when it works with material you supply. Instead of "What are the key points of our Q3 strategy?", paste the strategy document (if it's appropriate to share) and ask for the key points *from that text*. Add: "Only use information from the document above."

### 5. Break Big Tasks Into Steps

"Write my entire annual report" will get a shallow result. "First, propose an outline. Wait for my feedback before writing anything." gives you control at each stage. (Module 5 builds this into a full technique: prompt chaining.)

### 6. Ask for Options

"Give me 3 versions: one formal, one friendly, one very brief." Choosing between options is faster than describing exactly what you want in advance.

### 7. Ask the AI to Critique Its Own Work

After a draft: "Review your response as a demanding editor. What are the three weakest parts? Then revise to fix them." This frequently produces a noticeably better second version.

### 8. Save Templates

When a prompt works well, save it with placeholders:

```text
Summarize the following [document type] for [audience] in
[number] bullet points. Focus on [what matters most]. Flag
anything that needs a decision. Text: [paste here]
```

Over time, your prompt library becomes one of your most valuable work tools.

## Quick Reference: Fixing Common Problems

| The output is... | Try adding... |
|---|---|
| Too generic | More context about your situation and audience |
| Too long | A specific word or bullet limit |
| The wrong tone | A tone description *and* an example of the tone you want |
| Full of jargon | "Explain for someone with no background in [field]" |
| Making things up | Source material + "only use the information provided" + placeholders |
| Missing the point | A clearer Ask with an action verb and a stated goal |
| Not quite right, but close | Specific feedback in the same chat (Tweak) |

<TryIt title="Apply the Full Framework to a Real Task">

Pick a genuine task from your work or life — a newsletter, a process document, a presentation outline, a difficult message to a colleague.

1. **Write a CRAFT prompt** with Context, Role, Ask, and Format.
2. **Add at least two techniques** from this lesson — for example, an example of the style you want, and a "don't make up facts; use placeholders" constraint.
3. **Tweak at least twice** with specific feedback.
4. **Ask for self-critique:** "What are the three weakest parts of this? Revise to fix them."
5. **Verify:** read the final version carefully. Is every fact correct? Would you be comfortable putting your name on it?
6. **Save the prompt** as a template in your prompt library.

</TryIt>

<Pitfall title="Over-Engineering Simple Requests">

**Common mistake:** writing a 300-word, fully labeled CRAFT prompt to fix a typo or convert a list to a table.

Match the effort to the task. "Turn this into a table with columns for name, date, and status" is a perfectly good prompt. Frameworks are for tasks where a generic answer would waste your time — use your judgment about when you need them.

</Pitfall>
