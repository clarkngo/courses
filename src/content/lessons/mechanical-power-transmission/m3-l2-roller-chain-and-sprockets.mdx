## Chain: Positive, Strong, Forgiving of Heat

Roller chain transmits high torque at low speeds with **no slip**, tolerates heat and dirt better than belts, and works over long center distances. It does need **lubrication**, and it wears.

Inside each roller is a **bushing**, and the **pin** turns inside that bushing every time the chain wraps a sprocket. **Pin and bushing wear is what makes chain "stretch."** The plates don't actually stretch; the joints wear loose, so each pitch gets slightly longer.

---

## Reading ANSI Chain Numbers

In the ANSI standard, the **leading digits give the pitch in eighths of an inch**:

| Chain | Pitch |
|---|---|
| **#35** | 3/8 in (rollerless bushing chain, indicated by the 5) |
| **#40** | 4/8 = **1/2 in** |
| **#50** | 5/8 in |
| **#60** | 6/8 = 3/4 in |
| **#80** | 8/8 = 1 in |
| **#100** | 10/8 = 1-1/4 in |

The **last digit** is usually **0** for standard roller chain, **1** for lightweight, and **5** for rollerless. A **dash number** means multiple strands: **60-2** is double-strand #60 chain. Suffixes such as **H** (heavy plates) vary by maker.

ISO/British Standard chain (like **08B**) has similar pitches but **different dimensions**, so it doesn't interchange with ANSI.

---

## Connecting Links

| Link | Use |
|---|---|
| **Connecting (master) link** with spring clip or cotter | Joins chain ends. Clip's **closed end faces the direction of travel**. |
| **Offset (half) link** | Lets you use an odd number of pitches; it's weaker, so avoid on heavily loaded drives |

---

## Measuring Wear (Elongation)

Measure chain under light tension across a known number of pitches, then compare to the new length.

**Elongation % = (measured length − nominal length) ÷ nominal length × 100**

Most makers recommend replacement at about **3% elongation** for typical drives, and less (around **1.5%**) for timed drives, high-speed drives, or sprockets with many teeth. Chain wear gauges make this quick.

---

## Slack and Tension

Chain should **not be tight**. It needs some slack on the non-load side:

- A common guideline for horizontal drives is a total mid-span sag of roughly **2–3% of the span length**.
- Too tight: rapid joint wear, bearing overload, noise.
- Too loose: chain whip, climbing teeth, jumping off the sprocket.

Use idlers or adjustable bases to take up slack as the chain wears.

---

## Lubrication

Most chain wear happens inside the joints. Lubricant has to **get between the pin and bushing**, not just coat the outside.

- Apply oil to the **upper edges of the link plates on the slack side**, where it can wick into the joints.
- Methods range from **manual** (brush or drip) to **drip lubricators**, **oil bath**, and **forced spray** for high speeds.
- A dry, rusty, or reddish-brown chain is crying for oil.
- Avoid heavy grease on open chains; it doesn't penetrate, and it collects grit.

---

## Sprockets

Check sprockets every time you replace chain:

- **Hooked or shark-fin teeth** mean the sprocket is worn.
- Wear on **one side of the teeth** means misalignment.
- Small sprockets with few teeth wear fast and run rough. Many designers prefer a minimum of around **17 teeth** on the driver for smooth operation.

<Pitfall title="New Chain on Old Sprockets">

**Common mistake:** replacing a worn chain but keeping the hooked sprockets.

The new chain's correct pitch doesn't match the worn tooth profile. It rides up, wears quickly, and can jump teeth. Unless the sprockets are nearly new, **replace chain and sprockets together**. Also check alignment with a straightedge across both sprocket faces.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

ANSI chain numbers give pitch in **eighths of an inch** (#40 = 1/2 inch). "Stretch" is **joint wear**. Replace at about **3% elongation** (less for precision drives). Keep a little **slack**, oil the **joints on the slack side**, and **replace worn sprockets with the chain**.

<TryIt title="Measure Elongation">

You measure **20 pitches** of #60 chain (3/4 inch pitch).

- Nominal length: 20 × 0.75 = **15.00 inches**
- Measured length: **15.42 inches**
- Elongation: (15.42 − 15.00) ÷ 15.00 × 100 = **2.8%**

It's close to the typical 3% limit. Plan the replacement and order sprockets too.

</TryIt>
