## What a Drivetrain Does

Electric motors usually spin fast (often around **1,750 or 3,500 rpm** on 60 Hz power) with modest torque. Most machines need the opposite: **slow speed with high torque** to move a conveyor, turn a mixer, or drive a roll.

The **drivetrain** connects the two, changing speed and torque along the way.

## The Three Quantities

| Quantity | What it is | Common units |
|---|---|---|
| **Torque** | Twisting force | lb-ft, lb-in, N·m |
| **Speed** | How fast a shaft turns | rpm |
| **Power** | Rate of doing work: torque × speed | horsepower (hp), kilowatts (kW) |

### The Horsepower Formula

**hp = torque (lb-ft) × rpm ÷ 5,252**

Or, rearranged to find torque:

**torque (lb-ft) = hp × 5,252 ÷ rpm**

In metric: **kW = torque (N·m) × rpm ÷ 9,550**. Also, 1 hp ≈ 0.746 kW.

### Worked Example

A **10 hp** motor runs at **1,750 rpm**. Its full-load torque is:

10 × 5,252 ÷ 1,750 ≈ **30 lb-ft**

## Ratios: Trading Speed for Torque

When a drive reduces speed, it **multiplies torque** by about the same ratio (minus losses). Power stays roughly the same going in and out.

**Ratio = driver speed ÷ driven speed**

For belts and chains, the ratio comes from sizes:

- **Belt:** ratio ≈ driven sheave diameter ÷ driver sheave diameter
- **Chain or gears:** ratio = driven teeth ÷ driver teeth

### Continuing the Example

That 30 lb-ft motor drives a **10:1** gearbox:

| | Speed | Torque |
|---|---|---|
| Gearbox input | 1,750 rpm | 30 lb-ft |
| Gearbox output (ideal) | 175 rpm | 300 lb-ft |
| Output at 95% efficiency | 175 rpm | ≈ 285 lb-ft |

Every component **downstream** of a reduction must handle the **higher torque**. That's why output shafts, keys, and couplings are much larger than the motor's.

## Efficiency and Losses

No drive is perfect. Losses become heat.

| Drive | Typical efficiency (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Synchronous (toothed) belt | ~97–98% |
| Roller chain, well lubricated | ~97–98% |
| V-belt, properly tensioned | ~93–96%, dropping if it slips |
| Helical or bevel gear stage | ~97–98% per stage |
| Worm gear | Wide range, roughly 50–90%, lower at high ratios |

A hot gearbox or a squealing belt is power you paid for being turned into heat.

<Pitfall title="Upsizing the Motor to Fix a Stall">

**Common mistake:** a conveyor keeps tripping on overload, so someone installs a bigger motor.

Now the motor can deliver more torque than the gearbox, chain, and shafts were designed for. The next jam breaks a shaft or a gear instead of tripping a breaker. Find out **why** the load increased (a seized bearing, a dragging belt, product buildup) before changing the drive's power.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

**hp = torque × rpm ÷ 5,252.** Speed reductions **multiply torque** by the ratio, so parts get bigger downstream. Efficiency losses turn into heat, which is why worm gears and slipping belts run hot. Fix the cause of an overload before adding power.

<TryIt title="Follow the Torque">

A **5 hp, 1,750 rpm** motor drives a 4-inch sheave belted to a 12-inch sheave on a gearbox with a **15:1** ratio.

1. Motor torque? 5 × 5,252 ÷ 1,750 ≈ **15 lb-ft**
2. Belt ratio? 12 ÷ 4 = **3:1**, so gearbox input ≈ 583 rpm and ≈ 45 lb-ft
3. Gearbox output (ignoring losses)? 583 ÷ 15 ≈ **39 rpm**; 45 × 15 ≈ **675 lb-ft**

That output shaft sees **45 times** the motor torque.

</TryIt>
