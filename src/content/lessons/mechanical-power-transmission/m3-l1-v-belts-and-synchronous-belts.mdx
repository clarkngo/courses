## Why Belts?

Belt drives are cheap, quiet, need no lubrication, absorb shock, and let you change speed just by swapping a sheave (pulley). They're everywhere: fans, pumps, compressors, conveyors, and machine tools.

---

## Belt Types

### V-Belts (Friction Drive)

A V-belt **wedges** into the sheave groove. The wedging creates friction that transmits power. A V-belt should ride **near the top of the groove** and never touch the bottom.

| Family | Sections | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical** | A, B, C, D, E | Older standard; the letter sets width and depth |
| **Narrow (wedge)** | 3V, 5V, 8V | More power in less space; common in modern drives |
| **Fractional hp** | 3L, 4L, 5L | Light duty: shop equipment, small fans |
| **Cogged** (e.g., AX, BX, 3VX, 5VX) | Notched underside | Flexes around smaller sheaves, runs cooler, slightly more efficient |
| **Banded** | Multiple belts joined by a top band | Stops belts from turning over or jumping off on pulsing loads |

The **number on the belt** usually encodes its length. For classical belts, a **B75** is a B-section belt, and the number relates to its length in inches. For narrow belts, a **5V1000** is a 5V belt with an outside length of 100.0 inches.

### Synchronous (Timing) Belts

Synchronous belts have **teeth** that mesh with grooved sprockets, like a chain made of rubber.

- **No slip**, so they keep exact timing
- **Higher efficiency** than V-belts (around 98%)
- Common profiles include curvilinear designs such as **HTD** (e.g., 8M, 14M, the number being the tooth pitch in mm) and other proprietary high-torque profiles
- Very sensitive to **misalignment** and to wrong tension

### Key Rules

- **Never pry or roll belts onto sheaves.** It breaks the internal tensile cords even if you can't see it.
- **Replace all belts in a set at once.** New belts are slightly longer than stretched old ones, so mixing them puts the load on the shortest belts.
- Use **matched sets** from the same maker.
- **Check sheaves** with a groove gauge. Worn grooves let the belt bottom out and slip, and a "dished" sidewall eats belts quickly.

---

## Tensioning: The Deflection Method

The most common field method:

1. Measure the **span length** between sheave contact points (in inches).
2. Target deflection is **1/64 inch per inch of span**. A 32-inch span targets **1/2 inch** of deflection.
3. Use a **tension tester** to push the belt at mid-span until it deflects that amount.
4. Compare the force reading to the **belt maker's table** for the belt section and sheave size.
5. Adjust the motor and recheck.

Other options include **sonic tension meters**, which measure the belt's natural vibration frequency, and are especially useful for synchronous belts.

### Too Loose vs. Too Tight

| Too loose | Too tight |
|---|---|
| Slip, squeal, burning smell | Overloaded shaft and motor **bearings** |
| Glazed, hard sidewalls | Shaft fatigue |
| Heat, fast belt wear | Extra stretch and heat in the belt |
| Belt flip or jump on start | Wasted energy |

## Sheave Alignment

Belts need sheaves in the **same plane**.

- **Parallel (offset) misalignment:** shafts parallel but sheaves offset
- **Angular misalignment:** shafts not parallel

Check with a **straightedge or string** across both sheave faces (accounting for any difference in face widths) or with a **laser alignment tool** that clips to the sheave. Belt makers publish limits. A common guideline is about **½°** for V-belts and roughly **¼°** for synchronous belts. Synchronous drives need tighter alignment.

<Pitfall title="Fixing Squeal with Belt Dressing">

**Common mistake:** a belt squeals at startup, so someone sprays belt dressing.

Dressing is a temporary, sticky fix that attracts dirt and degrades rubber. Squeal means **slip**: low tension, worn sheaves, oil contamination, or an overloaded drive. Find and fix the cause.

</Pitfall>

## Belt Failure Clues

| What you see | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Glazed, shiny sidewalls | Slipping, from low tension or worn sheaves |
| Cracks on the underside | Sheaves too small, age, heat |
| Belt turned over in the groove | Misalignment, worn sheaves, pulsing loads (use banded belts) |
| One belt in a set much looser | Mixed old and new belts, or a damaged cord |
| Sudden break with clean cords | Shock load, or pried on during installation |
| Synchronous belt with sheared teeth | Too loose (tooth jumping), overload, sprocket too small |

---

## Key Takeaway

V-belts grip by **wedging**; synchronous belts mesh **teeth** and never slip. Never **pry** belts on; replace **whole sets**; **gauge sheaves**. Tension with **1/64 inch per inch of span** and the maker's force tables, then re-tension V-belts after run-in. Align carefully, especially synchronous drives.

<TryIt title="Calculate Target Deflection">

A fan drive has a span of **40 inches** between sheave contact points.

Target deflection = 40 × 1/64 = **0.625 inch (5/8 inch)**.

Push the belt at mid-span with a tension tester until it deflects 5/8 inch, read the force, and compare it to the maker's table for that belt section and small-sheave diameter.

</TryIt>
