## Gear Types

| Gear type | Shaft arrangement | Strengths | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spur** | Parallel | Simple, efficient, no thrust | Noisy at speed |
| **Helical** | Parallel | Smooth, quiet, high capacity | Creates **axial thrust** on bearings |
| **Herringbone / double helical** | Parallel | Helical benefits without net thrust | Costlier |
| **Bevel (straight or spiral)** | Intersecting, usually 90° | Turns the drive a corner | Needs precise setup |
| **Worm** | Perpendicular, non-intersecting | High ratio in one stage, compact | Low efficiency at high ratios, heat, wear |
| **Planetary** | Inline | High torque in a small, coaxial package | Complex |

### Common Gearbox (Reducer) Styles

- **Helical inline** — motor and output on the same axis
- **Parallel shaft** — output offset from input
- **Helical-bevel (right angle)** — efficient right-angle drive
- **Worm** — compact right-angle drive, high ratios
- **Shaft-mount** — hangs directly on the driven shaft, held by a torque arm
- **Gearmotor** — motor and reducer combined as one unit

## Ratings and Service Factor

Gearboxes are rated for torque and power under **smooth, uniform** operating conditions. Real machines have shock loads, frequent starts, and long run times. The **service factor (SF)** accounts for that:

**Required gearbox rating ≥ actual load × service factor**

Gear makers and **AGMA** publish service factor tables based on the driven equipment, hours per day, and the prime mover. For example, a uniformly loaded conveyor running 10 hours a day might need SF ≈ 1.0, while a heavy shock-loaded crusher might need 2.0 or more.

Check **thermal capacity** too: a gearbox can be strong enough but still overheat if it's working hard continuously.

---

## Worm Gears and "Self-Locking"

High-ratio worm gears can resist being driven backward from the output. That's sometimes called **self-locking**. But vibration, wear, and lubrication can let them **back-drive** unexpectedly.

> **A worm gearbox is not a brake.** If a load must be held (a hoist, an incline, a lift), use a **rated brake** or holding device.

## Gearbox Lubrication

Gear oil does three jobs: **lubricate** the teeth and bearings, **carry away heat**, and **flush away wear debris**.

### Basic Care

- Use the **specified oil type and viscosity grade** (e.g., ISO VG 220 or 320). Worm gears often need special compounded or synthetic oils.
- Check **oil level** with the gearbox stopped and the oil settled, at the sight glass or level plug for the **actual mounting position**. Mounting position changes where the level should be.
- Change oil on schedule, or better, based on **oil analysis**. The **first change after break-in** is especially important because it removes early wear particles.
- Look for **milky oil** (water), **dark, burnt-smelling** oil (overheating), or **glitter** (metal wear).

### Breathers and Seals

As a gearbox heats and cools, it "breathes" air in and out. A plain vent breather pulls in **dust and humid air**.

- Replace open vents with **desiccant breathers** in dirty or wet areas.
- A **plugged breather** lets pressure build, pushing oil past seals.
- Leaking seals let dirt in and oil out. Check shaft surfaces for grooves when replacing them.

<Pitfall title="Overfilling to Be Safe">

**Common mistake:** topping up a gearbox well above the level mark "so it doesn't run low."

Too much oil makes the gears churn it, which raises temperature, causes foaming, and pushes oil past seals and breathers. Fill to the correct level **for the mounting position**, no higher.

</Pitfall>

## Reading Gear Tooth Wear

| Pattern | Likely meaning |
|---|---|
| **Even polish across the whole tooth** | Normal run-in |
| **Contact only at one end of the tooth** | Misalignment, housing distortion, or shaft deflection |
| **Pitting (small craters)** | Surface fatigue from overload or poor lubrication; slight early pitting can stabilize |
| **Scuffing / scoring** (dragged, torn streaks) | Lubricant film broke down: wrong oil, overheating, or overload |
| **Abrasive wear** (scratched, dull surfaces) | Contamination in the oil |
| **Broken tooth** | Shock overload or fatigue crack from a stress raiser |

---

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rising temperature** compared with normal (an infrared thermometer trend is easy to collect)
- **New noise**: whine (often tooth mesh), growl or rumble (often bearings), knocking (broken tooth or loose part)
- **Vibration** changes
- **Leaks** at seals or the split line
- **Oil analysis** showing rising iron, water, or viscosity change

---

## Key Takeaway

Helical gears are smooth and efficient but create **thrust**; worm gears give **high ratios** but run hotter and are **not brakes**. Size with a **service factor**. Fill to the correct level **for the mounting position**, use the **right oil**, keep **breathers** clean and dry, and use **oil analysis** and **temperature trends** to catch problems early.

<TryIt title="Apply a Service Factor">

A conveyor drive needs **400 lb-ft** at the gearbox output. The service factor table for your application gives **SF = 1.4**.

Required rating = 400 × 1.4 = **560 lb-ft**.

If the catalog's options are rated 450, 600, and 900 lb-ft, choose the **600 lb-ft** unit and also confirm its **thermal rating** for continuous duty.
