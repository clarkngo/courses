## Why Alignment Pays

Misalignment is one of the leading causes of rotating equipment failure. When two coupled shafts don't share a common centerline, every revolution flexes the coupling and loads the bearings and seals with extra forces. Results include:

- **Shorter bearing life** (remember: life drops with the cube of load)
- **Seal leaks** on pumps and gearboxes
- **Coupling wear**, heat, and failure
- **Higher vibration** and noise
- **Wasted energy**
- **Cracked shafts** from cyclic bending

- **Offset (parallel) misalignment:** centerlines are parallel but displaced. Measured in **thousandths of an inch (mils)** or millimeters.
- **Angular misalignment:** centerlines meet at an angle. Usually expressed as a **slope**, such as mils per inch (or mm per 100 mm).

Real machines almost always have **both**, and in **both planes**:

- **Vertical plane:** corrected by adding or removing **shims** under the feet
- **Horizontal plane:** corrected by **moving the machine sideways**

So an alignment job corrects four values: vertical offset, vertical angle, horizontal offset, and horizontal angle.

### Which Machine Moves?

One machine is the **stationary** reference (often the pump or gearbox, which is piped or fixed). The other is the **movable** machine, usually the motor.

## Soft Foot: Fix It First

**Soft foot** is when one or more machine feet don't sit flat on the base. Tightening the hold-down bolt **distorts the machine frame**, which can bend the shaft, pinch bearings, and make alignment readings jump around every time you loosen a bolt.

### Checking for Soft Foot

1. With all feet bolted down, set a dial indicator (or use the laser system's soft-foot function) at one foot.
2. **Loosen that one bolt** and watch how far the foot rises.
3. Retighten, and repeat at each foot.
4. A common acceptance limit is about **0.002 inch (2 mils)** of movement.

### Types of Soft Foot

| Type | What's happening | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Parallel** (short leg) | Foot is simply too low | Add shims under that foot |
| **Angular / bent foot** | Foot or base is at an angle | Step shims, machine the foot, or correct the base |
| **Induced** | Pipe strain or a bent frame pulls the machine | Fix the pipe supports or the frame |
| **Squishy** | Too many shims, dirt, rust, or paint under the foot | Clean, and replace stacks with fewer, thicker shims |

<Pitfall title="The Shim Stack from History">

**Common mistake:** finding eight rusty, mismatched shims under one foot and adding a ninth.

Thick stacks of thin shims act like a spring, so readings won't repeat and soft foot returns. Pull the old stack, clean the base, and replace with **as few shims as possible** (a common guide is no more than three or four per foot), using clean pre-cut stainless shims.

</Pitfall>

## Pipe Strain

Pipe that doesn't line up with a pump's flanges can be forced into place, pulling the pump out of position. Check by monitoring the coupling with indicators while loosening the flange bolts. If the shaft moves more than a couple of mils, **fix the piping supports**, not the alignment.

---

## Key Takeaway

Misalignment is **offset** and **angular**, in both **vertical** (shims) and **horizontal** (move sideways) planes. Before aligning, **clean**, check **pipe strain**, and fix **soft foot** to about **2 mils or less**. Use few, clean shims.

<TryIt title="Soft Foot Check">

You check a motor's four feet by loosening one bolt at a time and measure lift of:

- Front left: 1 mil
- Front right: 6 mils
- Rear left: 1 mil
- Rear right: 2 mils

Which foot needs attention? *(Front right at 6 mils is well over the ~2 mil guideline.)* Next, use feeler gauges at the corners of that foot to tell if it's **parallel** (same gap all around, so add shims) or **angular** (wedge-shaped gap, so a step shim or a base correction is needed).

</TryIt>
