## Whose Voice Is That?

*"You're so stupid." "No one else would put up with you." "You always ruin everything."*

After abuse, many survivors find that the cruel voice didn't leave when the abuser did. It moved inside. Now it shows up as your own thoughts, which makes it extra convincing.

This is called an **internalized critic**. When you hear a message often enough, especially from someone you depended on, your mind starts repeating it to you on its own, as if to get ahead of the next attack.

The first step is simply **noticing**: *That's not my voice. That's an echo.*

---

## Common Thinking Traps

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) names patterns of distorted thinking. After abuse, these show up again and again:

| Thinking trap | What it sounds like | A more accurate thought |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-blame** | "I must have provoked it." | "Abuse is a choice the abuser made. Nothing I did justified it." |
| **All-or-nothing** | "I'm completely broken." | "I'm hurt, and I'm also healing. Both are true." |
| **Mind reading** | "Everyone thinks I'm pathetic for staying." | "I don't know what they think. The people who matter will understand." |
| **Fortune telling** | "I'll never have a healthy relationship." | "I'm learning what healthy looks like. My future isn't written yet." |
| **Should statements** | "I should be over this by now." | "Healing takes as long as it takes. There's no deadline." |
| **Labeling** | "I'm an idiot for not seeing it." | "Abusers hide who they are on purpose. Missing the signs doesn't make me an idiot." |

You don't have to believe the more accurate thought right away. Just practicing it, again and again, slowly changes which voice feels true.

---

## Self-Compassion Is a Skill

Psychologist Kristin Neff's research describes **self-compassion** as having three parts:

1. **Mindfulness:** Noticing that you're hurting, without ignoring it or blowing it up. *"This is really hard right now."*
2. **Common humanity:** Remembering you're not the only one. *"Many people who've been through abuse feel exactly this way."*
3. **Self-kindness:** Treating yourself the way you'd treat a friend. *"I'm doing the best I can. I deserve care."*

Research links self-compassion to lower anxiety, depression, and shame, and it isn't the same as letting yourself off the hook. It's what makes real growth possible, because people take risks and learn more when failing doesn't mean being attacked from the inside.

<Pitfall title="Fighting the Critic Head-On">

Yelling back at the critic, *"Shut up! I'm not stupid!"*, often makes it louder, because it turns your mind into a battlefield again. Try curiosity instead: *"There's that voice again. It's trying to protect me from being hurt by criticism. Thanks, but I don't need that kind of protection anymore."* You don't have to defeat it. You just don't have to obey it.

</Pitfall>

## Making Room to Grieve

Herman's second stage of recovery is **remembrance and mourning**. Survivors often grieve things that are hard to name:

- The person you thought they were, and the future you planned together
- Years of your life, opportunities, friendships, or money
- Your sense of safety, trust, or innocence
- The version of yourself before the abuse

Grief after abuse can be confusing. You might miss someone who hurt you. You might feel relief and sadness at the same time. **All of it makes sense.** Grief isn't proof that you made the wrong choice. It's proof that you loved, hoped, and lost something real.

Go gently. Big memories are often best worked through with a trauma-informed therapist, not alone.

<TryIt title="Talk Back with Kindness">

1. **Catch one critical thought** this week. Write it down exactly as it sounds.
2. **Ask:** *Whose voice does this sound like? Is it the whole truth?*
3. **Name the thinking trap** from the table, if one fits.
4. **Write a response** the way you'd talk to a close friend who said this about themselves.

If writing a kind response feels impossible, that's okay. Try: *"I'm not ready to believe something kinder yet, but I'm willing to consider it."* That counts.

</TryIt>

<Quiz question="A survivor thinks, 'I should have seen the red flags. I'm so stupid.' Which response best reflects this lesson?">
<div>

**Notice the self-blame and labeling, and answer with something like: "Abusers often hide their behavior early on. Missing signs that were deliberately hidden doesn't make me stupid. I'm learning to see them now."**

The critic's message gets the responsibility backward. Abusers often show charm and intensity early and bring out control slowly, which makes the pattern hard to spot from inside. A self-compassionate response is honest about the pain and puts responsibility where it belongs.
