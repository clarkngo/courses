## Isolation Was a Tactic. Connection Is the Cure.

Abusers often cut their targets off from friends, family, and community, because isolated people are easier to control. So it makes sense that Herman's third stage of recovery is **reconnection**: with others, and with yourself.

Reconnecting can feel risky. Trust was used against you. This lesson is about rebuilding it carefully, at your pace, with people who've earned it.

---

## Rebuilding Your Support Network

Think in **circles**, not all-or-nothing:

| Circle | Who | What you share |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner** | 1–3 people who have proven safe | The real stuff: fears, setbacks, the full story |
| **Middle** | Friends, coworkers, community members you enjoy | Company, fun, practical help, some sharing |
| **Outer** | Acquaintances, neighbors, online groups | Light connection and a sense of belonging |

People can move between circles as they show who they are. You don't owe anyone a spot in your inner circle.

**Reaching back out** to people you lost touch with can feel awkward. You don't have to explain everything. A simple message works: *"I've been through a really hard few years and I've missed you. Would you want to get coffee?"* Some people won't respond the way you hope. Others will surprise you.

## Professional Support

### Therapy

A **trauma-informed** therapist understands how abuse affects the brain, body, and relationships, and won't blame you or push you faster than you can go. Treatments with strong research behind them for trauma include:

- **Trauma-Focused CBT** and **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT):** Work with the thoughts and beliefs trauma left behind.
- **Prolonged Exposure (PE):** Gently, gradually face memories and situations you avoid.
- **EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing):** Process memories using guided eye movements or other back-and-forth stimulation.

**Questions to ask a possible therapist:**

- "What's your experience working with survivors of abuse?"
- "How do you approach trauma work?"
- "What happens if I feel overwhelmed in a session?"

It's okay to try a few therapists before you find the right fit. Research shows the relationship with your therapist is one of the biggest predictors of whether therapy helps.

**Low-cost options:** domestic violence programs (many offer free counseling), community mental health centers, university training clinics, sliding-scale therapists, and victim compensation programs, which in many US states can help pay for counseling for crime victims.

<Pitfall title="Couples Counseling with an Abusive Partner">

It seems logical to work on the relationship together, but most domestic violence experts advise **against** couples counseling when there's abuse. Abuse isn't a communication problem. It's a power problem. Being honest in session can lead to punishment at home, and the abuser can use the therapist's words as a weapon. If your partner wants to change, specialized programs for people who abuse are the place to start, alongside individual support for you.

</Pitfall>

### Support groups

Being in a room, or a video call, with people who *get it* can heal shame in a way nothing else does. Local domestic violence programs often run free survivor groups. Look for groups led by trained facilitators.

## Healthy vs. Familiar

After abuse, intensity can feel like love and calm can feel boring. Your nervous system may be drawn to what's familiar, not what's good for you. Learn the signs:

| Early red flags | Green flags |
|---|---|
| Moves very fast: "soulmate" talk, pushing to commit quickly | Lets things develop at a comfortable pace |
| Overwhelming attention and flattery ("love bombing") | Steady, consistent interest |
| Talks badly about every ex ("They were all crazy") | Takes some responsibility for past relationships |
| Gets upset when you have plans without them | Encourages your friendships and interests |
| Tests or pushes small boundaries | Respects a "no" the first time, without sulking |
| Jealousy framed as love | Trust, and comfort with your independence |
| You feel anxious, confused, or on edge | You feel calm, like yourself, and free to disagree |

**Your body is information.** If you keep feeling small, anxious, or like you're walking on eggshells, take that seriously, even if you can't explain why yet.

<TryIt title="Map Your Circles">

1. **Draw three circles** (inner, middle, outer) on paper or in your head.
2. **Place people** where they belong right now. It's okay if your inner circle is empty or has only a pet, a therapist, or a hotline in it.
3. **Pick one small step:** someone to reconnect with, a group to look into, or a therapist to email.

You're not rebuilding your whole network this week. You're adding one thread.

</TryIt>

<div>

**Their nervous system may be confusing intensity with connection. The calm relationship may feel "boring" because it lacks the highs and lows they got used to, not because it's wrong.**

After abuse, unpredictability can feel like chemistry because it matches what the body learned. That doesn't mean the survivor has to date the steady person, but it's worth noticing the pull and judging people by how they *treat* you, not by how intense it feels. Over time, safety usually starts to feel less boring and more like relief.
