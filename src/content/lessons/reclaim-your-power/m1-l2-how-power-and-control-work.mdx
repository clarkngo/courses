## Naming It Takes Away Its Power

Abuse works best when it has no name. If you can't describe what's happening, you can't explain it to anyone, and you start to wonder if the problem is you.

This lesson gives you words. You might read some of these and feel a jolt of recognition. That's normal. Take a breath, and remember you can stop at any time.

---

## The Tactics of Control

Advocates at the Domestic Abuse Intervention Programs in Duluth, Minnesota, interviewed survivors and mapped the tactics they described. The result was the well-known **Power and Control Wheel**. Physical and sexual violence form the rim of the wheel, and everyday tactics are the spokes that keep it turning. Here's a plain-language version:

| Tactic | What it can look like |
|---|---|
| **Intimidation** | Looks, gestures, smashing things, hurting pets, showing weapons |
| **Emotional abuse** | Name-calling, humiliation, making you feel crazy, guilt trips |
| **Isolation** | Controlling who you see, where you go, and who you talk to; moving you away from family |
| **Minimizing, denying, blaming** | "It wasn't that bad." "That never happened." "You made me do it." |
| **Using children** | Threats to take the kids, using them to pass messages, undermining you as a parent |
| **Privilege and entitlement** | Treating you like a servant, making all the big decisions, defining "roles" |
| **Economic abuse** | Controlling money, blocking you from working, running up debt in your name |
| **Coercion and threats** | Threats to leave, to harm you, to harm themselves, to report you, to "out" you |
| **Digital abuse** | Tracking your location, reading your messages, demanding passwords, posting private images |

Not every abusive relationship uses every tactic. Some rarely or never use physical violence. **The pattern matters more than any single incident.**

## Coercive Control

Sociologist Evan Stark coined the term **coercive control** for an ongoing pattern of domination that wraps around a person's whole life. It doesn't show up as one big event. It's thousands of small rules, checks, and punishments that slowly shrink your world.

Signs include:

- You run your choices past an imagined reaction first: *"What will they say if I...?"*
- You have to explain where you were, what you spent, and who you talked to.
- The rules keep changing, so you can never get it right.
- You've lost touch with friends, hobbies, or parts of yourself you used to love.

Several countries, including England and Wales, Scotland, and Ireland, now treat coercive control as a crime, along with some US states. The law is starting to see what survivors have always known: **you can be trapped without being locked in.**

## Gaslighting

**Gaslighting** means making someone doubt their own memory, perception, or sanity. The term comes from a 1938 play in which a husband secretly dims the gaslights and then insists his wife is imagining it.

It sounds like:

- "That never happened. You're remembering it wrong."
- "You're too sensitive. It was a joke."
- "Everyone agrees with me. You're the problem."

Over time, gaslighting teaches you to trust the abuser's version of reality over your own. Part of reclaiming your power is learning to trust what you saw, heard, and felt again.

Over time the calm periods often get shorter, and some relationships drop them altogether. Not every abusive relationship follows this exact loop. But the swing between cruelty and kindness creates a powerful bond, sometimes called a **trauma bond**. Your brain learns to look for relief from the same person who causes the pain. That isn't weakness. It's how attachment works under threat.

## The Wrong Question

"Why didn't you just leave?" puts the responsibility on the person who was harmed. Better questions are: *Why did they abuse? What made leaving dangerous or impossible?*

Real reasons people stay, or go back, include:

- **Danger.** Research consistently finds that the period around leaving is one of the most dangerous times in an abusive relationship. (Module 2 covers safety planning for exactly this reason.)
- **Money.** No access to income, accounts, or housing.
- **Children, pets, and immigration status.** Threats tied to the people and things you'd lose.
- **Trauma bonding and hope.** Loving the person they are during the calm periods.
- **Isolation.** No one left to turn to.
- **Shame and disbelief.** Fear that no one will believe you, or that you'll be blamed.

<Pitfall title="Measuring Yourself Against the Exit">

Survivors often judge themselves for how long they stayed: *"I should have known. I'm so stupid."* Staying was very likely a survival strategy, a way of managing a dangerous person with the resources you had. You weren't failing to leave. You were keeping yourself, and maybe others, alive.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Find Your Words">

Look back at the tactics table. Privately, and only if it feels okay:

1. **Mark any tactics you recognize.** You don't have to explain them or rank them.
2. **Pick one** and write a single sentence about it in plain words, like *"They controlled my money,"* not *"Maybe I was bad with money."*
3. **Notice your body** as you write. If it gets intense, pause and look around the room before you go on.

Writing it in plain words is a small act of reclaiming. Your reality, in your words.

</TryIt>

**This is the reconciliation and calm phase of the cycle of abuse. The kindness is real enough to create hope, and that hope helps form a trauma bond.**

The swing between harm and warmth is part of what makes abusive relationships so hard to leave. The loving moments don't cancel out the abuse, and they don't mean the survivor was foolish for hoping. Seeing the pattern makes it possible to judge the relationship by its whole cycle instead of its best moments.
