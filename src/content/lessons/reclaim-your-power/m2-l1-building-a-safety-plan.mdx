## Safety Comes First

Herman's first stage of recovery is **safety**, and it comes first for a reason. You can't do deep healing while you're still bracing for the next blow. A **safety plan** is a personalized, practical plan for lowering your risk and knowing what you'll do in a crisis.

It isn't a promise that nothing bad will happen. It's a way to take back some control over what you *can* control.

> **The best safety plans are made with a trained advocate.** In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) offers free, confidential help building one. Local domestic violence programs do too. This lesson helps you get ready for that conversation; it doesn't replace it.

## Warning Signs of Higher Danger

Researchers such as Jacquelyn Campbell, who developed the **Danger Assessment**, have identified factors linked to a higher risk of serious or deadly violence. Talk to an advocate right away if any of these are true:

- The person has **choked or strangled** you, or tried to.
- They have access to a **gun**, or have threatened you with a weapon.
- They have threatened to **kill you**, your children, or themselves.
- The violence is getting **more frequent or more severe**.
- They are **extremely jealous** or control most of your daily activities.
- You have recently **left**, or they suspect you're about to.

<Pitfall title="Underestimating Strangulation">

Many survivors don't mention being choked because it left no marks, or it "only lasted a second." Strangulation is one of the strongest predictors of later homicide, and it can cause serious injury that shows up hours or days later. If it has ever happened to you, tell a medical provider and an advocate, even if it happened a while ago.

</Pitfall>

## The Four Layers of a Safety Plan

### 1. Physical safety

- **Safe places:** Know which rooms in your home have exits and no weapons (avoid kitchens and bathrooms during an argument if you can).
- **A signal:** A code word or emoji you can send to a trusted person that means "call for help."
- **A go-bag:** If you might leave, quietly keep an ID, birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, medications, keys, some cash, a phone charger, and important documents somewhere safe, like with a friend.
- **An exit route:** Know how you'd get out, where you'd go, and how you'd get there.
- **Children:** Teach them how to call 911 and where to go. Don't make them responsible for protecting you.

### 2. Digital safety

- Check your phone for **location sharing** (Find My, Google Maps, Life360) and unfamiliar apps.
- **Change passwords** and turn on two-factor authentication from a device the abuser hasn't touched.
- Look for **shared accounts**: cloud photos, family plans, smart-home devices, connected cars.
- **Be careful about timing.** Suddenly locking someone out can tip them off. An advocate can help you decide what to change and when.

### 3. Financial safety

- Open an **account in your name only**, at a different bank, with statements going somewhere safe.
- Pull your **free credit reports** (in the US, at annualcreditreport.com) to check for debt opened in your name.
- Make **copies of financial documents**: tax returns, pay stubs, account numbers.
- Ask about local programs. Many domestic violence organizations offer emergency funds or relocation help.

### 4. Emotional safety

- Name **two or three people** you can call when you're struggling.
- Write down **what calms you**: a playlist, a walk, a shower, holding something warm.
- Plan for **hard moments**: a court date, an anniversary, seeing the person in public.

## Safety Looks Different at Each Stage

| Where you are | Focus your plan on |
|---|---|
| **Still in the relationship** | Lowering risk day to day, a signal and a support person, quietly saving documents and money |
| **Preparing to leave** | Timing, a go-bag, a safe place to go, digital lockdown, an advocate, possibly a protection order |
| **Already out** | Changing routines, securing your home and devices, protection orders, telling work or school, emotional support |

**Protection orders** (also called restraining orders) are court orders that can require someone to stay away from you. Rules vary by place. An advocate or legal aid office can explain your options and help you file.

<TryIt title="Draft Your Safety Basics">

Keep this somewhere the abuser can't find it, or just think through the answers:

1. **My signal:** a word or emoji, and who I'd send it to.
2. **My safe person:** someone I could call at 2 a.m.
3. **My one next step:** the single most doable item from the four layers above (for example, "Check my phone's location sharing" or "Call the hotline to talk through a plan").

One step is enough for today.

</TryIt>

<div>

**Talk the timing over with an advocate first. Sudden digital changes can tip off an abusive partner, and the time around leaving is especially dangerous.**

The instinct to lock everything down is understandable, and digital safety really does matter. But abusers often notice when they lose access, and that can raise the danger. An advocate can help put the steps in a safer order, such as changing passwords from a safe device after you've left, or leaving some things as they are for now.
