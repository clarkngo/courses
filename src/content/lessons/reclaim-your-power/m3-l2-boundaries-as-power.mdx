## What a Boundary Is (and Isn't)

Abuse teaches you that your needs don't matter, your "no" will be punished, and your limits are up for negotiation. Boundaries are how you reverse that lesson.

A **boundary** is a decision about what *you* will do to protect your well-being. It isn't a rule for other people.

| A demand (tries to control others) | A boundary (controls your own actions) |
|---|---|
| "You can't yell at me." | "If you yell, I'll end the call." |
| "You have to respect my decisions." | "I'm not going to discuss my choices with you anymore." |
| "Stop texting me." | "I'll only reply to messages about the kids." |

This difference matters. You can't control another person's behavior. Trying is exhausting, and it puts your safety back in their hands. You *can* control your response. **That's where your power lives.**

---

## Kinds of Boundaries

- **Physical:** Your body, personal space, home, and belongings. *"Please knock before coming in."*
- **Emotional:** What feelings you take on and what you share. *"I'm not responsible for managing your mood."*
- **Time:** How you spend your hours. *"I'm not available after 8 p.m."*
- **Digital:** Your devices, accounts, and online presence. *"I don't share my passwords."*
- **Financial:** Your money and resources. *"I'm not able to lend money."*
- **Sexual:** Consent, always, every time, and changeable at any moment.

## Setting a Boundary

A simple structure:

1. **State it clearly and briefly.** *"I'm not going to talk about this."*
2. **Skip the long explanation.** Long reasons invite argument. "No" is a complete sentence.
3. **Follow through.** A boundary only works if you act on it. End the call. Leave the room. Don't reply.

Phrases that help:

- *"That doesn't work for me."*
- *"I'm not willing to do that."*
- *"I'll get back to you."* (Buying time is allowed.)
- *"I've already answered that."*

<Pitfall title="Expecting the Boundary to Feel Good Right Away">

Your first boundaries may come with a wave of guilt, fear, or the urge to apologize. That's the fawn response from Module 2 firing up, not a sign that you did something wrong. It usually fades with practice. Also expect pushback: people who benefited from your lack of boundaries often test new ones. **Pushback is proof the boundary is needed, not proof it's unfair.**

</Pitfall>

## When You Can't Walk Away Completely

Sometimes full separation isn't possible right away, or ever: a co-parent, a family member at holidays, a coworker. These strategies help you limit contact and protect yourself.

### No contact

Ending all communication: blocking numbers and accounts, not responding to messages passed through others. Often the safest and fastest route to healing when it's possible.

### Low contact

Contact only when necessary, on your terms: short, scheduled, public, or with a support person there.

### Gray rock

Becoming as boring as a gray rock: short, neutral, emotionless answers that give the person nothing to react to. *"Okay." "I'll think about it." "Noted."* Use it as a short-term tool, not a way of life. It can cost a lot to keep up, and if it raises the danger, go back to your safety plan.

### Parallel parenting

With a high-conflict or abusive co-parent, **parallel parenting** usually works better than co-parenting. Each parent runs their own household with little direct contact. Communication can go through a **co-parenting app** (many courts recommend or require them, and they keep a record), stay in writing, and stick to the children only. A family law attorney or advocate can help put the structure into a custody order.

### Document everything

When contact continues, keep records: dates, times, screenshots, what was said. Store them somewhere safe. Documentation helps with protection orders and custody decisions, and it protects you from gaslighting about what happened.

<TryIt title="Write One Boundary">

1. **Pick one situation** where your limits keep getting crossed. Start small, not with the hardest relationship in your life.
2. **Write the boundary** as an action you'll take: *"If ___, I will ___."*
3. **Plan the follow-through:** What exactly will you do, and who can support you if you feel guilty afterward?
4. **Notice what comes up** when you imagine saying it. Fear? Guilt? Relief? All of it is information.

If setting this boundary with this person could put you in danger, stop and talk it over with an advocate first. Safety always comes before assertiveness.

</TryIt>

**Turn the demand into a boundary based on their own actions, for example: "I'll only discuss pickup times and the kids' schedule. If the messages turn to criticism, I won't respond." Then follow through, ideally through a co-parenting app that keeps a record.**

The first statement depended on the ex choosing to change, which put the survivor's peace in the ex's hands. A boundary moves the power back to the survivor, because they control whether they respond. Keeping communication in writing and focused on the kids also limits openings for abuse and creates documentation.
