## Your Body Was Doing Its Job

Maybe you've left, or things are calmer, but you still jump at loud noises. You can't sleep. A certain tone of voice makes you freeze. You feel numb, or constantly on edge.

**Nothing is wrong with you.** Your nervous system learned that the world was dangerous, and it's still trying to protect you. It just hasn't gotten the message that the emergency is over.

This lesson explains what's going on and gives you tools to help your body catch up with your situation.

---

## The Four Survival Responses

When your brain senses a threat, it switches into survival mode faster than you can think. There are four common responses:

| Response | What it looks like | What it was protecting |
|---|---|---|
| **Fight** | Anger, arguing back, irritability | Pushing the threat away |
| **Flight** | Anxiety, restlessness, staying busy, leaving | Getting away from the threat |
| **Freeze** | Going blank, numb, unable to move or speak | Hiding, playing dead, waiting it out |
| **Fawn** | People-pleasing, apologizing, calming the other person | Staying safe by keeping the threat happy |

The term **fawn** was popularized by therapist Pete Walker. It's especially common in survivors of long-term abuse. If you got very good at reading someone's moods and keeping them calm, that was a skill that protected you. It isn't a personality flaw.

<Pitfall title="Blaming Yourself for Freezing">

Many survivors carry shame for not fighting back: *"Why didn't I do something?"* Freezing isn't a choice. It's an automatic response your brain picks when it decides that fighting or running would make things worse. In the moment, your nervous system made a split-second call to keep you alive.

</Pitfall>

---

## Triggers

A **trigger** is anything that reminds your nervous system of past danger: a smell, a song, a phrase, a time of year, a type of car, even a facial expression. Your body reacts as if the danger is happening *now*.

Triggers aren't a sign that you're "not over it." They're signals that point to where healing is still happening. Over time, and often with support, they usually get less intense.

Trauma tends to shrink the window, so it takes less to push you out of it. Healing widens it again. Grounding skills are how you get back in when you've been pushed out.

## Grounding Skills

Grounding brings your attention back to your body and the present moment. Try a few and keep the ones that work for you.

**If you're too activated (hyperarousal):**

- **Longer exhales.** Breathe in for 4 counts and out for 6 to 8. A longer out-breath signals your body to calm down.
- **Cold water.** Splash your face, or hold something cold.
- **Push against a wall** with both hands for 10 seconds, then let go.

**If you're shut down (hypoarousal):**

- **5-4-3-2-1.** Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, and 1 you can taste.
- **Move.** Stand up, stretch, shake out your hands, walk around the room.
- **Strong sensations.** A sour candy, peppermint, or a strong smell like coffee.

**Anytime:**

- **Orient.** Slowly look around the room and tell yourself: *"I'm here. It's [day], [year]. I'm in [place]. Right now, I'm safe."* (Only say "safe" if it's true. If it isn't, go back to your safety plan.)
- **A safe touch.** Put a hand on your chest or wrap your arms around yourself.

## When to Get More Help

Self-help skills are a good start, but you deserve more support if you have:

- Flashbacks, nightmares, or panic that disrupt daily life
- Trouble feeling anything at all for weeks at a time
- Using alcohol or drugs to cope
- Thoughts of hurting yourself (call or text **988** in the US)

Trauma-focused treatments such as **trauma-focused CBT**, **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)**, **Prolonged Exposure**, and **EMDR** have strong research behind them for PTSD. Module 4 covers how to find a good fit.

<TryIt title="Build a Grounding Kit">

1. **Try three skills** from the lists above this week, one at a time, when you're calm. Practicing when you're calm makes them easier to use when you're not.
2. **Rate each one** from 0 to 10 for how much it helped.
3. **Keep your top two** somewhere easy to find, like a phone note or a card in your wallet.

Optional: put together a small physical kit, such as a smooth stone, a mint, a photo, or headphones with a calming song.

</TryIt>
