## Two Kinds of Measurement

Paper quality is measured in two places:

1. **Online, continuously** — a **quality control system (QCS)** scanner traverses back and forth across the sheet near the reel, measuring basis weight, moisture, and caliper in real time and feeding the control loops.
2. **Offline, in the lab** — testers cut samples from the end of every parent roll and run physical tests that the scanner can't measure.

Because the scanner moves across the sheet while the sheet moves forward, it measures a zig-zag path. Software splits the data into:
- **MD variation** — changes over time (e.g., a stock flow swing)
- **CD profile** — differences across the width (e.g., a wet streak)

---

## Conditioning: The First Rule of the Lab

Paper's strength depends heavily on moisture. A sample tested straight off the reel will give different numbers than the same sample tested an hour later.

So test labs **condition** samples before testing — under TAPPI T 402 and ISO 187, the standard atmosphere is **23 °C and 50% relative humidity** — and let them reach equilibrium.

<Pitfall title="Testing Unconditioned Samples">

**Common mistake:** running a quick strength test on a sample straight from the reel to "get an early read."

A dry, warm sample right off the machine will usually test differently than the same paper conditioned to 50% RH. Decisions made from that number — changing starch or refining — can push the process in the wrong direction. Quick checks have their place, but never compare them against conditioned specs.

</Pitfall>

## The Core Properties

### Structural Properties

| Property | What it is | Units |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis weight / grammage** | Mass per area | g/m², or lb/1,000 ft² for containerboard |
| **Caliper** | Thickness | mils (0.001 in) or µm |
| **Moisture** | Water content | % |
| **Density / bulk** | Mass per volume (bulk is its inverse) | g/cm³ |

### Strength Properties

| Test | What it simulates | Who cares most |
|---|---|---|
| **Tensile** | Pulling force until break, MD and CD | Everyone; runnability on presses and winders |
| **Tear** (Elmendorf) | Force to continue a tear | Sack paper, wrapping |
| **Burst** (Mullen) | Pressure needed to rupture the sheet | Linerboard — historically the key box spec |
| **Ring Crush (RCT)** | Edgewise compression of a ring of paper | Linerboard and medium; predicts box stacking strength |
| **SCT** (short-span compression) | Edgewise compression over a very short span | Increasingly preferred over RCT for containerboard |
| **CMT** (Concora) | Crushing a corrugated strip of medium | Corrugating medium |

Compression tests have become more important than burst over the past few decades because box buyers now specify **Edge Crush Test (ECT)** grades. Liner and medium compression directly drive ECT — you'll calculate that in the box course.

### Surface and Optical Properties

| Test | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Cobb** | Grams of water absorbed by an area of paper in a set time — tests sizing |
| **Porosity** (Gurley, Sheffield) | Air flow through the sheet |
| **Smoothness** (Sheffield, Parker Print-Surf) | Surface roughness |
| **Brightness** | Blue-light reflectance (ISO or TAPPI scale) |
| **Opacity** | How much light passes through |

## Statistical Thinking

A single test result means little. Mills track results on **control charts** with upper and lower control limits. The goal is a process that is **stable and centered** — not one that's constantly adjusted after every test.

- Reacting to every random point (**over-control** or "tampering") makes variation **worse**.
- A run of points trending in one direction, or a point outside the control limits, signals a real change worth investigating.

Customers increasingly ask for **Cpk** or similar capability numbers showing how comfortably the mill stays within spec.

---

## Key Takeaway

The **QCS scanner** controls basis weight, moisture, and caliper in real time, separating MD and CD variation. The **lab** conditions samples to 23 °C / 50% RH, then measures strength (tensile, burst, RCT, SCT, CMT), sizing (Cobb), and surface/optical properties. For containerboard, **compression strength** now matters more than burst.

<TryIt title="Calculate a Cobb Value">

A lab weighs a 100 cm² liner sample at 1.250 g. After 120 seconds in contact with water and blotting, it weighs 1.310 g. Cobb is reported in g/m².

Water absorbed = 1.310 − 1.250 = 0.060 g. The area is 100 cm² = 0.01 m², so 0.060 g ÷ 0.01 m² = **Cobb₁₂₀ = 60 g/m²**. That's weakly sized for many liner specs. Watch the unit conversion: forgetting that 100 cm² is only 0.01 m² is the most common Cobb math mistake.

</TryIt>
