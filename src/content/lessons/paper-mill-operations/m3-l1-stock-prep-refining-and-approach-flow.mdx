## Refining: Developing the Fiber

Pulp straight from the digester or pulper doesn't bond well. **Refining** fixes that. Stock is pumped between two discs (or a cone and shell) covered in metal **bars**, rotating against each other at a very small gap.

Refining does three things to fibers:

1. **Internal fibrillation** — loosens the fiber wall so the fiber becomes flexible and collapses flat.
2. **External fibrillation** — raises tiny fibrils on the surface, like fraying a rope, giving far more bonding area.
3. **Cutting** — shortens fibers. Usually undesirable for strength grades.

More refining means **more bonding → higher tensile and burst strength, a denser, smoother sheet**. But it comes at a cost.

### Freeness: The Number Everyone Watches

**Freeness** measures how quickly water drains from a pulp sample. In North America it's reported as **Canadian Standard Freeness (CSF)** in milliliters. (Europe uses Schopper-Riegler, °SR, which runs in the opposite direction.)

- **High CSF** = drains fast = lightly refined
- **Low CSF** = drains slowly = heavily refined

Refining creates a tug-of-war:

| More refining (lower CSF) | Effect |
|---|---|
| ✅ | Stronger, denser, smoother sheet |
| ❌ | Water drains slower on the wire |
| ❌ | Wetter sheet entering the dryers → more steam, or a slower machine |
| ❌ | More refiner electricity |

<Pitfall title="Refining Your Way Out of a Strength Problem">

**Common mistake:** a burst test comes back low, so the refiner load gets cranked up.

Strength improves — but freeness drops, the wire can't drain the stock, the sheet hits the dryers wetter, and the machine has to slow down. You traded a quality problem for a production problem. Before adding refining, check the fiber furnish, starch dosage, and wet-end chemistry. Refining is a lever, not a cure-all.

</Pitfall>

## Blending and the Approach Flow

After refining, stocks are combined in a **blend chest** — softwood, hardwood, recycled fiber, and **broke** (the mill's own off-spec or trimmed paper, repulped and reused). Additives are dosed here or just downstream.

The **approach flow** is the final stretch from the **machine chest** to the headbox. Its job is to deliver perfectly uniform, clean, pulse-free stock.

**White water** — the water that drains through the wire — is collected in the **wire pit** and reused to dilute the incoming stock at the fan pump. This short circulation loop saves water and recaptures fine fibers and fillers that drained through.

### The Headbox

The **headbox** turns a pipe of stock into a thin, perfectly even **jet** as wide as the machine — sometimes over 10 meters. Inside, a tube bank and turbulence generators break up fiber clumps (**flocs**). The jet exits through an adjustable **slice lip**.

Modern headboxes use **dilution control**: dozens of small valves across the width add white water locally to fine-tune basis weight in the **cross-machine direction (CD)**.

---

## Key Takeaway

**Refining** develops bonding at the expense of drainage, and **freeness (CSF)** tracks that trade-off. Stocks are blended, then the **approach flow** meters, dilutes, cleans, and screens the stock before the **headbox** delivers an even jet onto the wire.

<TryIt title="See Freeness in Your Kitchen">

Tear a sheet of paper towel and a sheet of copy paper into small pieces. Soak each in a separate cup of water and blend or stir hard until mushy. Pour each slurry through a kitchen strainer and watch the drainage.

The towel pulp (lightly refined, bulky) drains fast — high freeness. The copy paper pulp (more refined, more fines) drains slower — lower freeness.

</TryIt>
