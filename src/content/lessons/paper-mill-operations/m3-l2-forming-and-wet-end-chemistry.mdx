## The Forming Section

The headbox jet lands on the **forming fabric** (still called the **wire**, even though modern fabrics are woven plastic). In a few meters and a fraction of a second, the stock goes from ~99% water to a self-supporting wet web at roughly 20% solids.

Water is removed by elements under the fabric, in this order:

| Element | How it drains |
|---|---|
| **Forming board** | Supports the fabric where the jet lands; gentle initial drainage |
| **Foils** | Angled blades that create a mild vacuum as the fabric passes; also agitate stock to break up flocs |
| **Vacuum boxes** | Suction boxes that pull harder once a mat has formed |
| **Couch roll** | A perforated suction roll at the end; the web is transferred off the wire here |

You'll often see a **dry line** partway down the table — the point where the glossy water layer on top disappears and the sheet turns matte. Operators watch it: if the dry line moves, drainage has changed.

### Fourdrinier vs. Twin-Wire Formers

- **Fourdrinier:** a single flat wire; water drains downward only. Simple and still common for containerboard.
- **Gap and hybrid formers:** the jet is caught between **two fabrics**, draining water from both sides. This gives better **formation** (evenness), less **two-sidedness** (different top and bottom surfaces), and much higher speed. Most new printing-paper and tissue machines use twin-wire forming.
- **Multi-ply forming:** board machines often form two or three plies separately — for example, a cheaper recycled middle ply between clean outer plies — and combine them while wet.

## Machine Direction and Cross Direction

Because the jet is moving at nearly the same speed as the wire, most fibers line up pointing **downstream**. That gives every sheet of paper a "grain."

- **MD (machine direction):** the direction the paper travels. Higher tensile strength and stiffness.
- **CD (cross direction):** across the width. More expansion and shrinkage with moisture.

The **jet-to-wire speed ratio** controls how strongly fibers align. In corrugated board, the corrugator runs the paper so the flutes go across it — you'll see why that matters in the box course.

## Wet-End Chemistry

The **wet end** is where chemistry happens. Small doses of additives make a huge difference.

### Retention Aids
Fines, fillers, and additives are tiny — many would drain straight through the wire. **Retention aids** (usually cationic polymers, sometimes paired with microparticles like bentonite or colloidal silica) flocculate those small particles onto fibers so they stay in the sheet. Better retention means a cleaner white-water loop and less waste.

### Sizing
Untreated paper wicks water like a paper towel. **Internal sizing** makes it water-resistant:
- **AKD (alkyl ketene dimer)** and **ASA (alkenyl succinic anhydride)** react with the cellulose in neutral/alkaline papermaking — the modern standard.
- **Rosin size** with alum is the traditional acidic system, still used in some containerboard.

**Surface sizing** — starch applied at the size press — is covered in the next module.

### Strength Additives
- **Cationic starch** (dry strength) — the most common additive in containerboard.
- **Wet-strength resins** (such as PAE) — for towel, liquid packaging, and labels that must survive getting wet.

### Fillers
Printing papers add mineral **fillers** — precipitated calcium carbonate (PCC), ground calcium carbonate (GCC), clay, or titanium dioxide — for opacity, brightness, and smoothness. Fillers are cheaper than fiber but weaken bonding, so there's always a trade-off.

<Pitfall title="Ignoring Charge Balance">

**Common mistake:** adding more and more cationic polymer when retention drops.

Cellulose fibers and many contaminants are **anionic** (negatively charged). Recycled furnish brings in a lot of dissolved and colloidal "anionic trash" that soaks up cationic additives before they reach the fiber. Mills measure the **charge demand** of the white water (and the **zeta potential** of the fibers). When charge balance drifts, the fix might be a cheap fixative to neutralize the trash first — not more expensive retention aid.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

The **forming section** drains stock from ~99% to ~80% water with foils, vacuum, and the couch. **Twin-wire formers** drain from both sides for better formation and speed. Fiber alignment creates **MD and CD** properties every converter cares about. **Wet-end chemistry** — retention aids, sizing, starch, and fillers — tunes the sheet, and depends on keeping the system's charge in balance.

<TryIt title="Find the Grain">

Take a sheet of copy paper. Tear it slowly lengthwise, then across. One tear runs straight and clean — that's **with** the grain (MD). The other wanders. Next, wet one edge of a strip cut each way and watch which one curls more. You've just found MD and CD without a lab.

</TryIt>
