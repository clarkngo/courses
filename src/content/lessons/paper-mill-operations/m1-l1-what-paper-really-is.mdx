## A Sheet of Paper, Up Close

Tear a sheet of printer paper and hold the torn edge up to the light. Those fuzzy whiskers are **fibers** — tiny, hollow tubes of **cellulose** that once formed the cell walls of a tree.

Paper is nothing more than a thin, randomly woven mat of those fibers. There is usually no glue holding it together. So what keeps it from falling apart?

**Hydrogen bonds.** Cellulose is covered in hydroxyl (–OH) groups. When wet fibers are pressed close together and the water is removed, those groups bond directly to each other. Billions of these weak bonds, added up, give paper its strength.

This explains a fact every papermaker learns on day one:

> **Water is both the glue and the enemy.** Fibers need water to move into position and form bonds — but almost all of that water must be removed, and removing it is the most expensive part of making paper.

## The Main Families of Paper

Mills specialize. A machine built for tissue is very different from one built for linerboard. The grades fall into a few broad families:

| Family | Examples | What matters most |
|---|---|---|
| **Containerboard** | Linerboard, corrugating medium | Strength (burst, compression), low cost, high recycled content |
| **Boxboard / paperboard** | Cereal boxes, folding cartons, cup stock | Stiffness, printability, food safety |
| **Printing & writing** | Copy paper, coated magazine paper | Brightness, smoothness, opacity, runnability in printers |
| **Tissue & towel** | Bath tissue, paper towels, napkins | Softness, bulk, absorbency |
| **Specialty** | Filter papers, labels, release liners, currency | A single engineered property |

Containerboard — the brown paper that becomes corrugated boxes — is the largest grade by tonnage in North America. If you take the companion course *Flute to Freight*, you'll see exactly what happens to it next.

## Grammage and Basis Weight

Paper is sold by how much a standard area weighs.

- **Grammage** (used worldwide): grams per square meter, **g/m² (gsm)**. Copy paper is about 75–80 gsm.
- **Basis weight** (used in North America): pounds per a "basis ream" area — and the area depends on the grade. Containerboard uses **lb/1,000 ft²**, so a "42-lb liner" weighs 42 lb per 1,000 ft² (about 205 gsm).

<Pitfall title="Comparing Basis Weights Across Grades">

**Common mistake:** assuming a "20-lb" copy paper and a "26-lb" corrugating medium are similar weights.

They are measured on completely different basis areas (copy bond uses a 17 × 22 in, 500-sheet ream; containerboard uses 1,000 ft²). The 26-lb medium is actually about 127 gsm — far heavier than the 75 gsm copy paper. When in doubt, convert to **g/m²**, which never changes meaning.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

Paper is a network of cellulose fibers held together by hydrogen bonds that form as water is removed. Mills are specialized by grade, and every mill follows the same core path: **make pulp → form a wet web → press → dry → reel and wind.** The rest of the course is about doing each of those steps well.

<TryIt title="The Wet Strength Test">

Take two strips torn from the same sheet of ordinary copy paper. Pull one apart and notice the effort. Now dip the second in water for 30 seconds and pull again.

The wet strip falls apart with almost no force — water has broken the hydrogen bonds. Now try the same with a strip of paper towel. It holds together much better because towel grades include a **wet-strength resin** that adds bonds water can't dissolve. You've just seen why additives matter, which you'll study in Module 3.

</TryIt>
