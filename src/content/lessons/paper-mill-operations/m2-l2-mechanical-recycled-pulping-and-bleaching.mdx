## Mechanical Pulping: Tear Instead of Dissolve

Mechanical pulping separates fibers by brute force instead of chemistry. Because the lignin stays in, **yield is about 90–95%** — nearly double kraft.

- **Stone groundwood (SGW):** logs pressed against a rotating grindstone. The original method, now rare.
- **Thermomechanical pulp (TMP):** chips are steamed to soften the lignin, then shredded between rotating discs in a **refiner**. The dominant mechanical process today.
- **Chemi-thermomechanical pulp (CTMP):** a light chemical pretreatment before refining for stronger, cleaner fiber.

The trade-off:

| | Mechanical pulp (TMP) | Chemical pulp (kraft) |
|---|---|---|
| Yield | ~90–95% | ~45–55% |
| Strength | Low — fibers are damaged and stiff | High |
| Opacity & bulk | High | Lower |
| Brightness stability | Poor — lignin yellows in sunlight | Good once bleached |
| Energy | Very high electricity use in refiners | Largely self-supplied via recovery boiler |
| Typical use | Newsprint, magazine papers, paperboard middle plies | Linerboard, sack, fine papers, tissue |

That's why an old newspaper left in the sun turns yellow: the lignin was never removed.

## The Recycled Fiber Line

A **recycled fiber (RCF) mill** replaces the woodyard and digester with a line designed to turn bales of OCC into clean pulp.

Printing-grade recycling (old newspapers, office paper) adds a **deinking** step, usually **flotation**: air bubbles are blown through the stock, ink particles attach to the bubbles, and the froth is skimmed off.

### Stickies

**Stickies** are tacky contaminants — pressure-sensitive label adhesive, hot-melt glue, wax, and coatings. They pass through screens when soft, then deposit on wires, felts, and dryer cans. The result is holes, sheet breaks, and downtime for cleaning. Controlling stickies is one of the biggest challenges in any recycled mill.

## Bleaching

Unbleached kraft pulp is brown. For white grades, the remaining lignin must be removed or brightened in a **bleach plant** — a series of towers and washers, each running a different chemical stage.

Modern mills use **ECF (elemental chlorine free)** bleaching, built around **chlorine dioxide (ClO₂)**. A typical sequence looks like this:

  color="#047857"

    { badge: 'O', title: 'Oxygen delig.', detail: 'Removes ~40–50% of remaining lignin before bleaching' },
    { badge: 'D₀', title: 'Chlorine dioxide', detail: 'Main delignifying stage' },
    { badge: 'E', title: 'Alkaline extraction', detail: 'Washes out dissolved lignin; often boosted with O₂ or H₂O₂' },
    { badge: 'D₁', title: 'Chlorine dioxide', detail: 'Brightening stage' },
    { badge: 'D₂', title: 'Final ClO₂ / P', detail: 'Final brightness, ~88–90% ISO' },
  ]}
/>

- **ECF** eliminated elemental chlorine gas (Cl₂), dramatically cutting dioxin formation.
- **TCF (totally chlorine free)** uses only oxygen, ozone, and peroxide. It's used by some mills, especially in Europe, but is more expensive and harder to reach high brightness.
- Mechanical pulp is bleached differently — with **hydrogen peroxide** or **hydrosulfite** — which brightens the lignin without removing it (so yield is preserved, but the brightness can revert).

<Pitfall title="Chasing Brightness in the Bleach Plant">

**Common mistake:** overdosing chlorine dioxide to fix low brightness at the end of the line.

Brightness problems usually start upstream — a high or variable kappa number from the digester, or poor brown-stock washing that carries black liquor into the bleach plant ("carryover"). Throwing chemical at the final stage costs money and can damage fiber strength. Check washing and incoming kappa first.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

**Mechanical pulp** keeps the lignin: high yield and opacity, low strength, yellows with age. **Recycled fiber lines** clean OCC with pulpers, cleaners, and screens, fighting stickies all the way. **Bleaching** removes or brightens lignin; modern kraft mills use ECF sequences built on chlorine dioxide and oxygen.

<TryIt title="The Sunlight Test">

Cut a strip of newspaper (or the brown inner ply of a cereal box) and a strip of white copy paper. Cover half of each with a coin and leave them in direct sunlight for two or three days. Compare the covered and uncovered halves. The mechanical-pulp newsprint yellows visibly; the bleached kraft copy paper barely changes.

</TryIt>
