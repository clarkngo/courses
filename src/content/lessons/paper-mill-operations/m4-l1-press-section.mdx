## Why Pressing Matters So Much

The web leaves the couch at about **20% solids** — still four parts water to one part fiber. Water can leave in only three ways:

| Section | How water leaves | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Gravity, foils, vacuum | Cheapest |
| **Pressing** | Mechanical squeezing | Cheap |
| **Drying** | Evaporation with steam | Most expensive by far |

Every bit of water the press removes is water the dryers don't have to evaporate. A widely used rule of thumb:

> **Each 1 percentage point increase in solids leaving the press saves roughly 4% of dryer steam** — or lets the machine run faster with the same steam.

That's why press section performance is watched so closely.

## How a Press Nip Works

A **press nip** is the line of contact between two rolls loaded against each other. The wet sheet travels through the nip sandwiched against a **press felt** — a thick, porous fabric.

In the nip, pressure squeezes water out of the sheet and into the felt. The felt then has to give that water up — through **grooved** or **blind-drilled** roll covers, suction rolls, and **uhle boxes** (vacuum slots the felt passes over on every revolution).

### Rewet

As the sheet and felt leave the nip, the pressure releases and the fiber network springs back slightly, sucking some water **back** from the felt. This **rewet** is why sheets and felts are separated as soon as possible after the nip.

### Shoe Presses

A conventional roll nip lasts only a few milliseconds. A **shoe press** (extended nip press) replaces one roll with a stationary concave **shoe** and a flexible belt, making the nip up to about 10 times longer. More time under pressure means more water removed — often several points higher solids — with less **crushing** and better **bulk** preservation, which matters for board stiffness.

## Press Felts

Felts are consumables that directly control water removal. A new felt is open and drains well; as it ages, it **fills** with fines, fillers, pitch, and stickies and **compacts**.

Felt care includes:
- **Showers** — high-pressure needle showers and chemical cleaning showers
- **Uhle box vacuum and dwell** — monitored to track felt condition
- **Scheduled changes** — usually during planned shutdowns

<Pitfall title="Over-Loading the Nip">

**Common mistake:** raising nip load as far as it goes to chase dryness.

Past a point, water can't escape the sheet fast enough and is forced sideways through it, disrupting the fiber network — called **crushing**. It shows up as a mottled, blotchy appearance and lost strength. Too much load also compacts felts early and reduces bulk. Higher load is not always drier sheet; find the curve for your grade.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

The press section takes the web from about **20% to 40–50% solids**, and it removes water far more cheaply than the dryers. Felts carry the water away; **rewet** and **crushing** are the enemies. **Shoe presses** extend the nip time for higher dryness with better bulk.

<TryIt title="Calculate the Steam Savings">

A linerboard machine leaves the press at 44% solids. A new felt raises that to 46%. Using the 4%-per-point rule of thumb, roughly how much dryer steam is saved?

Answer: 2 points × ~4% ≈ **8% less steam**. If the dryers were the bottleneck, that could let the machine run several percent faster at the same steam — a big win from a felt change.

</TryIt>
