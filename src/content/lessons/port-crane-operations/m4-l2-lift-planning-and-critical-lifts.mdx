## What Makes a Lift "Critical"?

Definitions vary by company, but a lift is often designated **critical** when it involves:

- A load above a set percentage of the crane's chart capacity (commonly in the **75–90%** range, per company rules)
- **Two or more cranes** lifting one load (tandem lift)
- Lifting **personnel**
- Lifts over **occupied areas**, **operating equipment**, or **live process lines**
- **Unusual** or **high-value** loads, or loads with uncertain weight or CG
- Lifts near **power lines**

Critical lifts typically require a **written, engineered plan**, review and **sign-off** by qualified persons, and a documented **pre-lift meeting**.

---

## The Lift Plan

A written lift plan usually includes:

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Load** | Weight (verified), dimensions, CG, lifting points |
| **Crane** | Make, model, configuration, counterweight, outriggers or position |
| **Radii** | Pick, swing, and set radii; chart capacity at the **worst** radius |
| **Rigging** | Each component's WLL, weight, and calculated load (sling angles) |
| **Total load and percent of capacity** | Load + rigging ÷ chart capacity |
| **Ground / wharf** | Bearing capacity, mats, deck load limits on ships |
| **Environment** | Wind limits, visibility, tide and vessel movement, lighting |
| **Hazards** | Power lines, structures, other cranes, traffic |
| **Exclusion zone** | Barricades and spotters |
| **People** | Operator, signal person, riggers, lift director |
| **Communication** | Signals and radio channel |
| **Contingencies** | What to do if wind rises or the load shifts |

## Lifting On and Off Ships

Ship lifts have special challenges:

- **The ship moves.** Tide, wind, swell, and passing traffic make the vessel rise, fall, and surge. Landing a load on a moving deck takes coordination.
- **Ship stability.** Heavy lifts shift the vessel's weight and can cause it to **list**. The ship's officers must agree to the lift and manage ballast.
- **Deck strength.** Decks and hatch covers have **load limits**. Use dunnage to spread loads.
- **Ship's gear.** If the ship's own cranes are used, they must be certified and in good condition.

---

## The Exclusion Zone

- **Barricade** the area under and around the load path.
- **Never** stand or walk **under a suspended load**.
- Keep people out of **pinch points** between the load and fixed objects.
- Keep clear of the crane's **swing radius** (counterweight) on mobile and harbor cranes.

## The Test Lift

Lift the load a few inches off its supports and **hold**:

- Is the load **level** (hook over CG)?
- Are slings **seated** and hardware aligned?
- Do the **brakes** hold?
- Does the RCI read as expected?

If anything is off, **set it down** and fix it.

<Pitfall title="The Plan Didn't Include the Wind">

**Common mistake:** a large, flat piece of cargo (like a panel or a ship's hatch cover) is lifted on a day that's within the crane's general wind limit.

Large surface areas act like sails. The load spins, swings, or pulls the crane beyond its rating even in moderate wind. Lift plans for high-sail-area loads need **load-specific** wind limits and extra tag line control.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Plan every non-routine lift: **load, crane, radius, rigging, site, wind, zone, people, communication**. Designate **critical lifts** by percentage of capacity, tandem, personnel, or hazard, with **written plans and sign-off**. Ship lifts involve **vessel motion, stability, and deck limits**. Barricade, **never stand under a suspended load**, and always make a **test lift**.
