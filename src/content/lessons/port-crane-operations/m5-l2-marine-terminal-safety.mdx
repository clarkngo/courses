## The Rules

In the U.S., two OSHA standards cover most waterfront cargo work:

| Standard | Covers |
|---|---|
| **29 CFR 1917, Marine Terminals** | Work on the terminal: shore cranes, cargo handling equipment, terminal facilities, and operations on the land side |
| **29 CFR 1918, Longshoring** | Work **aboard vessels**: loading, unloading, and moving cargo on ships, including ship's gear |

The **U.S. Coast Guard** oversees port security and vessel safety, and terminal operators have their own detailed safety rules.

---

## The Big Hazards

### 1. Vehicles and Mobile Equipment

Yard tractors, straddle carriers, top handlers, trucks, and cranes are constantly moving. **Struck-by** incidents are a leading cause of serious waterfront injuries.

- Separate **pedestrians and vehicles** with marked walkways and controlled crossings.
- Wear **high-visibility** clothing, day and night.
- Make **eye contact** with drivers; assume they **can't** see you.
- Obey **speed limits** and traffic patterns; no phone use while driving.
- Use **spotters** in congested areas.

### 2. Suspended Loads

Containers, spreaders, and cargo move overhead all day. **Never** walk or stand under a load. Watch for **swinging** spreaders and **falling** twistlocks or debris.

### 3. Falls, Including Into the Water

- Falls from **ladders, gangways, hatch covers, lashing bridges**, and container tops
- **Falling into the water** between ship and wharf: cold Northwest water causes **cold shock** and hypothermia quickly
- Controls: guardrails, safe gangways with **nets**, fall protection, **life rings** and ladders along the wharf, and **PFDs** where required near the edge

### 4. Hazardous Cargo

Dangerous goods move under the **IMDG Code** (International Maritime Dangerous Goods) and U.S. hazardous materials rules.

- Learn the **placards** and **labels** on containers.
- Report **leaks, odors, or damage** immediately; don't investigate on your own.
- Know the terminal's **emergency response** procedures and segregation areas.

### 5. Fumigated and Poorly Ventilated Containers

Containers may be **fumigated** with toxic gases, or contain gases released by cargo. Opening a closed container can expose you to a **toxic atmosphere**. Look for **fumigation warning signs**, and follow procedures for **ventilating and testing** before entry.

### 6. Weather, Darkness, and Fatigue

- **Wind** stops crane work at set limits; rain and ice make decks slick.
- **Night operations** need good lighting.
- Long shifts and rotating schedules cause **fatigue**, a hidden contributor to incidents.

### 7. Maintenance Hazards

Crane and equipment maintenance involves **heights, high-voltage systems, hydraulics, and moving machinery**. Use **lockout/tagout** before servicing (see the Lockout/Tagout course), and follow fall protection rules on crane structures.

<Pitfall title="Shortcut Across the Stack Row">

**Common mistake:** a worker cuts between container rows to reach a vessel faster.

Yard equipment like RTGs and straddle carriers operate in those rows, often with limited visibility, and stacks can block the operator's view. Use **designated routes** and **transport**, even when it takes longer.

</Pitfall>

## Building a Safety Culture

- **Stop work authority**: anyone can stop unsafe work.
- **Pre-shift safety talks** cover the day's hazards (weather, special cargo, ship type).
- **Near-miss reporting** finds hazards before injuries do.
- **Training and certification** for every machine and task.

---

## Key Takeaway

**OSHA 1917** covers marine terminals and **1918** covers longshoring aboard ships. The biggest hazards are **vehicles, suspended loads, falls (including into cold water), hazardous and fumigated cargo, weather, fatigue, and maintenance energy**. Separate people from machines, never stand under loads, wear **high-vis**, report hazards, and use **stop work authority**.
