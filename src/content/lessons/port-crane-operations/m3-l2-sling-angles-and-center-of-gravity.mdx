## Angles Multiply Tension

When sling legs angle out from the hook, each leg carries **more** than its share of the load. The flatter the angle, the higher the tension.

### The Formula

**Tension per leg = (load ÷ number of legs) ÷ sin(angle from horizontal)**

Or use the **load angle factor** (1 ÷ sin of the angle):

| Angle from horizontal | Load angle factor |
|---|---|
| 90° (vertical) | 1.000 |
| 60° | 1.155 |
| 45° | 1.414 |
| 30° | **2.000** |

### Worked Example

A **10,000 lb** load on a two-leg bridle at **45°**:

(10,000 ÷ 2) × 1.414 = **7,070 lb per leg**

Each sling, shackle, and lifting point must be rated for at least that.

### Rules of Thumb

- Keep sling angles **at or above 45°** from horizontal when possible.
- Avoid angles **below 30°**. Tension climbs extremely fast, and many standards and companies prohibit them without engineering.
- Use a **spreader bar** or **lifting beam** to keep slings vertical on wide loads.

---

## Center of Gravity (CG)

The **center of gravity** is the balance point of the load. When lifted, a load will swing until its CG is **directly below the hook**.

- If the hook isn't over the CG, the load **tilts** and may **shift, slide, or swing** into people or structures.
- Determine CG from **drawings, markings, or shipping documents** (heavy cargo is often stenciled with a CG mark), or by calculation.
- **Test lift**: raise the load a few inches, check balance and rigging, then continue.

### Unequal Legs

When the CG is closer to one lifting point, the **shorter / steeper leg nearer the CG carries more** of the load. Rigging must be sized for the **highest-loaded** leg, not an average.

### Multi-Leg Slings

With three- or four-leg slings, legs rarely share load equally because of small length differences and rigid loads. A common practice is to **assume only two legs carry the load** unless an engineer determines otherwise.

---

## Protecting the Rigging

- **Edge protection** (corner pads, sleeves, wear pads) on sharp edges. Synthetics can be cut through instantly.
- Respect **D/d ratio**: bending wire rope around a small diameter (a thin pin or sharp corner) reduces its strength.
- Don't **drag** slings, and don't pull slings out from under a resting load.

## Controlling the Load

- **Tag lines** let workers guide and stop rotation **from a distance**, never by hand on the load.
- Never wrap a tag line around your hand or body.
- Keep **clear of pinch points** between the load and fixed objects.
- **Never stand under a suspended load.**

<Pitfall title="Short Slings on a Wide Load">

**Common mistake:** using short slings on a long, wide piece of cargo so the crane has more lift height.

The result is a very flat sling angle, sometimes under 30°, which can **double or triple** the tension in each leg and overload slings, shackles, and lifting lugs. Use **longer slings** or a **spreader bar**.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Sling tension = **(load ÷ legs) ÷ sin(angle)**. At **30°**, each leg carries **double** its share. Stay at **45° or more**, and use **spreader bars** for wide loads. Put the **hook over the CG**, make a **test lift**, size for the **most heavily loaded leg**, assume **two legs** carry multi-leg loads, protect **edges**, and control the load with **tag lines**.

<TryIt title="Calculate the Leg Tension">

A **24,000 lb** transformer is lifted with a two-leg bridle at **60°** from horizontal.

(24,000 ÷ 2) × 1.155 = **13,860 lb per leg**.

If the only available slings make a **30°** angle instead, what's the tension? (24,000 ÷ 2) × 2.000 = **24,000 lb per leg**. That's the **entire load** in each leg.
