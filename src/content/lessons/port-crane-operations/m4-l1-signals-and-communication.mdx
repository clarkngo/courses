## Why Signals Matter

Crane operators often **can't see** the load or the landing spot, especially when working deep in a ship's hold, behind a stack, or on the far side of a vessel. The **signal person** is the operator's eyes. Clear, standard communication prevents collisions, crushed hands, and dropped loads.

---

## The Signal Person

- Must be **qualified**: knows the standard signals, crane motions, and the hazards of the operation, as required by the applicable rules and site procedures.
- There should be **one designated signal person** giving directions at a time, so the operator isn't getting conflicting signals.
- Must be **clearly visible** to the operator (high-visibility gear or gloves) or in clear radio contact.
- Must have a **clear view** of the load and path, or relay through another qualified person.

### Anyone Can Stop

The operator follows only the designated signal person, **except for STOP**. **Anyone** who sees danger may signal **STOP** or **EMERGENCY STOP**, and the operator must obey immediately.

---

## Standard Hand Signals

These are based on the standard signals published in the **ASME B30** crane standards. Charts are usually **posted** on or near the crane. Learn them exactly.

| Command | Signal |
|---|---|
| **Hoist** | Forearm vertical, forefinger pointing **up**, move hand in small **horizontal circles** |
| **Lower** | Arm extended **down**, forefinger pointing down, move hand in small **horizontal circles** |
| **Stop** | Arm extended, palm **down**, move arm **back and forth horizontally** |
| **Emergency stop** | **Both** arms extended, palms down, move arms **back and forth horizontally** |
| **Dog everything** (pause all motions) | Clasp hands in front of the body |
| **Swing** (slewing cranes) | Arm extended, point finger in the direction to swing |
| **Trolley travel** | Palm up, fingers closed, thumb pointing in direction of travel, jerk hand horizontally |
| **Bridge / gantry travel** | Arm extended forward, hand open and slightly raised, make a pushing motion in the direction of travel |
| **Move slowly** | Use one hand for the motion signal and place the other hand motionless in front of it (e.g., above the hoist signal hand) |
| **Raise boom** | Arm extended, fingers closed, thumb pointing up |
| **Lower boom** | Arm extended, fingers closed, thumb pointing down |

Port terminals may have **additional site-specific signals** for spreader operations and ship work. Use the site's posted chart.

---

## Radio Communication

On ships and in terminals, many lifts use **radios** because of distance and noise.

- Use a **dedicated channel** for the lift when possible.
- **Identify** the crane and signal person at the start ("Crane 7, this is the hatch").
- Use **short, standard words**: "hoist," "lower," "stop," "trolley in," "trolley out."
- Many sites use **continuous talk** during the lift (e.g., "lower, lower, lower… slow… stop") so that if the radio goes silent, the operator **stops**.
- **Test radios** before the lift and carry spare batteries.
- If communication is lost, **STOP**.

<Pitfall title="Two People Signaling at Once">

**Common mistake:** the hatch tender signals "lower" while a lasher on deck waves the operator to "come this way."

The operator has to guess, and guessing with a 30-ton box overhead is how people get hurt. Establish **one signal person** before each operation, and everyone else uses only **STOP**.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

The **qualified signal person** is the operator's eyes. Use **one designated signaler** at a time, but **anyone can call STOP**. Learn the **ASME standard hand signals** exactly. On radio, use a **dedicated channel**, **standard words**, and **continuous talk**; if communication is lost, **stop**.

<TryIt title="Signal Drill">

With a partner, practice each signal from the table. One person signals, the other calls out the command. Then add radio practice: talk a "load" (a box on a rope) down onto a mark using continuous voice commands, and have the "operator" stop the moment you go silent.

</TryIt>
