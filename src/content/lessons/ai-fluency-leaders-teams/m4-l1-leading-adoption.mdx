## Start Small, Learn Fast

Team-wide AI rollouts that start with "everyone use AI for everything" usually stall or cause problems. A better pattern is a small, deliberate **pilot**:

Good first pilots are **frequent, well-understood, and medium-to-low stakes**: meeting summaries, first drafts of routine documents, internal research briefs. Avoid starting with decisions about people or anything in the critical review tier.

## Champions, Not Enforcers

Identify a few **AI champions**: people who are curious, careful, and respected by peers. Their job is to try things first, refine shared instructions, help colleagues, and bring problems back to you. They should be chosen for judgment, not just enthusiasm. A champion who never notices AI's mistakes will spread bad habits faster.

## Address Fears Honestly

People worry about AI, and many of their concerns are reasonable:

- **"Will AI replace my job?"** Be honest about what you know and don't know. Don't promise what you can't guarantee. Explain how roles may change, and what you're doing to help people build skills that stay valuable.
- **"Will I be judged for using it, or for not using it?"** Make expectations explicit, and avoid treating AI use as a loyalty test either way.
- **"What if I make a mistake with it?"** Point to the escalation process and blameless review (Lessons 2.2 and 3.1).
- **"I'm not technical."** AI fluency is about judgment, not technical skill. The people who know the work best are often the best at catching AI's mistakes.

Leaders who dismiss concerns lose trust. Leaders who acknowledge them, and involve people in shaping how AI is used, get far better adoption.

## Train for Judgment, Not Features

Feature tours go out of date quickly. Effective training uses **your team's real work** and focuses on the four competencies:

- When should we use AI on this workflow, and when not? (Delegation)
- How do we use our shared instructions? (Description)
- What do we check, and how? (Discernment)
- What are our rules for tools, data, and disclosure? (Diligence)

[AI Fluency: Foundations](/courses/ai-fluency-foundations/) is a good shared starting point for the whole team.

## Measure What Matters

The easiest thing to measure is usage: logins, prompts, licenses. It's also the least meaningful. Measure outcomes instead:

| Weak measure | Better measure |
|---|---|
| Number of prompts sent | Time saved on a specific workflow, compared with the baseline |
| Percentage of staff using AI | Quality: error rates, rework, customer satisfaction |
| Licenses purchased | Issues caught in review; incidents and near-misses |
| "AI-generated" document count | Employee confidence and skill, from surveys |

**Watch quality as closely as speed.** If output volume goes up and error rates go up too, the pilot isn't working yet.

<Pitfall title="Usage Targets">

**Common mistake:** setting a target like "80% of staff using AI daily by Q3."

People will hit the target by using AI for tasks where it doesn't help, or where it harms quality. Set goals around outcomes in specific workflows, and let usage follow value.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Design a Pilot">

1. Choose one workflow from your mapping in Lesson 1.2 that fits the "good first pilot" description.
2. Define the baseline: how long does it take now, and how do you measure quality?
3. Choose two or three champions and a pilot length.
4. List the shared instruction, review tier, and policy points that apply.
5. Write three success measures (at least one about quality) and one condition that would make you stop.
6. Write a short, honest message to your team announcing the pilot, including how it might affect their work and how they can raise concerns.

</TryIt>
