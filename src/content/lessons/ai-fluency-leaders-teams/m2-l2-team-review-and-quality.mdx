## Review Has to Scale

If AI makes your team produce more, review has to keep up. Reviewing everything with maximum rigor is impossible, and reviewing nothing is reckless. The answer is **risk-tiered review**: decide in advance how much checking each kind of work needs.

| Tier | Typical work | Review |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Internal notes, brainstorms, first drafts for your own use | Self-check by the author |
| **Medium** | Internal reports, routine customer replies from approved templates | Author checks against the definition of done; spot-checks by a lead |
| **High** | Client deliverables, published content, anything with figures, legal or financial claims | Second-person review of facts, figures, and claims against sources |
| **Critical** | Decisions about people, safety, money, legal commitments, regulated information | Expert human review and sign-off; AI informs at most, never decides |

Put the tiers in writing and attach them to your workflow maps (Lesson 1.2), so every checkpoint knows its tier.

For the techniques of checking itself, such as verifying facts, citations, numbers, reasoning, and bias, see [Discernment: Judging AI Output](/courses/ai-fluency-discernment/). This lesson focuses on the leader's job: **making sure review actually happens, at the right level.**

## Automation Bias: The Reviewer's Blind Spot

**Automation bias** is the tendency to trust output from an automated system more than we should, and to stop looking critically once a system "usually gets it right." It gets worse when:

- Output looks polished and confident
- Reviewers are busy and the volume is high
- The AI has been right many times in a row
- Nobody has ever been asked to find problems, only to approve

Leaders can counter it:

- **Ask reviewers to find something.** "What's the weakest claim in this?" works better than "Does this look okay?"
- **Rotate reviewers** so fresh eyes see the work.
- **Seed occasional known errors** in training exercises, so people stay alert and learn what slips through.
- **Protect review time** in schedules. Review that has to fit in the gaps gets skipped.

<Pitfall title="Rubber-Stamp Review">

**Common mistake:** a review step that exists on paper, where the reviewer approves 50 AI-assisted documents a day in a few seconds each.

If you measure reviewers only on throughput, you get rubber stamps. Measure what review catches, and staff it so careful checking is possible.

</Pitfall>

## When Something Goes Wrong: Blameless Review

AI-assisted errors will happen: a wrong figure in a client report, a hallucinated policy in a customer reply. How you respond determines whether your team learns or hides problems. A **blameless review** asks what about the *system* allowed the error, not who to blame:

1. **What happened?** The facts, without judgment.
2. **Where did it originate?** The AI output, the instructions, the source data?
3. **Why wasn't it caught?** Wrong review tier? Rushed review? Unclear standard?
4. **What will we change?** The instruction, the tier, the checklist, the workflow?
5. **Who needs to know?** Share the lesson with the team.

People still own their work. But if the response to every AI mistake is blame, people stop reporting near-misses, and the team loses its best source of improvement.

<TryIt title="Set Review Tiers for Your Team">

1. List the six to ten types of AI-assisted work your team produces.
2. Assign each a tier: Low, Medium, High, or Critical. Note why.
3. For each Medium-or-higher type, name who reviews, against which standard (from Lesson 2.1), and how much time it should take.
4. Pick one recent AI mistake or near-miss (yours or the team's) and walk through the five blameless review questions.
5. Identify one change to instructions, tiers, or workflow that would have caught it.

</TryIt>
