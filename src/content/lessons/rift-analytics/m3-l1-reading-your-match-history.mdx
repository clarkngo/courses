## A Season Is a Dataset

Your match history is a **dataset**: each game is an **observation**, and each stat (gold per minute, KDA, damage, vision score, game length) is a **variable**. Descriptive statistics compresses dozens of games into a handful of numbers that answer two questions:

1. **Center:** what's a typical game for you?
2. **Spread:** how much do your games vary?

### Types of Variables

| Type | Examples | Summaries that make sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Gold per minute, damage dealt, game length | Mean, median, SD, histogram |
| **Discrete count** | Kills, deaths, towers destroyed | Mean, median, mode, bar chart |
| **Categorical** | Role, champion, win/loss | Counts, proportions, mode |

You can average GPM. You can't average "Mid lane" and "Jungle." Picking the right summary starts with knowing the variable type.

## Measures of Center

Here are 20 games of **gold per minute (GPM)** for one mid laner:

```
412 455 388 501 437 466 420 398 445 520
431 455 470 405 455 489 362 441 478 430
```

- **Mean** (arithmetic average): add them up and divide by n. `8,858 / 20 = 442.9`
- **Median**: sort the values and take the middle. With 20 values, average the 10th and 11th: `(441 + 445) / 2 = 443`
- **Mode**: the most frequent value. `455` appears three times.

Here the mean and median agree closely, which suggests the data is roughly **symmetric**.

### When the Mean Lies

Now add one game where you stomped lane and finished at **780 GPM**:

- New mean: `9,638 / 21 ≈ 459.0` (up 16 points)
- New median: `445` (up 2 points)

One unusual game moved the mean eight times as much as the median. The mean uses every value's *size*; the median only uses their *order*. That makes the median **robust** to outliers. When a distribution is **skewed** (a long tail on one side), report the median, or report both.

## Measures of Spread

Two players can have the same average and very different games. Consider KDA over 15 games:

| | Mean KDA | Median KDA | Standard deviation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Player A** (steady) | 3.07 | 3.1 | 0.29 |
| **Player B** (coin-flip) | 3.07 | 2.4 | 2.27 |

Same mean, but Player B swings between 0.5 and 7.3. **Spread** is the statistic that tells them apart.

### Variance and Standard Deviation

Variance measures the average squared distance from the mean:

```
s² = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)
```

Standard deviation is its square root, `s = √s²`, which puts it back in the original units (GPM, KDA). For the 20-game GPM sample, `s ≈ 39.2` GPM.

Why divide by `n − 1` instead of `n`? Because we measured distances from the *sample* mean, which sits closer to the data than the true long-run mean does. Dividing by `n − 1` (**Bessel's correction**) corrects that bias when you treat your games as a sample of how you play in general.

### Rules of Thumb for SD

For roughly bell-shaped data, about **68%** of values fall within 1 SD of the mean and about **95%** within 2 SD. For our GPM data, that predicts most games between `443 ± 39`, about 404 to 482. Counting directly, 14 of 20 games (70%) land in that range.

### Interquartile Range (IQR)

The **first quartile (Q1)** is the value 25% of the way through the sorted data; **Q3** is 75% of the way. `IQR = Q3 − Q1` covers the middle half of your games. For the GPM data, Q1 = 418 and Q3 = 467, so IQR = 49. Like the median, the IQR ignores extreme values, which makes it the robust partner to the SD.

A common outlier rule flags values below `Q1 − 1.5 · IQR` or above `Q3 + 1.5 · IQR`. Here that range is roughly 345 to 541, so the 780 GPM stomp is flagged as an outlier.

### Coefficient of Variation

To compare spreads on different scales, divide SD by the mean: `CV = s / x̄`. Player A's KDA has `CV ≈ 9%`; Player B's is `≈ 74%`. CV lets you ask "is my GPM more or less consistent than my KDA?" even though the units differ.

### z-Scores

How unusual was a single game? Convert it to a **z-score**:

```
z = (x − x̄) / s
```

The 520 GPM game: `(520 − 442.9) / 39.2 ≈ 1.97`, almost 2 standard deviations above your average. z-scores will come back in Module 4 as the basis of hypothesis testing.

## Reading a Histogram

A histogram groups values into **bins** and shows how many fall in each. Look for:

- **Shape:** symmetric, skewed right (long tail of high values), skewed left, or bimodal (two peaks)
- **Center:** where the bulk of the data sits
- **Spread:** how wide the bulk is
- **Outliers:** isolated bars far from the rest

A **bimodal** GPM histogram is a clue that two different things are going on, for example games on a farming champion vs. games on a roaming one. When you see two peaks, split the data before summarizing it.

<Pitfall title="Averaging Ratios Like KDA">

**Common mistake:** averaging per-game KDA values. KDA is `(Kills + Assists) / Deaths`, and a 10/0/8 game is usually reported as 18 (dividing by 1 instead of 0), which can single-handedly drag the mean upward. A more stable season KDA divides **total** kills plus assists by **total** deaths. The same issue shows up whenever you average ratios, like click-through rates or batting averages: decide whether you want the average of ratios or the ratio of totals, and say which one you used.

</Pitfall>
