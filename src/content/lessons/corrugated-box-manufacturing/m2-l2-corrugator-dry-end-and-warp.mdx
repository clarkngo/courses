### Glue Machine and Double Backer

At the **glue machine**, starch is applied to the exposed flute tips of the single-face web. Then the **inside liner** (the "double-back" liner) meets the web at the **double backer**.

The board slides over a long row of steam-heated **hot plates** while a belt and ballast rolls or pressure shoes hold it down — gently, because too much pressure would crush the flutes. The heat gels the starch and drives out moisture.

For **double-wall** board, two single-face webs (from two single facers) are glued together and to the inside liner in the same step.

### Slitter-Scorer

The **slitter-scorer** does two jobs at once, across the width of the web:

- **Slitting:** rotating knives cut the web into one or more **outs** — separate sheets side by side.
- **Scoring:** rounded scoring heads crease the board along the lines where the **flaps** will fold.

On the corrugator, the flutes run **across** the web (cross direction), while slitter-scorer creases run **along** it (machine direction) — so corrugator scores are always **perpendicular to the flutes**. In a regular slotted box, these become the **flap scores**. The other folds, the **panel scores**, are added later in converting.

### Cut-off Knife and Stacker

A **rotary cut-off knife** cuts the board to sheet length. Modern lines can change sheet length on the fly. The **stacker** builds stacks, counts sheets, and sends **loads** onward to converting by conveyor or forklift.

## Orders, Trim, and Scheduling

A corrugator is fed by a **schedule** that groups orders to maximize output and minimize waste.

- **Trim:** paper rolls come in fixed widths. If an order's sheets are 1,200 mm wide and the paper is 2,500 mm wide, running **two outs** uses 2,400 mm and trims off 100 mm. Schedulers combine orders side by side to use the paper width efficiently — trim waste of a few percent is a common goal.
- **Grade grouping:** orders that use the same papers and flute run together to avoid splices.
- **Order changes:** modern corrugators change orders at full speed, so short runs are practical.

## Warp

**Warp** — board that isn't flat — is the most common corrugator quality problem. Warped board jams in converting machines, misprints, and makes boxes that won't erect properly.

The root cause is almost always **moisture imbalance**: when one liner holds more moisture than the other, it later shrinks more as it dries, pulling the board into a curve toward that side.

**Warp fixes** usually involve rebalancing heat and moisture between the two liners: changing **preheater wrap**, adjusting **steam showers**, reducing **glue** application, or changing **double-backer heat and speed**. Moisture also arrives with the paper rolls — rolls stored in a humid warehouse corner can cause warp on the next order.

<Pitfall title="Fixing Warp at the Stacker">

**Common mistake:** waiting until warped board is stacked, then trying to flatten it with weight or time.

By then, the moisture difference is locked in. Warp has to be corrected **upstream** in real time — at the preheaters, showers, and glue gap — while watching the board as it leaves the double backer. The best operators read the board's shape continuously and make small adjustments before warp shows up in converting.

</Pitfall>

## Other Board Defects

| Defect | Looks like | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Delamination / poor bond** | Liner peels cleanly from flutes | Not enough heat or time; low starch gel quality; glue starvation |
| **Washboarding** | Liner sinks between flutes, ridged surface | Too much glue/water; lightweight liner; excess heat |
| **High-low flutes** | Uneven flute heights | Worn corrugating rolls; poor medium moisture/tension |
| **Crushed board** | Low caliper, flattened flutes | Excess pressure in double backer or at feed rolls |
| **Blisters** | Unbonded bubbles | Uneven glue application; trapped steam |

---

## Key Takeaway

The dry end glues on the inside liner at the **double backer**, cures it on hot plates, then **slits, scores, cuts, and stacks** the board. Schedulers combine orders to minimize **trim**. **Warp** is a moisture-balance problem that must be fixed upstream, in real time.

<TryIt title="Create Warp on Purpose">

Cut two 6-inch squares of corrugated board. Lightly mist the **top** liner of one square with water and leave the other dry. Let them sit flat for an hour or two. As the wet side dries and shrinks, the misted square curls. Notice which way — that's the same physics that warps board on the corrugator.

</TryIt>
