## Stating Box Dimensions

Box dimensions are always given as **Length × Width × Depth**, and always as **inside dimensions** — the space available for the product.

- **Length (L):** the **longer** side of the opening
- **Width (W):** the **shorter** side of the opening
- **Depth (D):** the distance between the openings (sometimes called height)

So a box described as **18 × 12 × 10** has an 18 × 12 opening and is 10 deep. A box that is 12 × 18 × 10 is **not** a valid description — the first number is always the longer side of the opening.

<Pitfall title="Measuring the Outside and Calling It the Size">

**Common mistake:** measuring a sample box on the outside and ordering "the same size."

Outside dimensions include the board thickness on both sides — up to about ¼ inch (6 mm) larger per dimension for C flute, and more for double wall. A box made to your outside measurements as inside dimensions will be too big; the product rattles, and you pay for extra board and void fill. Always measure and specify **inside**.

</Pitfall>

## Laying Out an RSC Blank

A box starts life as a flat **blank**. For an RSC, the blank is a long strip with four **panels** side by side plus a **joint tab**, and flaps above and below.

For an RSC with inside dimensions L × W × D:

- **Blank length** ≈ 2L + 2W + joint tab (typically ~1¼–1½ in / 30–40 mm)
- **Blank width** ≈ D + W (flap depth W/2 on top + depth D + flap depth W/2 on bottom)

### Worked Example

Inside dimensions **18 × 12 × 10 in**, joint tab 1.5 in:

- Blank length ≈ 2(18) + 2(12) + 1.5 = **61.5 in**
- Blank width ≈ 10 + 12 = **22 in**
- Board area ≈ 61.5 × 22 = 1,353 in² ≈ **9.4 ft²** per box

That area, multiplied by the order quantity and board price per thousand square feet (**MSF**), drives most of the box cost. Designers usually reduce cost by shrinking the box, not by cutting board grade.

### Caliper Allowances

Real blanks are a bit larger than these formulas. Because the board has thickness, the **score-to-score** panel dimensions are increased by an **allowance** so the *inside* dimensions come out right after folding. Allowances depend on the flute (for example, a fraction of an inch more per panel for C flute than for E flute). Every box plant has its own allowance tables matched to its equipment.

## Flute Direction and the Manufacturer's Joint

In an RSC, **flutes run vertically** — parallel to the depth — so each wall acts like a row of columns. That's what gives a box its stacking strength.

The **manufacturer's joint** closes the tube. It can be:

- **Glued** — the most common; fast and strong
- **Taped** — for some heavy-duty or specialty applications
- **Stitched** — metal staples, often for heavy double- and triple-wall boxes

## Key Takeaway

Always specify **inside dimensions as L × W × D**, with L the longer side of the opening. An RSC blank is about **2L + 2W + joint** long and **D + W** high, with caliper allowances added. Flutes run vertically for stacking strength. New boxes move from **brief → CAD → sample → test → spec**.

<TryIt title="Lay Out Your Own Blank">

Pick a product at home — a stack of books works well — and measure it. Add about ¼ inch to each dimension for clearance. Calculate the RSC blank size and the square footage per box. Then try a different orientation of the product (swap which dimension is the depth). Does the square footage change? Usually it does — and that's a free cost saving designers look for.

</TryIt>
