## The Machine That Makes the Board

A **corrugator** is a continuous line — often 100 meters or more long — that turns rolls of linerboard and medium into flat sheets of corrugated board. A modern corrugator can run at **300+ meters per minute** and make board for hundreds of different orders in a single shift.

The line has two halves:

- **Wet end:** unwinds paper, conditions it, forms the flutes, and glues the first liner on.
- **Dry end:** glues the second liner, cures the bond, cools the board, and cuts it into sheets.

This lesson covers the wet end. The next lesson covers the dry end.

## 1. Roll Stands and Splicers

Each paper — medium, single-face liner, and double-backer liner — feeds from a **roll stand** holding two rolls. When the running roll nears its end, an **automatic splicer** joins the new roll to the running web at full speed, so the corrugator never stops for a roll change.

Order changes often also mean paper changes (a different liner weight or color), which the splicer handles as well.

## 2. Preheaters

Paper is wrapped around large steam-heated **preheater** drums. Preheating:
- Brings the paper to the right **temperature** for the starch to gel quickly
- Drives off and **balances moisture**

The operator controls heat with the **wrap angle** — how much of the drum's surface the paper contacts. More wrap = more heat. This is a key lever for preventing **warp**, which you'll study next lesson.

The medium often also passes a **steam shower** to soften it so it forms flutes without cracking.

---

## 3. The Single Facer

The **single facer** is the heart of the corrugator.

1. The medium passes between two hot, fluted **corrugating rolls** that mesh like gears, forming it into flutes.
2. A **glue roll** applies a thin line of starch to the flute tips on one side.
3. A **pressure roll** (or pressure belt) presses the liner against the glued flute tips while they're still held in the corrugating roll.
4. Heat gels the starch, creating the **single-face web** — one liner + one fluted medium.

Corrugating rolls are made in a specific flute profile. Changing from C to B flute means changing the roll set — many corrugators have **two single facers** (for example, one B and one C) so they can make double-wall board or switch flutes quickly.

## 4. The Bridge

The single-face web climbs up to the **bridge**, an overhead conveyor where it lies in loose, overlapping folds. The bridge acts as a **buffer**, decoupling the single facer's speed from the dry end, and lets the starch bond set a bit before the web moves on.

---

## Starch: The Glue

Corrugating adhesive is a **starch** made from corn, wheat, or tapioca — cheap, renewable, and safe. The classic recipe (the **Stein-Hall** system) has two parts:

- A **carrier** — a small amount of cooked starch that makes the mix thick enough to stay on the glue roll
- **Raw (uncooked) starch** suspended in the carrier
- **Caustic soda** to lower the gel temperature
- **Borax** to boost tack and viscosity

When the glue line hits a hot flute tip, the raw starch reaches its **gel point** (typically around 140–150 °F / 60–65 °C), swells, and turns into a strong, clear bond almost instantly.

<Pitfall title="Fixing Bond Problems With More Glue">

**Common mistake:** when bonding is weak, opening up the glue gap to apply more starch.

Excess starch adds water to the board, which needs more heat to cure and causes **warp** and **washboarding** (the liner sinking between flutes). Weak bonds are often a **heat or speed** problem — the starch never reached its gel point — or a **starch quality** problem like wrong viscosity or gel temperature. Check those first. Good corrugators run the *minimum* glue that makes a full-fiber-tear bond.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

The wet end unwinds and splices paper, **preheats** it to control temperature and moisture, and the **single facer** forms flutes and glues the first liner on. The **bridge** buffers the web. Starch adhesive bonds when heat brings it to its **gel point** — and too much starch causes more problems than it solves.

<TryIt title="Check the Bond">

Take a piece of corrugated board and peel the liner off the flutes slowly. A good bond tears **fibers** from the liner and medium — you'll see fuzzy brown paper stuck to the flute tips. A poor bond peels cleanly, leaving shiny starch lines. This **fiber-tear** check is exactly what corrugator operators do on the floor.

</TryIt>
