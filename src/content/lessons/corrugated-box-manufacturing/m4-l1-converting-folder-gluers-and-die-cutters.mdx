## What "Converting" Means

The corrugator makes flat **sheets**. **Converting** turns sheets into finished boxes: printing, slotting, scoring panels, die-cutting, folding, gluing, and bundling.

Box plants come in two types:
- **Corrugator plants:** run a corrugator to make their own board, then convert it.
- **Sheet plants:** buy sheets from corrugator plants and focus on converting — often shorter runs and specialty work.

---

## The Flexo Folder-Gluer (FFG)

For RSCs and other slotted boxes, the workhorse is the **flexo folder-gluer**. A sheet goes in one end and a glued, folded, counted box comes out the other — at speeds of hundreds of boxes per minute.

Notice the division of labor:
- The **corrugator** made the **flap scores** (parallel to the length of the sheet).
- The **FFG** makes the **panel scores** and cuts the **slots** between flaps.

### Crush: The Hidden Cost of Converting

Every time the sheet passes through **feed rolls**, print cylinders, and pull rolls, it can be squeezed. Too much **gap closure** crushes the flutes and lowers caliper — and **lost caliper means lost box compression strength**.

Modern FFGs use **vacuum transfer** (holding the sheet against a belt or anvil by suction) instead of pinch rolls to reduce crush. Operators set gaps to the actual board caliper for every order.

<Pitfall title="Closing the Gaps to Fix Registration">

**Common mistake:** tightening feed and pull roll gaps to stop print or slot registration from wandering.

It often works — but it crushes the board. Research and plant experience show converting crush can cost a meaningful share of a box's compression strength. Fix registration with proper vacuum, clean and correctly set feed equipment, and flat board (no warp) instead of squeezing harder.

</Pitfall>

## Die Cutters

For boxes that aren't simple rectangles — mailers, trays, displays, handholes, interlocking features — the shape is cut by a **die**: steel cutting and creasing rules set into a base.

| | Rotary die cutter (RDC) | Flatbed die cutter |
|---|---|---|
| **How it cuts** | Curved die on a rotating cylinder against a soft anvil | Flat die pressed down onto the sheet |
| **Speed** | Fast | Slower |
| **Precision** | Good; can stretch slightly | Very precise; intricate shapes |
| **Tooling cost** | Lower-to-moderate | Higher |
| **Printing** | Often has inline flexo units | Usually prints elsewhere (or uses litho-laminated sheets) |
| **Best for** | High-volume trays, mailers, simple die-cuts | Complex displays, tight tolerances, high-graphics retail |

After die cutting, a **stripping** step removes the waste pieces, and the pieces may go to a **specialty folder-gluer** for more complex glued styles (crash-lock bottoms, for example).

---

## Other Converting Steps

- **Partition assemblers** make the slotted dividers inside wine and bottle shippers.
- **Stitchers** close heavy boxes with metal staples.
- **Laminators** combine sheets or glue litho-printed paper to board (next lesson).
- **Bundling and palletizing:** finished boxes are strapped in bundles and loaded onto pallets for delivery — usually flat ("knocked down") so they ship efficiently.

---

## Key Takeaway

**Converting** turns sheets into boxes. The **flexo folder-gluer** prints, slots, scores panels, glues, and counts RSCs at high speed. **Rotary** die cutters are fast; **flatbed** die cutters are precise. Protect board **caliper** throughout — crushed flutes mean weaker boxes.

<TryIt title="Find the Converting Marks">

Take a shipping box apart at its manufacturer's joint and flatten it. Look for: the slots between flaps (made on the FFG), the scores between panels (FFG) and between panels and flaps (corrugator), and the glue lines on the joint. Run a fingertip across the board near the score lines — any flattened bands are signs of crush from feed rolls.

</TryIt>
