## The Linear Workhorse

A **cylinder** turns air pressure into straight-line push or pull. Most pneumatic motion in a plant comes from cylinders, including pushers, stops, clamps, lifts, and diverters.

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| **Barrel** | Smooth tube the piston slides in |
| **Piston + seals** | Separates the two chambers; the pressure difference makes force |
| **Wear band** | Keeps the piston centered so metal doesn't touch metal |
| **Rod** | Carries force out of the cylinder |
| **Rod seal** | Keeps air in where the rod exits |
| **Wiper** | Scrapes dirt off the rod as it retracts |
| **Cushions** | Slow the piston near the end of stroke |

## Single-Acting vs. Double-Acting

- **Single-acting:** air drives one direction; a **spring** (or gravity) returns it. Uses less air and fails to a known position, but the spring steals some force and limits stroke.
- **Double-acting:** air drives **both** directions through two ports. This is the most common type in industry.

---

## Calculating Force

**Force = Pressure × Area**

**Extend** uses the full piston area:

- Area = π × (bore ÷ 2)²

**Retract** uses the piston area **minus the rod area**, because the rod takes up space on that side.

### Worked Example

A cylinder has a **2-inch bore**, a **5/8-inch rod**, and runs at **80 psi**.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Piston area | π × 1² | 3.14 in² |
| Extend force | 80 × 3.14 | **≈ 251 lbf** |
| Rod area | π × 0.3125² | 0.31 in² |
| Retract area | 3.14 − 0.31 | 2.83 in² |
| Retract force | 80 × 2.83 | **≈ 226 lbf** |

These are **theoretical** forces. Seal friction and the need to accelerate the load mean you should size with margin. A common rule is to choose a cylinder with theoretical force of about **1.5 to 2 times** the actual load for dynamic moves.

## Speed and Cushioning

Cylinder speed depends on **how fast air can get in and out**: valve size, tubing size and length, fittings, and flow controls. You'll set speed with flow controls in Module 4.

Fast cylinders slam into their end caps. **Cushions** fix that: near the end of stroke, a spear on the piston plugs the main exhaust path, trapping air that can only escape through a small adjustable **cushion needle**. The trapped air acts as a brake.

- Bang at end of stroke → cushion needle open too far (or load too heavy for built-in cushions)
- Piston "bounces" or creeps to the end → cushion closed too far

Heavy, fast loads may need **shock absorbers** in addition to cushions.

## Mounting and Alignment

Cylinders are designed to push and pull **along their axis**. Common mountings include front and rear flange, foot, clevis (pivot), and trunnion.

<Pitfall title="Side Loading the Rod">

**Common mistake:** using the cylinder rod as a guide, so a sliding load pushes sideways on the rod.

Side loads wear the rod bushing and seal into an oval, scratch the rod, and cause leaks and early failure. If the load isn't perfectly in line, use a **guided cylinder**, external linear guides, or a **floating joint / self-aligning rod coupler** to absorb misalignment.

</Pitfall>

### Reading Cylinder Wear

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Air leaking along the rod | Worn rod seal, scored rod, side load |
| Cylinder drifts or loses force | Leaking piston seal (air bypasses to the other side) |
| Jerky "stick-slip" motion at low speed | Dry seals, too-low pressure, or friction from misalignment |
| Rod scratched or pitted | Damaged wiper, weld spatter, harsh environment; use a rod boot |

---

## Key Takeaway

**Force = Pressure × Area.** Retract force is lower because the rod takes up area. Size with margin. **Cushions** and **shock absorbers** stop slamming. Keep loads **in line** with the rod and use guides or floating joints when they aren't.

<TryIt title="Size a Cylinder">

A pusher must move a 120 lb carton across a roller conveyor. You decide the cylinder needs about 2× the load for reliable dynamic motion, so **240 lbf**, and the machine is regulated to **70 psi**.

What's the minimum bore area? 240 ÷ 70 ≈ **3.43 in²**.

Which standard bore fits? A 2-inch bore gives 3.14 in² (too small). A **2.5-inch** bore gives π × 1.25² ≈ **4.91 in²**, which works with margin.

</TryIt>
