## "It's Just Air"

Compressed air feels harmless because it's invisible and clean. It isn't harmless. At 90–100 psi, compressed air can:

- **Penetrate skin** and inject air or contaminants into the body, especially through a cut
- **Blow particles** into eyes at high speed
- **Whip a hose** violently if a fitting lets go
- **Damage hearing** with high-pitched blow-off noise
- **Move a machine** when you least expect it, because of energy stored in cylinders, receivers, and lines

Horseplay with air hoses has caused serious internal injuries and deaths. **Never point compressed air at anyone, including yourself.**

---

## Cleaning with Air

In the U.S., OSHA **29 CFR 1910.242(b)** says compressed air used for cleaning must be reduced to **less than 30 psi** (when the nozzle is dead-ended), and used only with **effective chip guarding** and **personal protective equipment**.

In practice that means:

- Use **OSHA-compliant safety nozzles** that limit dead-end pressure and have side relief ports
- Wear **eye protection**; a face shield too when chips can fly
- **Never** clean clothing or skin with compressed air
- Prefer **vacuums or brushes** for cleaning when possible

---

## Hose and Fitting Safety

- Use hose and fittings **rated** for the system pressure.
- Secure hose connections larger than about 1/2 inch with **whip checks** (safety cables) at couplings, or use **excess flow valves** (also called safety fuses) that shut off flow if a hose fails.
- Inspect hoses for cracks, bulges, and abrasion.
- **Never kink a hose to stop airflow.** Shut the valve.
- Always **bleed the line** before disconnecting.

## Stored Energy in Pneumatic Systems

Turning off a valve upstream doesn't make a pneumatic machine safe. Energy hides in:

| Where | Hazard |
|---|---|
| **Receivers and accumulators** | Can still supply motion after the main valve is closed |
| **Lines and manifolds downstream of a closed valve** | Can shift a cylinder if a valve is moved or overridden |
| **Cylinders with check valves or closed-center valves** | Hold trapped pressure that releases when a fitting is loosened |
| **Raised loads** | Gravity can drop a load held only by air |
| **Springs** | Spring-return cylinders and grippers store mechanical energy |

### The Lockout Dump Valve

The right isolation device on a pneumatic machine is a **lockable 3/2 shutoff valve** at the FRL. When it's closed and locked, it **blocks supply and exhausts all downstream air** at the same time. A plain ball valve that only blocks supply leaves the machine pressurized.

<Pitfall title="Trusting the Gauge Alone">

**Common mistake:** the FRL gauge reads zero, so the tech loosens a fitting at a cylinder.

A **pilot-operated check valve** at that cylinder was holding pressure. The gauge only sees the line up to the valve, not air trapped beyond it. The fitting blows off, and the load drops. Know where every check valve, accumulator, and closed-center valve is on the drawing, and bleed each one before you open the system.

</Pitfall>

## Restoring Air Safely

Turning air back on is a hazard too:

- **Soft-start valves** fill the system slowly, so cylinders move gently into position instead of slamming.
- **Double-solenoid** valves may move cylinders to their last position the moment air returns (Lesson 4.1).
- Before restoring air: everyone clear, tools removed, guards replaced, and a warning given.

---

## Noise

Valve exhausts and blow-offs can easily exceed safe noise levels. Use **exhaust mufflers** (and keep them clean, since clogged mufflers slow the machine), engineered nozzles, and hearing protection where required.

---

## Key Takeaway

Compressed air can inject, blind, whip, deafen, and move machines unexpectedly. Keep cleaning air **under 30 psi dead-ended** with chip guarding and PPE. Isolate with a **lockable 3/2 dump valve**, then **bleed trapped air**, **block loads**, and **verify**. Restore air slowly and deliberately.
