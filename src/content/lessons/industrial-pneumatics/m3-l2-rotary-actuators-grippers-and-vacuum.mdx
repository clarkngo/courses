## Rotary Actuators

When you need to **turn** something a fixed angle, such as flipping a part, opening a gate, or turning a valve, use a rotary actuator.

| Type | How it works | Typical rotation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rack and pinion** | Pistons drive a toothed rack that turns a pinion gear | 90°, 180°, adjustable | Very common on process valves and indexing |
| **Vane** | Air pushes a vane inside a round chamber | Up to about 270° | Compact; lower torque; sensitive to dirty air |
| **Helical / scotch yoke** | Linear piston motion converted to rotation | 90° | Scotch yoke gives high torque at the ends of travel, ideal for valves |

Rotary actuators are rated by **torque** (in·lb or N·m) at a given pressure. Like cylinders, they need cushioning or external stops when rotating heavy loads, because rotating inertia stores a lot of energy.

## Grippers

**Grippers** are the "fingers" of pick-and-place machines and robots.

- **Parallel grippers** — jaws move straight in and out; good for parts with parallel faces
- **Angular grippers** — jaws pivot open; cheaper, need more clearance
- **Three-jaw grippers** — center round parts

Key specs are **grip force**, **stroke**, and the **jaw length** the force is rated at. Force drops as fingers get longer, so always check the maker's chart.

### Fail-Safe Gripping

What happens if air is lost while a part is held? Options include:

- **Spring-assisted** grippers that hold on air loss
- **Check valves** that trap air in the gripper
- **Mechanical self-locking** jaw designs

Choose deliberately. A dropped part over a person or a fixture is a safety problem, not just a quality problem.

## Air Motors

Air motors spin continuously, like an electric motor, using vanes or pistons.

- **Speed varies with load**: free speed with no load drops as torque increases
- They can **stall without damage** and restart, which electric motors can't do safely
- Safe in **explosive atmospheres** and washdown areas because there's no spark
- **Very air-hungry**, so they cost a lot to run

You see them in mixers in hazardous areas, hoists, and handheld tools.

## Vacuum: Picking with Suction

Vacuum handling lifts parts like cartons, sheets, and glass using **suction cups**.

### Making Vacuum with a Venturi

Most plant vacuum comes from a **venturi** (vacuum ejector): compressed air rushes through a nozzle, and the fast-moving jet drags air out of a side port, creating vacuum. No moving parts, instant on and off, but it consumes compressed air the entire time it runs. Many ejectors include an **air-saving** function that shuts off supply once vacuum is reached and only resumes if vacuum decays.

For large, continuous vacuum needs, an electric **vacuum pump** is usually cheaper to run.

### Vacuum Units

Vacuum is often measured in **inches of mercury (inHg)** below atmosphere. One inHg is about **0.49 psi**. A good venturi might pull around 20–25 inHg (roughly 10–12 psi below atmosphere). You can never exceed atmospheric pressure, about 14.7 psi at sea level, as your maximum "push."

### Sizing a Suction Cup

**Holding force = vacuum (psi) × cup area (in²)**, then divide by a **safety factor**.

Common rules of thumb for safety factor:

- **2** for a horizontal lift (cup on top of the part, lifting straight up)
- **4** for a vertical lift (cup on the side of the part, relying on friction)

Use more when there's fast motion or a porous or oily surface.

<Pitfall title="Porous Cartons and Leaky Cups">

**Common mistake:** sizing the cup on clean, smooth test parts, then running recycled corrugated cartons.

Porous board and rough surfaces leak air through the material, so vacuum never builds fully. Measure the vacuum actually achieved on production parts, use **bellows** or foam cups for uneven surfaces, and add a **vacuum switch** that confirms grip before the robot moves.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

Rotary actuators turn a fixed angle and are rated in **torque**. Grippers need a defined **fail-safe** behavior on air loss. Air motors are spark-free and stall-tolerant but costly to run. Vacuum cups lift with **vacuum × area ÷ safety factor**, and you should always confirm vacuum before moving the load.

<TryIt title="Size a Vacuum Cup">

You need to lift a 12 lb flat steel sheet horizontally. The venturi delivers 20 inHg ≈ **9.8 psi**. Use a safety factor of **2**.

Required theoretical force = 12 × 2 = **24 lbf**.
Required area = 24 ÷ 9.8 ≈ **2.45 in²**.
Cup diameter = 2 × √(2.45 ÷ π) ≈ **1.77 in**.

Using **two 1.5-inch cups** (≈ 1.77 in² each, 3.53 in² total) gives margin and better balance.

</TryIt>
