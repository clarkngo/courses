## Flow Controls

A **flow control valve** is an adjustable needle restriction combined with a **check valve**. Air flows freely one way through the check and is restricted the other way through the needle.

Flow controls are often built into a **banjo fitting** that screws right into the cylinder port. That's the best place for them because the controlled volume is small and the motion is stiffer.

---

## Meter-In vs. Meter-Out

You can restrict the air going **in** to the cylinder (meter-in) or the air coming **out** (meter-out).

**Meter-out is the standard for double-acting cylinders.** The trapped exhaust air acts like a cushion on the other side of the piston, so motion is smooth even if the load changes or tries to run away.

**Meter-in** is used for **single-acting** cylinders (there's no exhaust side to control) and sometimes for very small cylinders.

### Setting Speed

1. Start with both flow controls **mostly closed**.
2. Cycle the cylinder and open each needle a little at a time.
3. Set extend and retract separately; each needle controls one direction.
4. Lock the adjustment once speed is right.

## Quick Exhaust Valves

When you need a cylinder to move **faster** than the main valve's exhaust allows, mount a **quick exhaust valve** right at the cylinder port. It dumps air straight to atmosphere instead of sending it back through the tubing and valve. It's also used on safety dump circuits for fast venting.

## Sensing Cylinder Position

The controller has to know where the cylinder is.

| Sensor | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Reed switch** | Magnet in the piston closes a reed contact | Simple, cheap; can bounce, fails with high current |
| **Solid-state (Hall/GMR) switch** | Magnet in the piston triggers an electronic sensor | No moving parts, longer life; PNP or NPN output |
| **Inductive proximity sensor** | Detects a metal target on the rod or tooling | Doesn't need a magnetic piston |
| **Linear position sensor** | Reports continuous position | For analog positioning |

Sensors clamp in a slot on the cylinder body. Most have an **LED** that lights when the magnet is detected, which is your first troubleshooting check.

<Pitfall title="Sensor Moved, Machine Faults">

**Common mistake:** a cylinder sensor gets bumped during cleaning or a changeover and slides along its slot.

Now the PLC never sees "extended," so the machine times out on a fault, or worse, sees "extended" too early and starts the next step before the cylinder is really there. Mark sensor positions with paint or a scribe line, and check them whenever a cylinder "faults but looks fine."

</Pitfall>

## Common Circuits

### 1. Basic Extend/Retract

A **5/2 single-solenoid** valve with **meter-out** flow controls on both cylinder ports and a sensor at each end. This covers a large share of plant pneumatics.

### 2. Two-Hand Control

For presses and similar hazards, the operator must press **two buttons at the same time** (within about half a second) and hold them, keeping both hands away from the hazard. Two-hand controls are safety devices, and their design is governed by standards such as **ISO 13851**. They're never improvised with standard valves.

### 3. Sequencing

Many machines run steps in order, with each step waiting for a sensor:

## Key Takeaway

Use **meter-out** for double-acting cylinders and mount flow controls **at the cylinder**. **Quick exhaust valves** speed up retraction or venting. Magnetic sensors report position, and when a sequence faults, **check the sensor for the step it stopped on**.

<TryIt title="Fix the Lurching Cylinder">

A vertical cylinder lowering a heavy tool **drops fast** on the way down and is fine going up. It has **meter-in** flow controls on both ports.

Why? *On the way down, the rod points down, so lowering means extending. Gravity pulls the load while the rod-side exhaust flows out freely, and only the supply side is restricted. Nothing holds the load back.*

Fix: switch to **meter-out** on the rod (bottom-side) port so the exhaust air holds back the falling load. For heavy vertical loads, also consider a counterbalance or load-holding check valves.

</TryIt>
