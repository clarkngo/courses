## Air as a Power Source

Walk any packaging line, box plant, or assembly cell and you'll hear it: the hiss and clack of **pneumatics**, which means machines powered by compressed air. Cylinders push cartons, grippers pick parts, blow-offs clear chips, and air motors spin tools.

Pneumatics is one of the three ways plants move things with fluid or electricity:

| Power type | Medium | Typical pressure | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pneumatic** | Air | 80–120 psi | Fast, clean, simple, safe in wet or explosive areas | Spongy (air compresses), expensive energy |
| **Hydraulic** | Oil | 1,000–5,000+ psi | Huge force, precise holding | Leaks are messy, heavier components |
| **Electric** | Electricity | — | Efficient, precise positioning | Higher component cost, sensitive to washdown |

Plants choose air when they need **fast, repetitive, light-to-medium force** motion that's cheap to build and easy to maintain.

The **supply side** (compressor, receiver, dryer) makes the air. The **demand side** (piping, FRLs, valves, actuators) uses it. Most problems show up on the demand side, but many start on the supply side.

## Pressure vs. Flow

These two words get mixed up constantly, and that leads to bad troubleshooting.

- **Pressure** is force per unit area, measured in **psi** (pounds per square inch) or **bar** (1 bar ≈ 14.5 psi). Pressure determines **how hard** an actuator can push.
- **Flow** is the volume of air moving per minute, measured in **scfm** (standard cubic feet per minute) or **L/min**. Flow determines **how fast** an actuator can move.

> **Pressure = force. Flow = speed.** A cylinder that pushes hard but moves slowly is usually starved for flow, not pressure.

### Why "Standard" Cubic Feet?

Air compresses, so a cubic foot of air at 100 psi holds far more air than a cubic foot at atmospheric pressure. **scfm** converts everything back to standard conditions (roughly sea-level pressure and room temperature) so you can compare compressors and loads fairly.

## Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure

We live under about **14.7 psi** of atmospheric pressure at sea level.

| Term | Abbreviation | Zero point | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gauge pressure** | psig | Atmosphere | A shop gauge reading 90 psig |
| **Absolute pressure** | psia | Perfect vacuum | That same air is 90 + 14.7 = 104.7 psia |

Shop gauges read **psig**. Gas law calculations and vacuum work use **psia**. When someone just says "psi" in a plant, they almost always mean gauge.

### Compression Ratio

How much the compressor squeezes the air is its compression ratio:

**Compression ratio = (gauge pressure + 14.7) ÷ 14.7**

At 100 psig: (100 + 14.7) ÷ 14.7 ≈ **7.8**. That means about 7.8 cubic feet of room air are squeezed into each cubic foot of plant air.

## The Hidden Cost of Air

Compressed air is often called the **"fourth utility"** (after electricity, water, and gas), and it's one of the most expensive. Most of the electrical energy that goes into a compressor leaves as **heat**, not as useful air.

A commonly cited rule of thumb: it takes roughly **7–8 horsepower of electricity to deliver 1 horsepower of work** at a pneumatic tool. In other words, air is only about 10–15% efficient end to end.

That's why later lessons focus on leaks, pressure settings, and right-sizing. Every wasted cubic foot was paid for at the compressor.

<Pitfall title="Cranking Up Header Pressure">

**Common mistake:** a machine is sluggish, so someone raises the plant header from 100 to 115 psi.

That raises energy use for the **whole plant**. A widely used rule of thumb is that each **2 psi** increase costs about **1%** more compressor energy, and higher pressure also makes every leak blow harder. Fix the real cause instead, such as an undersized hose, a clogged filter, or a failing regulator at that one machine.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Pneumatics is fast, clean, and simple, but air is costly to make. **Pressure** sets force; **flow** sets speed. Shop gauges read **psig**, and calculations need **psia**. Follow the air from compressor to actuator and you'll always know where to look.

<TryIt title="Read the Nameplate">

Find a compressor or a pneumatic machine in your workplace (or look up a spec sheet online). Write down:

1. Rated pressure (psig or bar)
2. Rated flow (scfm or L/min)
3. Motor horsepower

Then calculate the compression ratio at the rated pressure. For a 125 psig rating: (125 + 14.7) ÷ 14.7 ≈ **9.5**.

</TryIt>
