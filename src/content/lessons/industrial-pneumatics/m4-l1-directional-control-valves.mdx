## Valves Decide Where Air Goes

A **directional control valve (DCV)** routes air to start, stop, and reverse actuators. In Lesson 1.2 you learned to read them: **ports/positions**, boxes, arrows, and T's. Now let's see which valve to use where.

---

## The Common Valve Types

| Valve | Ports / positions | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **2/2** | In, out; open or closed | On/off of air, blow-offs, vacuum |
| **3/2** | Supply, outlet, exhaust | Single-acting cylinders, pilot signals, **lockout/dump valves** |
| **5/2** | Supply, two outlets, two exhausts | Double-acting cylinders (the most common) |
| **5/3** | Like 5/2 plus a spring-centered middle position | Stopping a cylinder mid-stroke or fail-safe behavior |

### Normally Closed vs. Normally Open

For 2/2 and 3/2 valves, the **normal** (unactuated) condition matters:

- **Normally closed (NC):** no output until actuated. Most common, so loss of signal means air stops.
- **Normally open (NO):** output flows until actuated. Used where loss of signal should send air, such as a clamp that must stay closed.

---

## Single vs. Double Solenoid: Valve Memory

This is one of the most important ideas for troubleshooting and safety.

| Valve | When the signal goes away... |
|---|---|
| **Single solenoid, spring return** | Spring shifts the valve back to normal. The cylinder **returns home**. |
| **Double solenoid (detented)** | Valve **stays where it was last shifted**. It has "memory." |

A double-solenoid valve needs a pulse on one coil to shift and a pulse on the other to shift back. If power fails mid-cycle, the cylinder keeps doing whatever it was last told to do.

<Pitfall title="Unexpected Motion After Restoring Air">

**Common mistake:** assuming every cylinder goes "home" after an E-stop or air dump.

With a **double-solenoid** valve, the spool remembers its last position. When air is restored, the cylinder can suddenly move to that position, even with no electrical signal. That's why machines use **soft-start valves**, why you should know each valve's type before restoring air, and why you must stay clear when air is turned back on.

</Pitfall>

## 5/3 Center Conditions

A **5/3** valve has a third, **spring-centered** middle position. What that center does defines how the cylinder behaves when both solenoids are off.

| Center | What happens | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed (all ports blocked)** | Air is trapped in both sides; cylinder tries to hold position | Stopping mid-stroke (but leakage lets it drift) |
| **Exhaust (open) center** | Both cylinder ports vent; cylinder is free to move by hand | Releasing a load, manual positioning, safe stop for many cases |
| **Pressure center** | Supply goes to both sides | Special applications; acts like a pressure-balanced hold |

Because air compresses and seals leak, **a 5/3 closed center is not a reliable lock**. If a cylinder must hold a load safely, add **piloted check valves** at the cylinder ports or a mechanical **rod lock**.

## How Valves Are Actuated

| Actuation | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Manual** | Push button, lever, foot pedal |
| **Mechanical** | Roller, plunger, cam |
| **Pneumatic pilot** | An air signal shifts the valve |
| **Solenoid** | An electric coil shifts the valve; usually 24 VDC in modern machines |

### Pilot-Operated Solenoid Valves

Moving a large spool directly with a solenoid would take a large coil. Most industrial solenoid valves are **pilot operated**: a small solenoid opens a tiny pilot passage, and that pilot air shifts the main spool. Because of this, many pilot-operated valves need a **minimum supply pressure** (often roughly 20–30 psi, depending on the model) to shift reliably.

### Manual Override

Most solenoid valves have a small **manual override** button or screw that shifts the spool without electricity. It's a powerful troubleshooting tool: if the valve works by override but not electrically, look at the coil, wiring, or PLC output.

Use it only when you understand exactly what the machine will do. **Never leave an override locked in.**

---

## Valve Manifolds and Fieldbus

Modern machines mount many valves side by side on a **manifold**, sharing supply and exhaust galleries. The electrical connection is often a single multi-pin connector or an **industrial network** (such as EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, or IO-Link) instead of separate wires per coil.

Troubleshooting tips:

- Each station usually has an **LED per solenoid**. LED on with no motion points to a mechanical or air problem. LED off points to the controls side.
- Station numbering and coil addressing are on the machine's drawings, so check them before swapping valves.

---

## Key Takeaway

Use **3/2** for single-acting and dump functions, **5/2** for most double-acting cylinders, and **5/3** when the stop condition matters. **Single-solenoid** valves return home on signal loss; **double-solenoid** valves **remember**. A closed-center valve is **not a lock**. Overrides and LEDs split air problems from electrical ones.

<TryIt title="Valve Detective">

A cylinder won't extend. The manifold LED for that coil is **on**.

1. What does the LED tell you? *(The PLC is commanding the coil, so the electrical side is probably fine.)*
2. You press the manual override and the cylinder **still** won't move. What next? *(Check supply pressure at the manifold, a stuck spool, blocked tubing, a closed flow control, or a mechanically jammed cylinder.)*
3. Now it extends on override but not on the coil. Where is the fault? *(The coil or its connector. The LED may be on at the plug while the coil itself is open.)*

</TryIt>
