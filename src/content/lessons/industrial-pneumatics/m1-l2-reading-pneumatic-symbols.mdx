## Why Schematics Matter

A pneumatic machine can have dozens of valves and cylinders behind guards and inside cabinets. The **schematic** is the map. If you can read it, you can trace where air should go, then go check where it actually goes.

Pneumatic and hydraulic schematics use the same international standard, **ISO 1219**. Learn it once and you can read both.

---

## The Valve Is a Row of Boxes

The single most important idea: **each box is one position the valve can be in.**

- The **number of boxes** = the number of **positions**
- The **lines touching one box** (usually the normal box) = the number of **ports**
- Arrows inside a box show where air flows **in that position**
- A **T** shape inside a box means that port is **blocked**

Valves are named **ports/positions**. A **5/2 valve** has 5 ports and 2 positions. A **3/2 valve** has 3 ports and 2 positions.

### Reading the Example

- **Normal position (right box, spring holds it there):** supply **1 → 2**, and **4 → 5** exhausts. Port 3 is blocked.
- **Shifted position (left box, solenoid energized):** supply **1 → 4**, and **2 → 3** exhausts. Port 5 is blocked.

Imagine sliding the left box over to line up with the port lines. That's what happens when the solenoid fires.

Hook port 4 to the cap end and port 2 to the rod end of the cylinder, and you have the most common circuit in pneumatics: energize to extend, de-energize to retract.

## Port Numbering (ISO 5599)

Port numbers tell you a port's job, even when the drawing is cluttered.

| Port | Function |
|---|---|
| **1** | Supply (pressure in) |
| **2, 4** | Working ports (out to the actuator) |
| **3, 5** | Exhaust |
| **12, 14** | Pilot signals: "12" connects 1 to 2, "14" connects 1 to 4 |

Older drawings use letters: **P** (pressure), **A** and **B** (working), **R** and **S** or **EA/EB** (exhaust).

## Common Symbols

| Symbol idea | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circle with a dark triangle pointing **out** | Compressor (source) |
| Small open circle with a dot | Pressure source connection |
| Open triangle at a port | Exhaust to atmosphere |
| Rectangle with a diagonal line at a valve end | Solenoid actuator |
| Zig-zag at a valve end | Spring return |
| Diamond | Conditioning device (filter, dryer, lubricator) |
| Arrow through a symbol | Adjustable (e.g., regulator, flow control) |
| Rectangle with piston line and rod | Cylinder |

## The "Normal" Position

Schematics are drawn with the machine **at rest, with power and air applied but nothing actuated**. The port lines always attach to the box that represents that state.

<Pitfall title="Assuming the Drawing Shows Air Off">

**Common mistake:** reading a schematic as if air is shut off.

Schematics show the **at-rest, air-on** condition. A spring-return valve in its normal box is **still sending air** to one side of the cylinder. That cylinder is held under pressure even though nothing is "on." This is stored energy, and it's why lockout requires dumping air (Module 6).

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Boxes are **positions**, lines are **ports**, arrows are **flow paths**, and T's are **blocked ports**. Port **1** is supply, **2/4** are working, and **3/5** are exhaust. The drawing shows the machine at rest **with air on**.
