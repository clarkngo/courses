## What a Compressor Does

A compressor takes in ambient air and reduces its volume, which raises its pressure. Two side effects come with every compressor:

1. **Heat.** Compressing air makes it hot. Most of the motor's energy ends up as heat.
2. **Water.** Room air carries water vapor. Squeezing it concentrates that water, and when the air cools, liquid water drops out.

Managing heat and water is half of compressed-air maintenance.

---

## The Three Main Compressor Types

| Type | How it works | Typical size | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocating (piston)** | A piston in a cylinder, like an engine in reverse | Fractional hp to ~30 hp (larger industrial units exist) | Small shops, intermittent use, high pressure needs |
| **Rotary screw** | Two meshing helical rotors trap and squeeze air continuously | ~5 hp to 500+ hp | The workhorse of most plants |
| **Centrifugal** | High-speed impellers accelerate air, then a diffuser converts speed to pressure | Several hundred hp and up | Large plants with big, steady base loads |

### Oil-Flooded vs. Oil-Free

Most rotary screws are **oil-flooded**: oil is injected into the rotors to seal, cool, and lubricate, then separated from the air afterward. **Oil-free** compressors cost more but are required where oil carryover can't be tolerated, such as food, pharmaceutical, and some electronics work.

---

## Controlling Output: Matching Supply to Demand

Plant demand rises and falls all day. The compressor control decides how to follow it.

| Control | How it works | Efficiency at part load |
|---|---|---|
| **Start/stop** | Motor shuts off at the high setpoint, restarts at the low one | Good, but limited starts per hour; small units only |
| **Load/unload** | Motor keeps running; inlet closes and the unit "unloads" at the high setpoint | Poor: an unloaded screw can still draw a large share of full-load power |
| **Modulation** | Inlet valve throttles to reduce output | Poor |
| **Variable speed drive (VSD)** | Motor speed changes to match demand | Best for varying loads |

A common plant strategy is to run one or more fixed-speed compressors fully loaded for the **base load** and let one **VSD trim compressor** follow the swings.

### Duty Cycle

Reciprocating compressors are often rated for a **duty cycle** below 100%. Run them continuously and they overheat. Rotary screws are designed for continuous duty. If a piston compressor never shuts off, the plant has outgrown it, or there's a big leak.

---

## Heat of Compression and Cooling

Compressor discharge air can be very hot. An **aftercooler** (air-cooled or water-cooled) brings it down near ambient, which condenses most of the water right away. A **moisture separator** and **drain** after the aftercooler catch that water.

Heat recovery is a real opportunity: much of the compressor's input energy can be captured from the cooling air or oil to heat buildings or process water.

---

## Daily and Weekly Compressor Room Checks

- **Discharge pressure and temperature** — compare to normal readings
- **Oil level** (oil-flooded units) and any oil carryover downstream
- **Separator differential pressure** — high readings mean the oil separator element is loading up
- **Inlet filter condition** — a dirty inlet starves the compressor
- **Drains** — verify they actually discharge water
- **Unusual noise, vibration, or leaks**
- **Ventilation** — the room must get rid of the compressor's heat

<Pitfall title="Ignoring the Compressor Room Temperature">

**Common mistake:** closing up a compressor room in winter, or putting a new compressor in a room built for a smaller one.

Compressors recirculate their own hot exhaust air, run hot, and trip on high temperature, usually on the hottest day of production. Hot intake air is also less dense, so output drops. Duct the hot air out and bring cool air in.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

**Rotary screws** run most plants; **pistons** suit small, intermittent loads; **centrifugals** suit large steady loads. Every compressor produces **heat and water** that must be handled. **VSD trim** with base-loaded fixed units is a common efficient setup, and daily checks catch most problems early.

<TryIt title="Estimate Unloaded Waste">

A 100 hp load/unload screw compressor spends 50% of the day unloaded. Assume it still draws about 30% of full power while unloaded (actual values vary by machine, so check the manufacturer's data).

Roughly how many horsepower are wasted on average? 100 hp × 30% × 50% ≈ **15 hp**, around the clock, producing no air. That's the case for a VSD trim unit or better storage.

</TryIt>
