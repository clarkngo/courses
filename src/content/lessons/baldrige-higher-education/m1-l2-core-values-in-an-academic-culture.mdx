## Why Start With Values

The Criteria questions only make sense once you understand the beliefs behind them. Baldrige calls these the **core values and concepts**. They're the behaviors found in high-performing organizations, and every Criteria question can be traced back to one or more of them.

There are eleven. In the Education edition, the third reads **student-focused excellence**:

1. **Systems perspective**
2. **Visionary leadership**
3. **Student-focused excellence** (customer-focused excellence in other sectors)
4. **Valuing people**
5. **Agility and resilience**
6. **Organizational learning**
7. **Focus on success and innovation**
8. **Management by fact**
9. **Societal contributions**
10. **Ethics and transparency**
11. **Delivering value and results**

## What Each Value Looks Like at STC

| Core value | At Northbridge's School of Technology and Computing, it looks like… |
|---|---|
| **Systems perspective** | Seeing that a change to the intro programming course affects data structures, the capstone, advising load, and retention. |
| **Visionary leadership** | The dean sets a clear direction ("every graduate can build responsibly with AI") and models it. |
| **Student-focused excellence** | Designing schedules, support, and assessments around working adults who study at night across time zones. |
| **Valuing people** | Treating practitioner adjunct faculty as colleagues: onboarding, a voice in curriculum, development, and recognition. |
| **Agility and resilience** | Updating a cloud course in one quarter when a provider retires a service, not waiting for a two-year review cycle. |
| **Organizational learning** | Turning each quarter's course data and faculty debriefs into changes for the next quarter. |
| **Focus on success and innovation** | Running small pilots, like AI tutoring in one section, and scaling what works. |
| **Management by fact** | Deciding where to add tutoring based on DFW rates (D, F, and withdrawal) by course and student group, not on anecdotes. |
| **Societal contributions** | Free cyber-hygiene workshops for local nonprofits; building a diverse regional tech workforce. |
| **Ethics and transparency** | Publishing outcomes honestly, protecting student data, and having clear rules on academic integrity and AI use. |
| **Delivering value and results** | Graduates who get and keep good jobs, at a cost and time that make sense for working adults. |

## Answering the Objections

Faculty will push back, and they should. Have honest answers ready.

**"Students aren't customers."** Agreed, they aren't *only* customers. They're also learners and partners in their own education. The Education Criteria say "students **and other customers**" on purpose. Being student-focused doesn't mean giving students whatever they ask for. It means understanding what they need to succeed, including rigor, and designing for it. Employers, transfer partners, and the community are customers too.

A student plays three roles at once, and a student-focused school designs for all of them:

**"This threatens academic freedom."** Baldrige never prescribes *what* to teach or research. It asks how the school decides on curriculum, how it knows students are learning, and how it improves. Faculty keep authority over content, and Baldrige makes their decisions visible and evidence-based.

**"We have shared governance; we can't 'manage' like a company."** Category 1 asks about *your* leadership and governance system, whatever it is. Shared governance is a structure Baldrige expects you to describe and use well, for example in how the faculty council reviews program changes.

**"This is corporate jargon."** Some terms are. Translate them into your own language ("work processes" becomes "how we design and deliver courses"). The questions are what matter, not the words.

<Pitfall title="Values on the Wall, Not in the Work">

**Common mistake:** a school puts "student-centered" in its values statement. Meanwhile, advising is only available 9 to 5 Pacific time, for a student body that mostly works days and partly lives in other time zones.

Examiners look for values **in how work is done**, not in statements. For each value you claim, ask: *Which process shows this, and what result proves it?* If you can't answer, the value is aspirational, which is fine, but say so.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

The **eleven core values** are the beliefs behind every Criteria question. In the Education edition, the customer value becomes **student-focused excellence**. Translate each value into **concrete behaviors** in your school, and treat faculty objections as useful: Baldrige asks *how* and *how do you know*, never *what* to teach. A value only counts when you can point to **a process that shows it and a result that proves it**.

<TryIt title="Run a Values Evidence Check">

With two or three colleagues, pick **three core values** your school says it holds. For each, fill in:

| Value | A process that shows it | A result that proves it | Gap? |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

Example for STC: *Student-focused excellence* → evening and weekend virtual advising → advising satisfaction 4.4/5 for online students, up from 3.6 → *no gap*.

Any row you can't complete is a candidate improvement project.

</TryIt>
