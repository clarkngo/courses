## Data-Intensive, Not Compute-Intensive

Most applications today aren't limited by CPU. They're limited by **data**: how much of it there is, how fast it changes, and how many places it has to be stored, indexed, cached, and moved. A typical backend combines several systems, each good at one job:

- A **database** to store data durably.
- A **cache** to make expensive reads fast.
- A **search index** to query by keyword.
- A **message queue or log** to pass events between services.
- **Batch and stream processors** to compute derived data.

When you wire these together, you're designing a data system, whether you call it that or not. The rest of this course is about the ideas inside those building blocks and the trade-offs in combining them.

Three goals frame every design decision.

## Reliability: Working Correctly When Things Go Wrong

A reliable system keeps doing the right thing even when parts of it fail. Distinguish two words:

- A **fault** is one component deviating from its spec: a disk dies, a process crashes, a network link drops packets.
- A **failure** is the system as a whole stopping providing the service users expect.

You can't prevent all faults, so the goal is **fault tolerance**: stopping faults from becoming failures.

Faults come in three families:

1. **Hardware faults.** Disks, memory, power, and networks fail regularly at scale. Large fleets see machine failures every day. Redundancy and the ability to lose whole machines handle these.
2. **Software faults.** Bugs that hit many machines at once: a bad config pushed everywhere, a leap-second bug, a runaway process exhausting a shared resource. These are often worse than hardware faults because they're correlated.
3. **Human errors.** Operators misconfigure things. Studies of large services have repeatedly found configuration errors among the leading causes of outages. Good defaults, staged rollouts, easy rollback, and sandboxes reduce them.

<Pitfall title="Assuming Faults Are Independent">

**Common mistake:** "We have three replicas, each 99.9% available, so we're effectively never down."

That math assumes failures are independent. A bad deploy, a shared dependency, or a region-wide outage can take down all three at once. Ask what your replicas **share**: code, config, power, network, operators.

</Pitfall>

## Scalability: Coping with Growth

"Is it scalable?" is meaningless on its own. The useful question is: **if load grows in a particular way, what happens to performance, and what can we do about it?**

### Describe load with load parameters

Pick the numbers that actually drive your system's work:

- Requests per second, split by type (reads vs. writes).
- Ratio of reads to writes.
- Number of concurrent active users.
- Data volume, and how fast it grows.
- **Fan-out**: how many downstream operations one request triggers.

Fan-out often matters most. Consider a social feed. When a user posts, you can either:

- **Write-time fan-out:** push the post into each follower's precomputed feed. Reads are cheap; writes are expensive for accounts with millions of followers.
- **Read-time fan-out:** store the post once and assemble feeds when users open the app. Writes are cheap; reads are expensive.

Many real systems use a **hybrid**: push for most accounts, pull for the few with huge follower counts. The right answer depends on your load parameters, not on a general rule.

### Describe performance with percentiles

Response times vary. Averages hide that. Use **percentiles**:

- **p50 (median):** half of requests are faster than this. The typical experience.
- **p95, p99, p99.9:** the slow tail. One in a hundred requests is slower than p99.

Tail latencies matter because:

- The slowest requests often belong to your **most valuable users**, who have the most data.
- **Tail latency amplification:** if one user action calls 10 backends in parallel and waits for all of them, the action is as slow as the slowest call. Even if each backend is slow only 1% of the time, about 1 in 10 actions will hit at least one slow call.

Service level objectives are usually written in percentiles: "p99 under 300 ms, measured over 5 minutes."

### Scaling up and out

- **Scale up (vertical):** a bigger machine. Simple, eventually limited and expensive.
- **Scale out (horizontal):** more machines. Adds the complexity of distributing data, which Modules 3–5 cover.

Stateless services scale out easily. Stateful data systems are where the hard problems live.

## Maintainability: Working Well for Years

Most software cost comes **after** launch: operating, debugging, adapting, and extending. Three principles help:

- **Operability:** make it easy for operators to monitor, deploy, recover, and understand the system.
- **Simplicity:** reduce accidental complexity with good abstractions that hide detail without hiding behavior you need to reason about.
- **Evolvability:** make it easy to change data models, schemas, and behavior as requirements change. Lesson 2.2 returns to this for data formats.
