## Know the Failure Modes Before You Trust the Tool

You'd never prescribe a drug without knowing its adverse effects. Treat AI the same way. Most clinical AI errors fall into a handful of recognizable patterns. Once you know them, you'll spot them quickly.

---

## 1. Fabrication ("Hallucination")

Generative models produce text that is **plausible**, not text that is **verified**. They can invent:

- Citations that look real (correct-sounding journal, authors, year) but don't exist
- Exam findings, history, or medication doses in a summarized note
- Drug interactions that don't exist, or omit ones that do
- Guideline recommendations attributed to the wrong society or year

Fabrications are dangerous precisely because they're **formatted like facts**. They don't come with a warning label.

## 2. Stale or Incomplete Knowledge

A model's built-in knowledge stops at its training cutoff and may be incomplete well before that. It may not know about a newly approved drug, a revised guideline, a recent safety communication, or your institution's formulary. Some tools can search the web or a curated evidence database; many can't, and they rarely tell you which mode they're in unless you ask.

## 3. Sycophancy and Anchoring

LLMs are trained to be helpful and agreeable, so they tend to **follow your framing**. Ask *"Why is this presentation consistent with GERD?"* and you'll get a fluent case for GERD, even if the details should have raised concern for ACS. Push back on a correct answer ("Are you sure?") and the model may abandon it.

This mirrors a familiar human bias, anchoring, and it compounds it: the model reflects your anchor back with added confidence.

## 4. Bias

AI learns from historical data, and historical care was not equitable. A widely cited 2019 study in *Science* (Obermeyer and colleagues) found that a commercial algorithm used to target care-management programs **systematically underestimated the needs of Black patients**, because it used past healthcare **cost** as a proxy for health **need**, and less had historically been spent on Black patients with the same illness burden.

Bias can also appear in generative output: stereotyped assumptions in a summary, differential diagnoses that shift with a patient's stated race or gender in ways not supported by evidence, or lower performance in languages other than English.

## 5. Dataset Shift

A model is validated on one population, at one time, under one workflow. Change any of those and performance can degrade, sometimes sharply. In a 2021 external validation published in *JAMA Internal Medicine*, Wong and colleagues found that a widely deployed proprietary sepsis prediction model performed far below its developer's reported accuracy, missed about two thirds of sepsis cases, and generated alerts on nearly one in five hospitalized patients.

The lesson isn't that predictive models are useless. It's that **performance must be checked locally and monitored over time**.

## 6. Exam Scores Aren't Bedside Performance

Headlines about AI "passing the medical boards" are real, but multiple-choice exams present clean, complete, pre-selected information with one right answer. Real patients present incomplete, contradictory, evolving information, much of it unspoken, and much of the clinical skill lies in deciding **what to ask and examine next**. High exam scores show broad knowledge, not readiness for unsupervised care.

<Pitfall title="Trusting Fluency as a Signal of Accuracy">

**Common mistake:** treating a well-organized, confident, terminology-rich answer as more reliable than a hesitant one.

For generative AI, fluency and accuracy are largely independent. A model will describe a fabricated drug interaction in the same polished register as a real one. Calibrate trust to **verification**, not to tone.

</Pitfall>

## A Failure-Mode Quick Reference

| Failure mode | Most likely in | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Fabrication | Generative AI | Verify facts, citations, and anything you didn't personally observe |
| Stale knowledge | Generative AI | Check against current guidelines and drug labeling |
| Sycophancy | Generative AI | Ask neutral questions and request alternatives |
| Bias | All three | Ask how the tool performs across subgroups. Watch for patterns |
| Dataset shift | Predictive and device AI | Ask about local validation and ongoing monitoring |
| Exam ≠ bedside | Generative AI | Don't extrapolate benchmark results to autonomous use |

<TryIt title="Provoke Two Failure Modes (Safely)">

Use a general AI assistant and **no patient information**.

1. **Sycophancy:** Write a brief, fictional vignette with features that point toward a serious diagnosis but could be mistaken for a benign one. Ask the model *"Why is this most consistent with [benign diagnosis]?"* Then, in a new chat, ask neutrally: *"What is the differential diagnosis, and what can't be missed?"* Compare the two answers.
2. **Fabrication:** Ask for *"three recent peer-reviewed studies, with full citations,"* on a narrow clinical question in your specialty. Look up each citation in PubMed or the journal's site. Record how many exist exactly as cited.

</TryIt>
