## "AI" Means Several Different Things in Healthcare

When a colleague says "we're rolling out AI," they could mean a deterioration score in the EHR, an FDA-cleared algorithm that flags intracranial hemorrhage on CT, or a chatbot that drafts replies to patient portal messages. These tools fail in different ways, carry different regulatory status, and demand different kinds of oversight. Fluency starts with telling them apart.

| | **Predictive models** | **Device AI (SaMD)** | **Generative AI** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | Sepsis and deterioration scores, readmission risk, no-show prediction | Imaging triage and detection, ECG interpretation, diabetic retinopathy screening | Ambient scribes, inbox reply drafts, chart summarization, general chat assistants |
| **Output** | A score or alert | A finding, flag, or measurement | Free text (notes, letters, answers) |
| **Built from** | Historical EHR data, often at one institution | Curated, labeled datasets for a narrow task | Very large general text corpora, sometimes tuned on clinical text |
| **Typical oversight** | Local governance committee | FDA clearance or approval (US), CE marking (EU) | Varies widely; often none specific to the use case |
| **Classic failure** | Performs worse at a new site or over time | Narrow: misses what it wasn't trained on | Fluent, plausible, and wrong |

### Predictive models

These estimate the probability of an outcome from structured data: vitals, labs, demographics, utilization. They've been in EHRs for years. Their key weakness is **dataset shift**: a model trained on one hospital's patients, documentation habits, and coding practices may perform very differently at another hospital, or at the same hospital after a workflow change.

### Device AI

Software intended to diagnose, treat, or prevent disease can be regulated as a medical device: **Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)**. The US FDA's public list of AI-enabled devices passed one thousand entries in 2025, with radiology accounting for roughly three quarters. Clearance means the device met a regulatory bar for a **specific intended use** and population. It doesn't mean it will work equally well for every patient in your clinic.

### Generative AI

Large language models (LLMs) produce text by predicting likely next words. They power ambient documentation, inbox drafting, chart summarization, and general-purpose assistants clinicians use on their own. They're remarkably capable with language and can perform well on licensing-exam questions, but they **have no built-in mechanism to know when they're wrong**. Most of this course focuses here, because generative AI is where individual clinicians make the most day-to-day judgment calls.

<Pitfall title="Treating All 'AI' as Equally Validated">

**Common mistake:** assuming a general chatbot carries the same evidence base as an FDA-cleared imaging algorithm, or that either is validated for *your* patients.

Ask of any tool: *What exactly is it intended to do? On whom was it tested? Who monitors it here?* A general-purpose assistant answering a dosing question has no clinical validation for that use at all.

</Pitfall>

## A Framework for Using Any of Them: The 4Ds

This course applies the **4D AI Fluency Framework** to clinical work. Each competency answers a question you face whenever AI touches patient care:

    { title: "Diligence", detail: "Is my use ethical, compliant, and accountable?" },
  ]}
/>

| Competency | In the clinic, it sounds like… |
|---|---|
| **Delegation** | "An AI draft of this patient education handout is fine. An AI deciding whether this chest pain needs a troponin is not." |
| **Description** | "I gave the model the patient's age, renal function, and current medications, and asked it to flag uncertainty." |
| **Discernment** | "The scribe note documented a normal abdominal exam I never performed. I removed it." |
| **Diligence** | "I used our BAA-covered tool, not a consumer app, and I signed the note knowing I own every word." |

The modules follow this structure: the landscape and failure modes first, then one or more modules per competency.

<TryIt title="Inventory the AI in Your Workflow">

1. List every AI-powered tool you encounter in a typical week: EHR alerts and scores, imaging flags, scribe or dictation tools, inbox drafts, and anything you use on your own (including a phone app).
2. Classify each as **predictive**, **device**, or **generative**.
3. For each, write whether you know (a) who approved it for use in your organization and (b) whether it's been validated on a population like yours.
4. Circle any tool where you can't answer either question. You'll revisit this list in Module 6.

</TryIt>
