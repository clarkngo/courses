## A Fast Research Assistant With a Fabrication Problem

AI can dramatically speed up the early stages of answering a clinical question: framing it, suggesting search terms, summarizing papers you provide, and comparing recommendations across guidelines. It can also produce **references that don't exist**, misstate effect sizes, and present an outdated recommendation as current. The difference comes down to **which kind of tool** you use and **how you verify**.

---

## Two Kinds of Evidence Tools

| | **General assistant (no retrieval)** | **Retrieval-grounded tool** |
|---|---|---|
| **How it answers** | From patterns in training data | Searches a defined source (literature database, guideline library, the web), then answers with links |
| **Citations** | May be fabricated or garbled | Linked to actual documents, though the summary can still misstate them |
| **Currency** | Fixed at training cutoff | As current as its source index |
| **Best for** | Framing questions, explaining concepts, drafting search strategies | Finding and summarizing specific evidence |

Several clinical evidence platforms now offer retrieval-grounded AI answers, and many general assistants can search the web. Retrieval reduces fabrication, but it doesn't eliminate **misrepresentation**: the model can still cite a real paper and get its findings wrong.

### Framing with PICO

> *"Help me turn this into a PICO question and suggest MeSH terms and a PubMed search string: In older adults with atrial fibrillation and a history of falls, does anticoagulation still provide net benefit?"*

This is a low-risk, high-value use: AI is good at structuring questions and suggesting terminology, and you can see the result.

### Summarizing papers you provide

Rather than asking AI what the literature says, **give it the paper** (an open-access PDF or abstract, consistent with copyright and your tool's terms) and ask:

> *"Summarize the population, intervention, comparator, primary outcome, effect size with confidence interval, and key limitations. Quote the sentence supporting each number."*

Asking for **quoted support** makes misstatements immediately checkable.

### Comparing guidelines

> *"Compare the recommendations on [topic] from [Society A, year] and [Society B, year]. Note where they differ and the evidence grade for each."*

Then open both guidelines. Check dates: a model may confidently describe a superseded version.

<Pitfall title="Citing a Reference You Haven't Opened">

**Common mistake:** copying an AI-provided citation into a note, presentation, manuscript, or policy without opening it.

Fabricated references have appeared in submitted manuscripts and legal filings. Even real references can be misattributed or misdescribed. **If you haven't opened it and confirmed it supports the claim, don't cite it.**

</Pitfall>

## Appraisal Still Belongs to You

AI summaries tend to flatten nuance. Watch for:

- **Population mismatch.** The trial excluded patients over 75, or with CKD, like yours.
- **Surrogate vs. clinical outcomes.** An improved biomarker isn't necessarily an improved outcome.
- **Relative vs. absolute effects.** "Reduced risk by 30%" might mean 1.0% → 0.7%.
- **Evidence grade.** Expert consensus presented with the same confidence as an RCT.
- **Date.** Newer evidence or safety communications may have changed the picture.

You can ask AI to help surface these (*"What are the main threats to applicability for an 82-year-old with CKD stage 4?"*), but the judgment is yours.

<TryIt title="Audit an AI Evidence Answer">

1. Pick a focused clinical question in your specialty that you know reasonably well.
2. Ask a general AI assistant for an answer with **three supporting citations**.
3. Ask a retrieval-grounded tool (if you have access) the same question.
4. For every citation from both: does it exist? Does it say what the AI claimed? Is it the most current evidence?
5. Note the error rate for each. Use that to calibrate how you'll use each tool going forward.

</TryIt>
