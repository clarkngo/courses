## Delegation Is a Clinical Judgment

Clinicians delegate constantly: to residents, nurses, MAs, pharmacists, scribes. Good delegation matches the task to the delegate's competence and sets the right level of supervision. AI is a new kind of delegate with an unusual profile: **broad knowledge, excellent language, no accountability, no examination skills, and unpredictable errors.**

The question is never just "Can AI do this?" It's **"What happens to the patient if AI does this wrong, and will I catch it?"**

| Tier | Examples | Required review |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Low risk** | Meeting summaries, policy drafts, teaching slides, generic patient-education outlines (no PHI), scheduling templates, appeal-letter structure | Normal proofreading |
| **2. Moderate risk** | Ambient scribe notes, discharge instruction drafts, portal message replies, chart summaries, prior-authorization letters with clinical detail | **Line-by-line review** against what you know and observed, before signing or sending |
| **3. High risk** | Differential diagnosis brainstorming, "what am I missing?" checks, literature synthesis for a specific patient, drug-interaction screening | Use **only after forming your own judgment**. Verify any actionable claim against an authoritative source |
| **4. Not delegable** | Final diagnosis, treatment and dosing decisions, triage disposition, goals-of-care conversations, informing a patient of bad news, anything requiring licensure | AI may support preparation; **a clinician decides and communicates** |

### Notes on applying the tiers

- **Tier moves with context.** A discharge-instruction draft is Tier 2 for a straightforward case, but closer to Tier 3 for a complex anticoagulation plan.
- **Validated, purpose-built tools may sit lower** than general assistants for the same task. An FDA-cleared ECG algorithm used per its label differs from asking a chatbot to read an ECG.
- **Your organization's policy overrides this model.** If a use isn't approved, it's not available to you, whatever its tier.

<Pitfall title="Delegating the Part You Most Needed to Do">

**Common mistake:** using AI to summarize a complex chart *before* you've read it, then reasoning from the summary.

The summary may be accurate, but you skipped the process that builds clinical understanding, and you can't detect an omission in material you haven't seen. For high-stakes cases, **read first, then use AI to check what you might have missed**, not the other way around.

</Pitfall>

## Augment, Don't Abdicate

Three questions to ask before delegating any clinical task:

1. **Severity:** If the output is wrong and I miss it, what's the worst plausible outcome for the patient?
2. **Detectability:** Am I positioned to catch the error? (Did I examine the patient? Do I know this domain well?)
3. **Reversibility:** Can the error be corrected before it reaches the patient?

High severity + low detectability + low reversibility = don't delegate, or delegate only as a supplementary check.

### Watch for deskilling

Repeatedly delegating a skill can erode it. Trainees in particular should build core competencies (note writing, differential construction, literature appraisal) **before** leaning on AI for them, so they can recognize when it's wrong.
