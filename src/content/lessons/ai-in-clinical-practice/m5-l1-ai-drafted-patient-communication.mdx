## The Inbox Problem

Patient portal messages have become one of the largest sources of after-hours work for clinicians. Many health systems now offer AI tools that draft replies for clinician review. Early evaluations have reported reduced feelings of burden among users, though not always measurable time savings. And patients often rate AI-drafted text as empathetic: in a 2023 *JAMA Internal Medicine* study, Ayers and colleagues found that evaluators preferred chatbot responses to public online medical questions over physician responses for both quality and empathy.

That doesn't mean AI should talk to your patients for you. It means AI can help you produce **clear, warm first drafts** that you make accurate and appropriate.

---

## Where AI Helps Most

| Use | Why it helps | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Portal reply drafts** | Reduces blank-page time; consistent tone | Wrong clinical content, over-reassurance, answering what wasn't asked |
| **Plain-language rewrites** | Translates jargon to a target reading level | Lost nuance; oversimplified risk |
| **Patient education handouts** | Fast, tailored to a condition and audience | Outdated recommendations; generic content not matching your plan |
| **Results explanations** | Helps explain what a result means in context | Explaining a result without the clinical context you hold |
| **Difficult-message structure** | Helps organize a message about a delay, error, or limit | Tone that sounds scripted or evasive |

### Accuracy first

AI drafts can be confidently wrong about the patient's own situation: assuming a medication they're not on, misreading the question, or offering reassurance you wouldn't. **Edit the clinical content before you edit the tone.**

### Escalate, don't reassure

If a message describes a potentially urgent symptom, a draft that says "this is common and usually not serious" is a safety risk. Make sure the reply directs the patient to the right level of care and states explicit return precautions.

### Readability

Aim for roughly a 6th–8th grade reading level for general patient materials. You can prompt for this directly (*"Rewrite at a 6th-grade reading level, keeping all numbers and instructions unchanged"*) and then check that no meaning was lost.

<Pitfall title="Using AI Translation as a Substitute for an Interpreter">

**Common mistake:** sending an AI-translated message or handout to a patient with limited English proficiency without qualified review.

Machine translation of medical content can drop negations, mistranslate dosing, or use the wrong register. For clinical instructions, use your organization's **qualified interpreter and translation services**. Language-access obligations (such as Section 1557 of the ACA in the US) apply regardless of the tool.

</Pitfall>

## Transparency

Should patients know AI helped draft a message? Practices vary, and some jurisdictions now regulate it. For example, a California law effective in 2025 requires certain AI-generated clinical communications to include a disclaimer unless a licensed provider reviews them. Regardless of legal requirements:

- **Follow your organization's disclosure policy.**
- **Never imply a message is personally written in a way that misleads** the patient about who considered their case.
- **Make sure you've actually reviewed it.** A signature means you stand behind the content.

## When Not to Use AI

- Delivering a new serious diagnosis or bad news
- Responding to a complaint about care or a possible error (involve your risk or patient-relations team)
- Messages where the patient's emotional state or relationship history matters more than the information
- Anything where you can't fully review the content before sending

<TryIt title="Draft, Then Fix">

Using an approved tool or a general assistant with **no PHI**:

1. Write a realistic fictional patient message, such as a question about a new medication side effect.
2. Ask AI for a reply draft at a 6th-grade reading level with explicit when-to-call guidance.
3. Apply the four-step review. List every change you had to make, labeling each as accuracy, safety, readability, or voice.
4. Which category needed the most edits? That's where to focus your review going forward.

</TryIt>
