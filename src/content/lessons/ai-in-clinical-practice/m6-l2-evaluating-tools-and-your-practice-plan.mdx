## You Have a Voice in What Gets Deployed

Clinicians are increasingly asked to pilot, evaluate, or champion AI tools. Even if you never sit on a governance committee, you'll be asked "Do you like it?" A fluent answer goes beyond "it saves me time" to whether it's **safe, effective, equitable, and worth it**.

### 1. Purpose

- What specific clinical or operational problem does it solve? How is that problem measured today?
- What is the **intended use** and intended population? Is our use within it?
- What happens if we don't adopt it?

### 2. Evidence

- Was it validated **externally**, not just at the developer's site?
- Was it validated **prospectively**, in real workflow, or only retrospectively?
- What outcomes were measured: accuracy, or actual patient and clinician outcomes?
- Has it been tested **here**, in a silent or shadow mode, before going live?

### 3. Equity

- How does performance vary by age, sex, race and ethnicity, language, insurance, and other relevant subgroups?
- Does it use proxies (like cost or utilization) that may encode historical inequity?
- For generative tools: how does it perform for patients who speak languages other than English, or use non-standard dialects?

### 4. Workflow

- Who sees the output, when, and what are they expected to do with it?
- What's the expected alert burden or review time?
- Does it make the right thing easier, or just add a step?

### 5. Data and privacy

- Is there a BAA? Where is data processed and stored, and for how long?
- Is our data used to train the vendor's models? Can we opt out?
- What's the security posture and incident-response commitment?

### 6. Monitoring

- Who owns ongoing performance monitoring? What metrics, how often?
- How do frontline staff report problems, and who responds?
- What's the plan if performance drifts, or the vendor updates the model?

<Pitfall title="Evaluating on Demo Cases">

**Common mistake:** judging a tool by a polished vendor demo or a handful of hand-picked examples.

Demos show best cases. Ask for performance data on populations like yours, request a silent pilot on local data, and include the clinicians who'll actually use it in the evaluation, especially those who care for underserved patients.

</Pitfall>

## Your Clinical AI Practice Plan

Bring the course together into a one-page plan you'll actually use. Organize it by the four competencies.

### Delegation

- **Tasks I'll use AI for (Tier 1–2):** _______________
- **Tasks I'll use AI for only as a second look (Tier 3):** _______________
- **Tasks I won't delegate (Tier 4):** _______________
- **Approved tools I'm cleared to use with PHI:** _______________

### Description

- **My saved prompt templates** (no PHI): _______________
- **My default safety instructions:** e.g., "flag uncertainty," "use only provided text," "show calculations"

### Discernment

- **My scribe-note review routine** (top error category to check first): _______________
- **My rule for decision support:** e.g., "form my impression before viewing the score"
- **My evidence rule:** e.g., "never cite a source I haven't opened"

### Diligence

- **Where I report AI errors:** _______________
- **How I disclose AI use to patients:** _______________
- **My documentation habit when I override a tool:** _______________
- **Who I ask about AI policy:** _______________

## Staying Fluent

Clinical AI will change faster than any course. The competencies won't. Revisit your plan every six months, or whenever your organization introduces a new tool. Teach the habits to trainees and colleagues: the clinicians who know how AI fails are the ones best placed to make it safe.

1. Fill in every line of the practice plan above.
2. Revisit your tool inventory from Module 1. For each tool you couldn't answer questions about, apply at least three questions from the evaluation checklist, and find out the answers.
3. Share your plan with a colleague or your team and compare. Where do your Tier 4 lists differ? Discuss why.
4. Put a reminder on your calendar to review the plan in six months.
