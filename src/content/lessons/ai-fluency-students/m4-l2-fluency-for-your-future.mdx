## What Employers Actually Need

In most jobs you're likely to have, AI will be part of the work. That doesn't make human skill less important. It changes which skills matter most. Anyone can get AI to produce something. What's valuable is the person who:

- Knows **which tasks** AI should do and which need human judgment (**Delegation**)
- Can **explain clearly** what's needed, to AI and to people (**Description**)
- Can **tell when AI is wrong**, which requires actually knowing the subject (**Discernment**)
- Can be **trusted** to use AI honestly and protect sensitive information (**Diligence**)

Notice what all four have in common: **they depend on knowing things yourself.** You can't catch AI's mistake in a financial report if you don't understand finance. You can't judge a design if you've never learned design. The subject knowledge you build in school is what makes you useful in a world full of AI.

## The Skills You're Already Building

Everything in this course is practice for work:

| In school | At work |
|---|---|
| Asking whether AI helps or skips your learning | Deciding what to automate and what to own |
| Using AI as a tutor that quizzes you | Using AI to learn new tools and topics quickly on the job |
| Checking AI sources against your library | Verifying AI output before it goes to a client or boss |
| Keeping your voice in writing | Writing that sounds like a real, credible person |
| Writing an AI use statement | Following company AI policy and being transparent with colleagues |
| Agreeing on AI rules in group projects | Working on teams with shared AI norms |

When an interviewer asks "How do you use AI?", the strongest answer isn't a list of tools. It's an example of a time you used AI well, **including a time you caught it being wrong or decided not to use it.**

<Pitfall title="Skipping the Basics Because AI Can Do Them">

**Common mistake:** thinking "AI can do math, writing, and coding, so I don't need to learn them."

If you can't do the basics, you can't check AI's work, and someone who can will get the job. Foundational knowledge is what lets you supervise AI instead of depending on it.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Write Your Personal AI Learning Plan">

1. Write a short plan (half a page is fine) answering:
   - **Where AI helps my learning:** two or three specific uses you'll keep doing (for example, retrieval quizzes before tests).
   - **Where I don't use AI:** two or three things you'll protect for your own learning, and why.
   - **How I check AI:** your personal verification habits.
   - **How I stay honest:** your disclosure and record-keeping habits.
   - **One AI skill for my future:** something connected to a career you're interested in.
2. Ask an AI assistant to review your plan:
   ```text
   Here's my plan for using AI as a student. Don't rewrite it. Ask
   me two questions that would make it more specific, and point out
   anything that might conflict with typical school AI policies.
   ```
3. Revise it yourself. Keep it where you'll see it at the start of each term.

</TryIt>
