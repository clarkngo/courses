## Disclosure Is a Skill, Not a Confession

When your course allows AI and asks you to disclose it, think of disclosure the way a scientist thinks about a methods section: an honest description of **how the work was made**. It protects you, it helps your teacher understand your process, and it's a habit professionals increasingly need too.

A clear **AI use statement** answers four questions:

```text
AI use statement
- Tool(s) I used: [name]
- What I used it for: [e.g. brainstorming angles, quizzing myself
  on the reading, feedback on my outline]
- What I did myself: [e.g. chose the thesis, wrote all paragraphs,
  found and read all sources]
- How I checked the AI's output: [e.g. verified the two facts it
  suggested against my textbook]
```

If your teacher provides a different format, use theirs. If you're not sure whether a use needs disclosing, disclose it. Over-disclosing is never the problem.

---

## Keep a Record of Your Process

The best protection against a misunderstanding is **evidence of how your work developed**. It costs almost nothing to keep:

- **Write in tools with version history**, such as Google Docs or Microsoft Word with saved versions, so your drafts are timestamped.
- **Keep your notes, outlines, and brainstorming**, even messy ones.
- **Save AI chats** you used for an assignment, if the tool allows, or note what you asked.
- **Keep your sources:** links, PDFs, and notes on what each one says.

## If You're Ever Accused

Accusations of AI misuse happen, sometimes based on unreliable AI detectors that wrongly flag human writing, especially writing by people for whom English is an additional language. If it happens to you:

1. **Stay calm and respectful.** Your teacher may have a real concern, or may be relying on a tool that got it wrong.
2. **Ask what the concern is based on.** It's reasonable to ask what evidence they're looking at.
3. **Show your process:** version history, notes, outlines, drafts, sources, and any AI use statement.
4. **Offer to talk through your work.** Explaining your argument, your choices, and your sources is strong evidence that the thinking is yours.
5. **Know the process.** Your school has an academic integrity policy that describes your rights, including how to appeal. Read it, and ask an advisor or student advocate for help if needed.

And if you *did* use AI in a way that wasn't allowed: being honest, early, is almost always better than defending something you can't explain. Many teachers treat an honest admission very differently from a denial.

<Pitfall title="Deleting the Evidence">

**Common mistake:** writing in a fresh document each time, deleting outlines and notes when done, and closing AI chats without saving anything.

If your work is ever questioned, you have nothing to show how you made it. Keeping your process takes no extra effort and can make all the difference.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Set Up Your Integrity Kit">

1. Pick a writing tool with version history for your next assignment. Find where you view previous versions.
2. Create a folder per course for notes, outlines, and sources.
3. Write an AI use statement for a recent assignment where you used AI (or a practice one), using the four-question format.
4. Find your school's academic integrity policy. Note one thing it says about AI, and one thing about how disputes are handled.
5. Practice: in two minutes, explain the main argument and evidence of a recent piece of your work out loud, as if your teacher asked.

</TryIt>
