## One Word, Three Very Different Things

"We're using AI" can mean almost anything in a life-science company. A protein-structure model, a chatbot that drafts a clinical study report section, and a vision system inspecting vials on a fill line are all "AI," but they fail differently, are regulated differently, and need different kinds of checking.

Before you decide how much to trust any tool, sort it into one of three groups.

### 1. Scientific and predictive models

Purpose-built models trained on scientific data to make a specific prediction:

- **Structure prediction** (for example, AlphaFold and its successors) estimates how a protein folds or how molecules bind.
- **Property prediction** scores candidate molecules for solubility, toxicity, or binding affinity.
- **Image analysis** counts cells, segments tissue, or flags anomalies in histology slides.
- **Operational prediction** forecasts trial enrollment, equipment failure, or batch yield.

These models usually output a number, a structure, or a classification, often with a confidence score. Their main risk is being used **outside the conditions they were trained and validated on**.

### 2. General-purpose generative assistants

Large language models (LLMs) such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, or Copilot, used to read, summarize, draft, translate, or write code. They're flexible and fluent, which is exactly the problem: they produce confident text whether or not it's true. Most of this course focuses here, because these are the tools most professionals now use daily, often without any formal process.

### 3. AI embedded in regulated systems

AI features built into validated systems: a quality-management system that suggests deviation root causes, a pharmacovigilance platform that auto-codes adverse events, an electronic data capture system that flags data anomalies, or a visual-inspection system on a manufacturing line. These sit inside **GxP** processes (Good Laboratory, Clinical, Manufacturing, and Pharmacovigilance Practice), so they carry validation, data-integrity, and audit-trail obligations that a stand-alone chatbot does not.

<Pitfall title="Assuming the Vendor Validated It for You">

**Common mistake:** "It's a validated system, so the new AI feature is validated too."

A vendor's release notes are not your validation. When AI is added to a GxP system, your organization still has to assess the feature against **your intended use**, document that assessment, and control changes to it. Ask your quality unit before turning on any new AI feature in a validated system.

</Pitfall>

## The Four Competencies

This course is organized around the **4D AI Fluency Framework**:

<LoopDiagram
  label="The four AI fluency competencies applied to life-science work"
  colors={['#047857', '#059669', '#10b981', '#065f46']}
  steps={[
    { title: "Delegation", subtitle: "what to hand to AI" },
    { title: "Description", subtitle: "how to ask well" },
    { title: "Discernment", subtitle: "how to check output" },
    { title: "Diligence", subtitle: "how to stay accountable" },
  ]}
/>

- **Delegation:** deciding which tasks AI should do, which it should assist with, and which it should stay out of, given the scientific, patient, and regulatory stakes.
- **Description:** giving AI the context, constraints, and format it needs, without giving it data it shouldn't have.
- **Discernment:** checking AI output against primary sources, raw data, and your own expertise.
- **Diligence:** owning the result. Documenting AI use where it matters, following SOPs, and being transparent with colleagues, regulators, journals, and patients.

The four competencies form a loop. What you learn while checking output (Discernment) changes what you delegate next time.
