## The Job in One Sentence

A Solutions Architect (SA) **decides how a system should be built** so that it meets the business's needs, and explains why. SAs rarely write most of the code. They decide which services to use, how the pieces connect, how the system fails and recovers, and what it will cost to run.

The title shows up in three common places:

| Where | Who the SA works for | Typical work |
|---|---|---|
| **AWS itself** | AWS customers, as part of the account team | Design reviews, proofs of concept, workshops, migration plans, Well-Architected reviews |
| **AWS partners and consultancies** | Clients on a project | Design and often build a workload, then hand it over |
| **In-house** | Their own company | Set standards, review designs from product teams, own the cloud architecture |

The skills are the same in all three. You listen for requirements, translate them into an architecture, and defend the trade-offs.

### Functional vs non-functional requirements

**Functional requirements** say what the system does: "users can upload a photo and share a link." **Non-functional requirements** say how well it must do it, and they drive almost every architecture decision:

- **Availability:** how much downtime is acceptable (99.9% allows about 8.8 hours a year; 99.99% allows about 53 minutes)
- **Recovery:** RTO (how long you can be down) and RPO (how much data you can lose)
- **Performance:** latency targets, peak requests per second, data volume
- **Security and compliance:** data residency, encryption, audit requirements, who may access what
- **Cost:** a monthly budget, or a target cost per transaction
- **Operations:** the team's skills, and how much infrastructure they're willing to manage

Each requirement pushes the design in a specific direction:

## Everything Is a Trade-Off

There is almost never a single right architecture. There is the one that best fits **these** requirements. Common tensions:

- **Availability vs cost.** Multi-Region active/active is very resilient and very expensive.
- **Managed vs control.** Aurora removes database operations work; self-managed databases on EC2 give you every knob.
- **Consistency vs latency.** Global users want local reads; strongly consistent writes need coordination.
- **Speed of delivery vs long-term fit.** A quick lift-and-shift ships this quarter; a rearchitecture pays off later.

The **AWS Well-Architected Framework** gives you a checklist for these conversations. It has six pillars: **Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, and Sustainability**. The SAA-C03 exam focuses on four of them: security, reliability (resilience), performance, and cost.

### Write the decision down

Good SAs record important choices in an **architecture decision record (ADR)**: a short note with the context, the options considered, the decision, and its consequences. Six months later, when someone asks "why didn't we use DynamoDB?", the answer is on the page.

<Pitfall title="Starting From the Service">

**Common mistake:** an architect opens with "let's use Kubernetes and DynamoDB" before asking how many users there are, what the data looks like, or who will run it.

Service-first designs fit the architect's favorite tools, not the problem. Ask about requirements and constraints **first**. If you can't say which requirement a component satisfies, you probably don't need it.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

A Solutions Architect turns **requirements into design choices** and can explain each one. Start from the business, pin down the **non-functional requirements** (availability, RPO/RTO, compliance, performance, cost, operations), compare options, and **write down why** you chose one.

<TryIt title="Requirements Interview">

A friend runs an online bakery. Today it's a single WordPress server on one EC2 instance. They want to "move it to the cloud properly."

Write **eight questions** you'd ask before drawing anything. Cover availability, data loss, traffic patterns, compliance (they take card payments), budget, and who will maintain it.

Then, for two of your questions, write how two different answers would change the design. For example: "If they can tolerate a day of downtime, a single instance with nightly backups is fine. If an outage during the holiday rush costs them $10,000 an hour, they need Multi-AZ."

</TryIt>
