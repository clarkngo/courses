## So What Is AI?

**AI** stands for **artificial intelligence**. It's a broad name for computer programs that do things we usually think need a human mind: understanding speech, recognizing faces in photos, or writing a sentence.

The kind of AI everyone is talking about right now is the **AI assistant** (sometimes called a *chatbot*). Examples include **ChatGPT**, **Google Gemini**, **Microsoft Copilot**, and **Claude**. You type or speak a question in ordinary words, and it answers in ordinary words, like a conversation.

### A helpful way to picture it

Imagine a very well-read helper who has skimmed an enormous library: books, websites, recipes, manuals, letters. This helper never met you, can't see your home, and doesn't remember the library perfectly. But they are very good at **putting words together in a way that sounds right**, based on everything they've read.

That's close to how an AI assistant works. When you ask a question, it doesn't look up a single "correct" answer in a filing cabinet. It **predicts** what a good, helpful reply would probably say, one word at a time.

This explains both its strengths and its weaknesses:

| What AI is good at | Where AI can stumble |
|---|---|
| Explaining things in simple words | Getting specific facts wrong (dates, phone numbers, prices) |
| Drafting letters, cards, and emails | Knowing what happened this week, unless it can search the web |
| Summarizing long or confusing text | Knowing anything about *you* that you haven't told it |
| Answering "how do I…" questions | Admitting when it doesn't know. It may guess instead |
| Being patient: it never sighs or gets tired | Giving advice that only a doctor, lawyer, or banker should give |

## AI Can Sound Sure and Still Be Wrong

This is the single most important idea in the whole course, so it gets its own heading.

Because an AI assistant is built to produce answers that *sound* right, it can state something false in a calm, confident, well-written way. People call this **"making things up"** or a **"hallucination."** It isn't lying on purpose. It just doesn't have a built-in sense of when it's on shaky ground.

That means:

- **Use AI as a helpful first draft, not the final word.**
- **Double-check anything important:** medicine, money, legal matters, phone numbers, addresses, and dates.
- **Trust your own experience.** If an answer doesn't match what you know, you're probably right to doubt it.

<Pitfall title="Believing It Because It Sounds Professional">

**Common mistake:** trusting an answer because it's well-written and confident.

Good grammar is not the same as good information. An AI assistant can give you a phone number for "Medicare customer service" that looks perfectly official and is simply wrong. For anything that matters, confirm it from a trusted source: the official website, the back of your card, your doctor's office, or a person you know.

</Pitfall>

## What AI Is Not

A few common worries, answered plainly:

- **It is not a person.** It has no feelings, even when it sounds warm and friendly. That's fine, but it's worth remembering.
- **It is not watching you.** An AI assistant only sees what you type, say, or share with it. (We'll cover privacy carefully in Module 5.)
- **It is not always right**, as you just learned.
- **It is not just for young people.** Many older adults find AI especially useful because it's patient, available any time, and happy to explain the same thing three different ways.
