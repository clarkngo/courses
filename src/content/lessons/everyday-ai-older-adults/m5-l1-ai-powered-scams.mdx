## Scammers Use AI Too

The same technology that writes your thank-you notes can be used by criminals. Scams targeting older adults are a serious and growing problem: according to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, people over 60 reported losing nearly **$5 billion** to fraud in 2024 alone, and many losses are never reported.

The good news: the **defenses are simple**, and they work no matter how convincing the scam is. You don't need to out-smart the technology. You just need a few habits.

---

## Three AI Scams to Know

### 1. The voice-clone "family emergency" call

AI can now copy a person's voice from a short clip, such as a video posted on social media. Scammers use this to call you **sounding exactly like your grandchild, child, or spouse**:

> *"Grandma, it's me. I'm in trouble. I was in a car accident and I'm at the police station. Please don't tell Mom. I need $3,000 for bail right now…"*

Sometimes a second voice comes on claiming to be a lawyer or police officer. The details feel real, the panic feels real, and the voice sounds real.

### 2. Deepfake videos and celebrity endorsements

AI can create fake videos of famous people, news anchors, or even government officials "recommending" an investment, a miracle health product, or a giveaway. If a celebrity is urging you to buy cryptocurrency or a supplement in a video ad, assume it's fake.

### 3. Perfectly written phishing messages

Scam emails and texts used to be easy to spot because of bad spelling and odd grammar. AI now writes them **flawlessly**, in a polished, official-sounding voice, and can even include details about you found online. Typos are no longer a reliable warning sign.

---

## The Warning Signs Haven't Changed

The technology is new, but the scam playbook is old. Watch for these **red flags**, however real the voice or message seems:

| Red flag | Example |
|---|---|
| **Urgency** | "You must act in the next hour." |
| **Secrecy** | "Don't tell anyone. Don't tell Mom." |
| **Strange payment** | Gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, cash couriers, payment apps |
| **Fear or excitement** | "You'll be arrested" or "You've won a prize" |
| **Unexpected contact** | A call, text, or pop-up you didn't ask for |

**Any real emergency will still be real in ten minutes.** Scammers rely on you acting before you can think.

### Set up a family safe word

This is the single best defense against voice-clone scams. Agree on a **secret word or phrase** with your close family: something unusual that wouldn't appear online. ("Purple pancakes," a childhood pet's nickname, a silly inside joke.) If someone calls in an "emergency," ask for the safe word. A scammer won't know it, no matter how perfect the voice.

Tell family members to **never post the safe word anywhere** and to use it themselves if they ever truly need help.

<Pitfall title="Thinking 'I'd Recognize My Own Grandchild's Voice'">

**Common mistake:** believing a voice clone couldn't fool you.

Modern voice cloning can be very convincing, especially over a phone line and when you're frightened. Smart, careful people are fooled every day. That's why the defense is not "listen carefully." It's **hang up and call them back on their known number**, or **ask for the safe word**. Those steps work regardless of how good the fake is.

</Pitfall>

## If You Think You've Been Scammed

It's not your fault, and acting quickly helps:

1. **Call your bank or card company** using the number on your card. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment.
2. **Change passwords** for any account you may have shared.
3. **Report it.** In the US, report to the FBI at **ic3.gov** and the FTC at **ReportFraud.ftc.gov**. The **AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline (877-908-3360)** offers free support to anyone, members or not.
4. **Tell someone you trust.** Scammers often target the same person again, and talking it through helps.

<TryIt title="Create Your Family Safe Word">

1. Call or meet with the family members you'd most likely help in an emergency.
2. Agree on a safe word or phrase together. Make it unusual and don't write it anywhere online.
3. Practice once: have someone pretend to call with an "emergency," and you ask for the word.
4. Write the AARP helpline number (877-908-3360) somewhere near your phone.

</TryIt>
