## You're Not Starting From Zero

If AI feels brand new, here's some good news: you've almost certainly been using it for years. It just wasn't called "AI" on the box.

| Where | The AI part | What it does for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Your phone's keyboard** | Word suggestions and autocorrect | Guesses the next word you're likely to type |
| **Voice helpers** (Siri, Google Assistant, Alexa) | Speech recognition | Turns your spoken words into text and actions |
| **Email** | Spam filter | Moves junk and many scam emails out of your inbox |
| **TV and streaming** (Netflix, YouTube) | Recommendations | Suggests shows based on what you've watched |
| **Photos app** | Face and object recognition | Groups photos of the same person, or finds "beach" pictures |
| **Maps** | Traffic prediction | Estimates how long a drive will take right now |
| **Bank and credit card** | Fraud detection | Flags a purchase that doesn't look like you |
| **Hearing aids and phones** | Live captions and noise reduction | Turns speech into on-screen words; reduces background noise |

Each of these is a narrow tool that does one job. You never had to "learn AI" to benefit from them.

## What's Different About the New AI Assistants?

The older voice helpers were good at **short commands**: "Set a timer for 10 minutes." "What's the weather?" "Call Susan." Ask anything more complicated and they'd often say, "Here's what I found on the web."

The new **AI assistants** can handle a real back-and-forth conversation:

- **Old voice helper:** "What's a good recipe for chicken?" → reads one web result.
- **New AI assistant:** "I have chicken thighs, a can of tomatoes, and some rice. I'm cooking for one and I'm watching my salt. What could I make?" → suggests a recipe built around exactly what you said, and will adjust it if you reply "I don't have an oven, just a stovetop."

The difference is that you can **talk to it like you'd talk to a knowledgeable friend**, with all the details of your situation, and it can **follow up** on what you said earlier in the conversation.

Many voice helpers are being upgraded with this newer kind of AI, so the line between "old" and "new" is blurring. The skills in this course work with both.

## Where Can You Find an AI Assistant?

You can reach the main AI assistants in three ways:

1. **An app on your phone or tablet.** Search the App Store (iPhone/iPad) or Play Store (Android) for the assistant's name. Look carefully at who made it (more on fakes below).
2. **A website on your computer.** Type the address into your web browser.
3. **Already built in.** Some phones, Windows computers, and email programs now include an AI assistant. You may see a small sparkle icon ✨ or a button labeled "Ask AI," "Copilot," or "Gemini."

You'll set one up step by step in the next module.

<Pitfall title="Downloading a Copycat App">

**Common mistake:** installing the first app that shows up when you search "AI chat."

App stores contain many look-alike apps that charge expensive weekly subscriptions or collect your information. Before installing, check that the **developer name** matches the real company: **OpenAI** (ChatGPT), **Google** (Gemini), **Microsoft** (Copilot), or **Anthropic** (Claude). The real versions have free options. If an app asks for payment before you've even tried it, close it.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Find the AI You Already Have">

1. Look at your list from the last lesson. Match each item to a row in the table above.
2. On your phone, try the voice helper if you have one. Hold the home or side button, or say "Hey Siri" or "Hey Google." Ask: *"What's the weather tomorrow?"*
3. Now ask it something more conversational: *"I'm going for a walk tomorrow morning. Should I bring a jacket?"*
4. Notice how it handles the second question. Does it give you a real answer or just read a web result? Keep that in mind for the next module.
