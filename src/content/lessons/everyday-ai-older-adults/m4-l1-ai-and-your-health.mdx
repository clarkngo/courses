## A Helper, Never Your Doctor

Health is one of the most valuable ways to use AI, and one of the places where caution matters most. The rule for this whole lesson is simple:

> **AI can help you understand and prepare. Your doctor, nurse, and pharmacist make the decisions with you.**

**In an emergency, call 911 (or your local emergency number). Don't type symptoms into an AI.** Chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness on one side, confusion, or severe bleeding need help right now.

---

## Where AI Helps

### 1. Preparing for an appointment

Doctor visits are short, and it's easy to forget a question the moment you walk in. AI can help you get organized:

> *"I have a check-up with my doctor next week. I've been more tired than usual for about a month and my ankles swell in the evening. Help me write a short list of what to tell the doctor and questions to ask. Keep it to one page."*

Print it or keep it on your phone, and hand it to your doctor. Most doctors appreciate an organized patient.

### 2. Understanding what you were told

After an appointment, or when results appear in your patient portal:

> *"My doctor said I have 'mild osteopenia.' What does that mean in simple words, and what questions should I ask at my next visit?"*

> *"My blood test shows an A1C of 6.1%. In general terms, what does A1C measure?"*

AI can explain **general** meanings well. It doesn't know your full history, and normal ranges can vary by lab and by person, so let your care team tell you what **your** results mean for **you**.

### 3. Organizing medications

> *"Help me make a simple chart for my medications: name, dose, what time I take it, and what it's for. I'll fill in the details. Make it easy to print in large type."*

Bring that chart to every appointment and pharmacy visit.

### 4. Turning instructions into steps

> *"My discharge papers say to 'elevate the extremity and apply ice 20 minutes on, 20 off.' Can you explain that as simple step-by-step instructions?"*

**Never let AI:**

- **Tell you to start, stop, or change a medication or dose.** Always ask your doctor or pharmacist.
- **Diagnose you.** It may give you a list of possibilities, which can be useful to bring to your doctor, but it can't examine you and it can be wrong.
- **Talk you out of seeing a doctor.** If you're worried enough to ask, that's a good reason to call your doctor's office or nurse line.

<Pitfall title="Checking Drug Interactions With AI Alone">

**Common mistake:** asking an AI whether two medications are safe together and treating the answer as final.

AI may miss interactions, especially with supplements or recently approved drugs, and it doesn't know your kidney function, allergies, or other medications. **Your pharmacist** checks interactions as part of their job, usually for free. Call or stop by and ask. That's what they're there for.

</Pitfall>

## Keep Your Health Information Private

Health details are some of the most sensitive information you have. A few habits:

- **Describe, don't identify.** "A 72-year-old woman with high blood pressure" gives the AI enough context without your name, birthday, or Medicare number.
- **Don't upload your entire medical record** to a general AI assistant. Share only the part you need explained, with your name and ID numbers covered.
- **Use your patient portal's built-in tools** if your health system offers them. They're covered by health privacy rules in ways general AI apps may not be.

<TryIt title="Prepare for Your Next Appointment">

1. Think of your next doctor's visit (or a recent one).
2. Ask your AI assistant to help you build a one-page list: *"what's changed since my last visit," "my current medications" (you fill in the names), and "questions to ask."*
3. Ask: *"What's one question people often forget to ask at a visit like this?"*
4. Print it or save it to your phone, and bring it to your appointment.

</TryIt>
