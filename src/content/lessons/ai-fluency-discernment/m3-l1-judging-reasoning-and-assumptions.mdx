## Right Answer, Wrong Reasons

AI increasingly gives you more than facts: recommendations, comparisons, plans, and explanations of *why*. These can't be checked against a single source, because they're arguments. To judge an argument, you look at its parts:

- **Premises:** what it starts from (facts and assumptions)
- **Steps:** how it gets from one point to the next
- **Conclusion:** where it lands

A conclusion is only as strong as its weakest premise and its weakest step. A recommendation built on a wrong assumption about *your* situation can be perfectly logical and still wrong for you.

---

## Surface the Hidden Assumptions

Most of the risk sits in what the AI **assumed without saying**. When you didn't give it some piece of context, it filled the gap with something typical. Make those gaps visible:

```text
Before I act on this recommendation, list every assumption you made
about my situation, budget, timeline, constraints, and goals. Mark
which ones came from what I told you and which ones you filled in.
```

Then check each filled-in assumption against reality. A typical result: the AI assumed you have a dedicated budget, a team of several people, and six months. You have none of those, so the recommendation needs to change.

## Common Reasoning Failures

| Failure | What it looks like | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Leap** | A step that doesn't follow from the one before | "How exactly does B follow from A?" |
| **Cherry-picking** | Only evidence for one side appears | "What's the strongest evidence against this?" |
| **Uneven comparison** | Options judged on different criteria, or one option's costs ignored | "Apply the same criteria to every option in a table." |
| **Correlation as cause** | Two things happen together, so one must cause the other | "What else could explain this pattern?" |
| **False choice** | Only two options presented when more exist | "What options are missing?" |
| **Generic fit** | Advice that's right in general but ignores your specifics | "What about my situation would change this?" |

## Stress-Test the Argument

Three tests use the AI to generate material for *your* judgment:

1. **Counter-argument test.** Ask for the strongest case *against* the recommendation, in a new chat if the first conversation has been agreeable. If the counter-case is strong and the original ignored it, the reasoning was one-sided.
2. **Premise-change test.** Change one assumption and see if the conclusion moves. *"If our budget were half that, would you still recommend this?"* A conclusion that doesn't respond to important changes wasn't really based on them.
3. **Explain-it-back test.** Summarize the argument in your own words, in three steps. If you can't, either the reasoning has a gap or you don't yet understand it well enough to rely on it.

<Pitfall title="Treating Shown Reasoning as a Window Into the Model">

**Common mistake:** assuming that a step-by-step explanation is an accurate record of how the AI reached its answer.

Explanations, including the "thinking" some tools display, are useful because they give you something to inspect. But a model can produce a tidy explanation for an answer it reached some other way, and a convincing explanation can accompany a wrong conclusion. Judge the explanation as an argument on its own merits: do the steps hold up when you check them?

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Stress-Test an AI Recommendation">

1. Ask an AI assistant for advice on a real decision with trade-offs, giving only brief context:
   ```text
   I'm deciding between [option A] and [option B] for [a real
   decision, e.g. "two project management tools for my 6-person
   team" or "taking an evening course vs. an online certificate"].
   Which should I choose and why?
   ```
2. **Assumptions:** Use the assumptions prompt above. Mark each filled-in assumption ✅ true for me or ❌ not true for me.
3. **Counter-argument:** In a new chat, ask for the strongest case for the option it didn't recommend.
4. **Premise change:** Back in the first chat, correct the ❌ assumptions. Does the recommendation change?
5. **Explain it back:** Write the final reasoning in three sentences of your own. Would you defend it to a skeptical colleague?

</TryIt>
