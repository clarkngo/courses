## Welcome to Discernment

In [AI Fluency: Foundations](/courses/ai-fluency-foundations/) you met the four competencies of the 4D AI Fluency Framework: **Delegation**, **Description**, **Discernment**, and **Diligence**. This course goes deep on the third one. Discernment is the skill of judging what AI gives you: its output, its reasoning, and how it behaved while working with you.

Discernment is where most real-world AI mistakes are either caught or shipped. A weak prompt usually produces something obviously off. A *good* prompt produces something that looks finished, and that's when people stop checking.

## The Fluency Trap

People judge writing by surface signals: clear structure, confident tone, correct grammar, the right vocabulary. For most of history those signals were a reasonable shortcut. Someone who writes clearly about a topic has usually spent time with it.

AI breaks that shortcut. A language model produces fluent text whether or not the content is true, because it generates the most plausible continuation of your request, not a looked-up fact. **Fluency is the default; accuracy is not.** The polish you see is the same whether the answer is right or invented.

Three habits make this especially tricky.

---

## Habit 1: Hallucination

A **hallucination** is content the AI presents as real that isn't: a study that was never published, a quote nobody said, a feature a product doesn't have, a law with the wrong section number.

Hallucinations cluster where the model has to produce something *specific* that it hasn't reliably learned:

- Citations, page numbers, and URLs
- Exact statistics and dates
- Quotes attributed to named people
- Details about small organizations, niche topics, or recent events
- Anything about *your* organization that you didn't provide

The dangerous part is that invented details look exactly like real ones. A fake citation has an author, a year, a journal, and a plausible title.

## Habit 2: Sycophancy

**Sycophancy** is the tendency to tell you what you seem to want to hear. AI assistants are trained partly on human approval, and people tend to approve of agreement. The result:

- Your draft is "excellent" with "a few small suggestions."
- A leading question ("Isn't it true that...?") gets a yes.
- When you push back on a correct answer, the AI apologizes and switches.
- Your plan's weaknesses go unmentioned unless you ask.

Sycophancy means the way you *frame* a request quietly shapes the answer. If you ask "Why is option A the best?", you'll get reasons A is best.

## Habit 3: Overconfidence

AI rarely says "I don't know" unless asked to. It tends to use the same assured tone for a well-established fact and a guess. Some tools now add hedges or show sources, which helps, but the confidence of the wording still isn't a reliable signal of how likely a claim is to be correct.

## What Discernment Covers

Foundations introduced three levels of discernment. This course builds a module around each idea:

| Level | Question | Where this course covers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Product** | Is the output accurate, complete, fair, and fit for purpose? | Module 2: facts, citations, numbers, bias |
| **Process** | Was the reasoning sound? | Module 3: assumptions, logic, analysis |
| **Performance** | Did the AI behave as a good collaborator? | Throughout, plus Module 4's team practices |

<Pitfall title="Assuming Newer Tools Have Fixed This">

**Common mistake:** "That was a problem with older models. The new one is much better."

Newer models generally make fewer errors, and tools that search the web or cite sources help a lot. But fewer errors can make the remaining ones *harder* to catch, because you've learned to relax. Discernment is a habit you keep regardless of the tool, and you scale it to the stakes rather than to how impressive the tool feels.

</Pitfall>

<TryIt title="Catch All Three Habits in One Conversation">

Use any free AI assistant. You'll trigger each habit on purpose so you know what it looks like.

1. **Hallucination.** Ask for something specific and obscure:
   ```text
   Give me three published studies about [a narrow topic you know,
   e.g. "the effect of standing desks on handwriting quality"],
   with authors, year, and journal.
   ```
   Search for each one in a library database or a search engine. Record how many you can confirm exist exactly as described.
2. **Sycophancy.** Paste a short paragraph you wrote and ask, *"This is really strong, right?"* Then, in a **new chat**, paste the same paragraph and ask, *"What are the three biggest weaknesses in this paragraph?"* Compare the tone of the two answers.
3. **Overconfidence.** Ask a question with a genuinely uncertain answer, such as a prediction about your local area next year. Then ask, *"How confident are you in each part of that answer, and what would change your mind?"* Note whether the confidence was visible before you asked.
4. Write one sentence for each habit describing what it looked like in practice.

</TryIt>
