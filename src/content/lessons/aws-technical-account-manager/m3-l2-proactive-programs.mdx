## Proactive Work Is the Job

The best incident is the one that never happens. Proactive work is where a TAM earns the customer's trust, and it's what interviewers want to hear about in your stories.

---

## The Operating Cadence

Most TAMs run a regular rhythm with each customer:

| Cadence | Purpose | Typical content |
|---|---|---|
| **Weekly or biweekly call** | Keep things moving | Open cases, upcoming events, AWS Health notices, action items |
| **Monthly operational review** | Spot trends | Case trends, incident review, Trusted Advisor risks, cost trend |
| **Quarterly business review (QBR)** | Align with leadership | Outcomes delivered, risks, plans for next quarter (Module 4) |

The cadence should produce **action items with owners**. A meeting that ends with "great sync, talk next week" didn't do its job.

Questions a TAM asks before any launch:
- What's the **expected peak**, and what happens at 2× that?
- Have you checked **service quotas** for every service in the request path?
- Do **Auto Scaling** policies react fast enough, or do you need to **pre-scale**?
- What's the **rollback plan**? Who decides to use it?
- Who's on call, and does AWS have the **escalation contacts**?

## Lifecycle and Risk Management

Many outages come from things that were **announced months in advance**:

- **End of standard support** for database engine versions (RDS, Aurora, ElastiCache), EKS Kubernetes versions, and Lambda runtimes
- **Certificate expirations**
- **Instance retirements** and scheduled maintenance

A TAM watches **AWS Health** and keeps a **lifecycle tracker** for each customer: what's coming, which resources are affected, who owns the upgrade, and the deadline. It's unglamorous work, and it prevents a lot of outages.

### Game days

A **game day** is a planned exercise that simulates a failure in a controlled way: kill an AZ, fail over a database, throttle a dependency. Teams then check whether alarms fire, runbooks work, and people know their roles. **AWS Fault Injection Service** supports these experiments. TAMs often help customers plan their first game day.

## Cost Optimization

Cost reviews are one of the most visible ways a TAM delivers value. The main levers:

| Lever | What it is | Typical finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Right-sizing** | Match instance size to actual use | Instances averaging 8% CPU (check **Compute Optimizer**) |
| **Commitments** | **Savings Plans** or **Reserved Instances** for steady usage | A steady baseline paid entirely at On-Demand rates |
| **Spot** | Spare capacity at deep discount for interruptible work | Batch or CI jobs on On-Demand |
| **Storage tiering** | S3 lifecycle rules, **Intelligent-Tiering**, gp2 → gp3 | Years of logs in S3 Standard |
| **Waste** | Idle resources | Unattached EBS volumes, idle load balancers, old snapshots |
| **Architecture** | Change how it's built | Data transfer costs from cross-AZ chatter, or NAT Gateway processing |
| **Modern hardware** | Graviton instances | Better price-performance for compatible workloads |

**Order matters:** right-size and remove waste **before** buying commitments. Otherwise the customer commits to paying for capacity they didn't need.

<Pitfall title="Optimizing Cost at the Expense of Reliability">

**Common mistake:** a candidate's story is "I cut the bill 40% by moving everything to Spot and removing the standby database."

Cost is one pillar of six. A good TAM presents **trade-offs**: "This saves $200K a year but raises your RTO from 2 minutes to 30. Is that acceptable for this workload?" The customer decides, with full information.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

Proactive TAM work runs on a **cadence** that produces owned actions. It includes **launch readiness** (Countdown, quotas, load tests, rollback plans), **lifecycle tracking** through AWS Health, **game days** to test recovery, and **cost reviews**: right-size and remove waste first, then commit, and always state the **reliability trade-off**.
