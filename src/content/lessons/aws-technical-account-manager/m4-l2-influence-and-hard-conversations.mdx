## You Own Almost Nothing

A TAM doesn't own the customer's engineers, the customer's systems, the AWS support engineers, or the AWS service teams. Yet the TAM is accountable for outcomes that need all of them. That's **influence without authority**, and interviewers will probe it directly: "Tell me about a time you got someone to do something they didn't have to do."

Influence comes from three things:

- **Credibility**: you're technically right, and you've been right before
- **Data**: you bring evidence, not opinions
- **Relationships**: people help you because you've helped them and you're easy to work with

---

## Advocating Inside AWS

TAMs are the customer's voice inside AWS. When a customer needs something AWS doesn't do, the TAM files a **product feature request (PFR)** with the service team. Strong PFRs:

- Describe the **customer problem**, not just the requested feature
- Quantify **impact**: revenue at risk, workload size, how many customers ask for it
- Include the **current workaround** and why it hurts
- Get **aggregated** with other customers' requests, since service teams prioritize by demand

Be honest with the customer: a PFR is **not a promise**. Set expectations, offer the best workaround now, and follow up.

## Prioritizing Across Customers

TAMs often support several customers, and everything feels urgent. A simple order:

1. **Active business-critical impact** (outage, security event)
2. **Time-bound risk** (a launch next week, an end-of-life deadline)
3. **High-value proactive work** (the review that prevents the next Sev-1)
4. **Everything else**, scheduled and communicated

When you can't do something, say so early, explain why, and offer an alternative or another person. Customers forgive "not this week, here's when." They don't forgive silence.

### The angry executive after an AWS-side event

Don't get defensive and don't minimize. Acknowledge the business impact in **their** terms. Share what you know, and commit to a detailed explanation by a specific time. Then turn it into resilience: "Here's how we can make sure a similar event in one AZ doesn't affect you next time."

### Telling a customer their architecture is risky

They may have built it themselves and be proud of it. Lead with their goal ("you told me checkout uptime is your top metric"), show the risk with data, offer options, and **respect their decision**. If they accept the risk, document it. That's **Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit**.

### Saying no to the customer

Sometimes the customer asks for something a TAM can't or shouldn't do: write their code, guarantee a feature date, bypass a security control. Say no clearly, explain why, and offer what you *can* do. A clear no keeps trust. A vague maybe destroys it.

<Pitfall title="Siding Against Your Own Company to Win Favor">

**Common mistake:** "I told the customer AWS support was terrible and I'd personally make sure it got fixed."

Advocating for the customer doesn't mean trashing your colleagues. Own the problem ("We didn't meet the bar here"), fix it through the right channels, and keep both relationships intact. Interviewers listen for this balance.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

A TAM drives outcomes through **credibility, data, and relationships**, not authority. Advocate inside AWS with **quantified feature requests**, prioritize by **business impact and deadlines**, and handle hard conversations by **listening, stating facts, owning mistakes, offering options, and following through**. Say no clearly, and disagree with respect.

<TryIt title="Role-Play the Angry Call">

Have a friend play the customer's CTO, or write out both sides:

*"Your Region had a networking event yesterday. Our app was down for 47 minutes during our biggest sales day of the year. I'm paying for Enterprise Support. Why didn't you warn us? Why should we stay on AWS?"*

Respond in under 3 minutes. Check: Did you acknowledge the impact before explaining? Did you avoid making promises you can't keep? Did you end with a specific next step and time?

</TryIt>
