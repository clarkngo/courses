## From Support Contact to Trusted Advisor

A customer *trusts* a TAM when they call the TAM **before** making a big decision, not just after something breaks. That shift doesn't come from technical skill alone. It comes from understanding the customer's **business**, knowing their **people**, and consistently doing what you said you'd do.

---

## Know the Business First

Before you can recommend anything, you need answers to:

- How does this customer **make money**? Which workloads, if they went down, would stop that?
- What are leadership's **goals this year**? Growth, cost cutting, a new market, a compliance deadline?
- What does **"good" look like** to them? Uptime? Release speed? Unit cost per transaction?
- Where are they in their **cloud journey**? Migrating, optimizing, modernizing?

Read their annual report or investor materials if they're public. Ask the AM what the commercial relationship looks like. Then **tie every recommendation to one of these goals**.

## Map the Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Usually cares about | How the TAM helps |
|---|---|---|
| **CIO / CTO** | Strategy, risk, cost, speed | Outcome summaries; strategic risks |
| **VP / Director of Engineering** | Delivery and reliability | Launch readiness; incident reduction |
| **Platform / cloud team** | Landing zone, standards, tooling | Best practices; roadmap briefings |
| **SRE / operations** | On-call load, observability | Case escalations; COEs; game days |
| **Security / CISO** | Compliance, threat detection | Security reviews; Security Incident Response |
| **FinOps / finance** | Predictable, lower spend | Cost reviews; commitment planning |
| **Application teams** | Shipping features | Service guidance; workshops |

Identify your **champion** (who advocates for your work internally), the **decision makers**, and anyone **skeptical** of AWS. A TAM who only knows the platform team will be surprised when the CFO cuts the support budget.

## The Account Plan

The account team (AM, SA, TAM, and others) shares an **account plan**. The TAM usually owns the **operational and support** part of it, sometimes called a **success plan**:

1. **Customer goals** for the year, in their own words
2. **Critical workloads** and their owners
3. **Top risks** (from Well-Architected reviews, Trusted Advisor, incidents, lifecycle events), ranked
4. **Planned events** (launches, migrations, peaks)
5. **Initiatives** with measurable targets: "Reduce Sev-1/2 incidents by 30%," "Upgrade all Aurora clusters before end of standard support," "Cut compute cost 15%"
6. **Cadence**: who meets when

Write it **with** the customer, not **for** them. A plan the customer agreed to gets done.

## The Quarterly Business Review (QBR)

A QBR is a meeting with **customer leadership** to review the partnership. A strong QBR is short and outcome-focused:

1. **Their goals** (recap): what we agreed to work on
2. **Outcomes delivered**: numbers, not activities ("Sev-2 incidents down from 14 to 6," "$1.2M annualized savings")
3. **Operational health**: case trends, incident summary, open high risks
4. **What's next**: upcoming events, top recommendations, decisions needed from them
5. **Asks**: of the customer, and of AWS

<Pitfall title="The Activity Report">

**Common mistake:** the QBR opens with "This quarter we held 12 cadence calls, ran 3 workshops, and handled 147 cases."

Executives don't care how busy you were. They care what **changed for their business**. Lead with outcomes, then use activity only to explain how you got there. And keep the case-count chart to one slide.

</Pitfall>

## Translating Technical to Business

Practice turning technical findings into business language:

| Technical | Business |
|---|---|
| "Your RDS instance is single-AZ." | "If one data center has a problem, checkout is down until we restore from backup, around 2 hours, roughly $400K at peak." |
| "You have 340 gp2 volumes." | "Switching storage types saves about $90K a year with no downtime and no code changes." |
| "EKS 1.28 reaches end of standard support in March." | "Without an upgrade by March, you'll either pay extended-support fees or run on an unsupported version that blocks security patches." |

Each business version names the **impact**, the **size**, and the **choice**.

---

## Key Takeaway

Trust comes from knowing the customer's **business goals and people**, not just their architecture. Map **stakeholders**, co-write a **success plan** with measurable targets, and run **QBRs that lead with outcomes**. Translate every technical risk into **impact, size, and the decision needed**.

<TryIt title="Draft a One-Slide QBR Summary">

Pick a real system you've supported (or invent one). Draft a single slide with:

- **Goal**: one sentence in the customer's words
- **Three outcomes** with numbers
- **Top two risks**, each translated into business impact
- **One decision** you need from leadership this quarter

If an executive read only this slide, would they know what to do next?

</TryIt>
