## Getting Rid of Heat

Only part of the fuel's energy turns into shaft power. A large share leaves as **heat** in the exhaust and cooling water. The cooling system keeps metal temperatures in a safe, steady range: hot enough for efficient combustion, cool enough to protect liners, heads, and oil.

- **Fresh-water (jacket) circuit:** a **closed** loop of treated coolant circulates through the engine block, liners, and heads. It stays clean, so corrosion and scale are controlled.
- **Sea-water (raw water) circuit:** an **open** loop draws seawater through a **sea chest** and **strainer**, pumps it through the **heat exchanger** (and often oil and charge air coolers), and discharges it overboard. On many small vessels, it's injected into the **wet exhaust** to cool it.

### Keel Coolers and Box Coolers

Many workboats, tugs, and fishing vessels use **keel coolers**: tubes or channels mounted **outside the hull** below the waterline. Engine coolant flows through them and sheds heat directly to the surrounding sea. No raw water pump, strainer, or heat exchanger is needed for the jacket water, which means fewer clogging problems in silty or icy water. **Box coolers** do the same inside a recessed hull box.

Keel coolers lose efficiency when **fouled** with marine growth or painted with the wrong coating.

## Key Components

| Component | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Sea chest and sea valve** | Hull opening for seawater; valve must be operable (and is a critical watertight boundary) |
| **Sea strainer** | Catches debris, kelp, jellyfish; check and clean regularly |
| **Raw water pump** | Often a **flexible rubber impeller** pump on small engines; impellers wear and fail if run dry |
| **Heat exchanger** | Tube bundle; seawater in the tubes, coolant around them (or plate types) |
| **Thermostat** | Bypasses the cooler until the engine reaches operating temperature |
| **Expansion tank** | Allows for coolant expansion; check level here |
| **Sacrificial anodes (zincs)** | Corrode instead of engine metals; inspect and replace on schedule |

---

## Coolant Treatment

Fresh water coolant must be **treated** with corrosion inhibitors (and antifreeze where needed) per the engine maker. Test regularly for **inhibitor concentration**, **pH**, and **chlorides**. Rising chlorides suggest **seawater leaking** into the closed circuit through a heat exchanger failure.

---

## Overheating: A Quick Checklist

1. **Raw water flow:** Is water coming out of the exhaust or discharge? Is the sea valve open? Is the strainer clogged? Has the impeller failed?
2. **Coolant level:** Check the expansion tank (only when safe; never open a hot pressurized cap).
3. **Heat exchanger:** Fouled or plugged tubes?
4. **Thermostat:** Stuck closed?
5. **Load:** Overloaded engine, fouled hull, or wrong propeller?
6. **Keel cooler:** Fouling or air trapped in the circuit?

<Pitfall title="Running a Dry Impeller">

**Common mistake:** starting the engine with the sea valve closed after maintenance, or with a clogged strainer.

A flexible impeller running dry can be destroyed in **seconds**, and its broken vanes travel downstream to block the heat exchanger. Check the sea valve and strainer **before starting**, and confirm water flow immediately after. Carry **spare impellers** and know how to change one.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Most marine engines use a **closed fresh-water circuit** cooled by an **open sea-water circuit** through a **heat exchanger**, or by **keel coolers** outside the hull. Watch the **sea strainer**, **raw water impeller**, **anodes**, and **coolant chemistry**. When overheating, check **raw water flow first**.
