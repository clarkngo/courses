## Why Ships Run on Diesel

From a 40-foot crab boat to a container ship longer than three football fields, most of the world's commercial vessels are powered by **diesel engines**. Diesels are:

- **Efficient**: large marine diesels are among the most fuel-efficient engines ever built
- **Durable**: designed to run for tens of thousands of hours
- **Torque-rich**: ideal for turning propellers
- **Fuel-flexible**: able to burn a range of fuels, from marine gas oil to heavy fuel oil

---

## Compression Ignition

A gasoline engine uses a **spark plug** to ignite fuel. A diesel has no spark plug. It relies on **compression ignition**:

1. The piston compresses **air** to a small fraction of its original volume (compression ratios are often somewhere around **14:1 to 22:1**, depending on design).
2. Compressing air makes it **very hot**, hotter than the fuel's ignition temperature.
3. Fuel is **injected** as a fine mist into that hot air near the top of the stroke.
4. The fuel **ignites on its own**, and the expanding gases push the piston down.

Because ignition depends on hot, compressed air, anything that reduces compression, such as worn rings, leaking valves, or a cold engine, makes starting harder.

## The Four-Stroke Cycle

Most small and medium marine diesels are **four-stroke** engines. Each cylinder completes four piston strokes (two crankshaft revolutions) per power stroke.

## The Two-Stroke Cycle

Large ocean-going ships often use huge **two-stroke** engines. They produce a power stroke **every revolution**:

- Near the bottom of the stroke, **scavenge ports** in the cylinder liner open, and pressurized air from the turbocharger blows exhaust out through an **exhaust valve** in the head (this is called **uniflow scavenging**).
- The piston rises, compressing fresh air; fuel is injected and ignites near the top.

These big engines are often **crosshead** designs, where a crosshead and guides take the side load off the piston, allowing very long strokes.

## Main Engine Parts

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| **Cylinder block / frame** | Holds everything in alignment |
| **Cylinder liner** | Replaceable bore the piston runs in; surrounded by cooling water |
| **Piston and rings** | Seal compression; transfer force; scrape oil |
| **Connecting rod** | Links piston to crankshaft |
| **Crankshaft** | Converts up-and-down motion into rotation |
| **Cylinder head** | Holds intake and exhaust valves and the fuel injector |
| **Camshaft** | Times the valves (and on many engines, the fuel pumps) |
| **Injector** | Atomizes fuel into the cylinder |
| **Flywheel** | Smooths rotation; turning gear engages it for maintenance |

## Speed Classes

| Class | Typical speed | Typical design | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low-speed** | Below about 300 rpm | Two-stroke crosshead, direct drive to the propeller | Large cargo ships, tankers, container ships |
| **Medium-speed** | About 300–1,000 rpm | Four-stroke trunk piston, usually with a reduction gear | Ferries, cruise ships, tugs, generators |
| **High-speed** | Above about 1,000 rpm | Four-stroke, compact | Fishing boats, workboats, yachts, emergency generators |

Low-speed engines can turn the propeller directly. Faster engines need a **reduction gear** to bring speed down to an efficient propeller speed.

<Pitfall title="Cranking a Hard-Starting Engine Forever">

**Common mistake:** an engine won't fire, so someone keeps cranking until the batteries are dead or the air receivers are empty.

A diesel needs **air, compression, and fuel at the right time**. If it doesn't fire after a few attempts, stop and check: Is fuel reaching the injectors (air in lines, closed valve, clogged filter)? Is the air intake clear? Is the engine too cold (pre-heat)? Is starting air pressure or battery voltage high enough to turn it fast enough to build compression heat?

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

Diesels ignite fuel with the **heat of compression**, not a spark. **Four-stroke** engines fire every two revolutions; **two-stroke** engines fire every revolution. Low-speed two-strokes drive big ships directly, while medium- and high-speed four-strokes usually drive through a **reduction gear**. Starting needs **air, compression, and fuel at the right time**.
