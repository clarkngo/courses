## The Order Matters

OSHA 1910.147 lays out the application of energy control in a specific **sequence**. Doing the steps out of order creates gaps. For example, isolating before a normal shutdown can damage equipment or release energy violently.

## 1. Prepare

Before touching anything, the authorized employee must know:

- The **type and magnitude** of every energy source
- The **hazards** of that energy
- The **methods** to control it

Get the **machine-specific procedure**. Gather the right locks, tags, devices, test equipment, and blocks. If the job scope changed (for example, now you need to open a hydraulic line), make sure the procedure covers it.

## 2. Notify Affected Employees

Tell the operators and others in the area that the machine is being locked out, and why. This prevents surprises, like an operator trying to "help" by restarting.

## 3. Shut Down

Stop the machine using its **normal stopping procedure**: the stop button, the cycle-stop, or the control system. A normal stop brings moving parts to rest in a controlled way and avoids damage or added hazards from cutting power to a running machine.

## 4. Isolate

Operate **every** energy-isolating device so the machine is isolated from **all** sources: electrical disconnects, air valves, hydraulic valves, steam, process lines, and so on.

- Isolate at the **device**, not at controls.
- Watch for machines fed from **more than one** electrical source (main power, a separate control transformer feed, a UPS, heater circuits).

## 5. Apply Lockout/Tagout Devices

Each authorized employee applies **their own** lock (and tag, where used) to **each** isolating device:

- Lock holds the device in the **safe or OFF** position.
- Tag is attached at the same point, stating who, when, and why.
- Use hasps or group lockboxes when several people work on the job (Module 4).

## 6. Control Stored Energy

After isolation, release, restrain, or otherwise make safe all **stored and residual** energy:

- Bleed **air** and **hydraulic** pressure; drain or block accumulators
- **Discharge capacitors** (and wait for drives to discharge)
- **Block or lower** raised parts; install safety pins
- Release or block **spring** tension
- Let **flywheels** and rolls coast to a full stop
- **Cool** hot surfaces or allow time; vent and drain process lines

If stored energy could **re-accumulate** (for example, pressure building again), **keep checking** it until the job is done.

## 7. Verify Isolation

Before starting work, **verify** the machine is isolated and de-energized:

1. Make sure **everyone is clear** of the machine.
2. **Try to start** it with the normal controls (the "try-out").
3. **Test** electrical circuits for absence of voltage with a properly rated meter.
4. Check **gauges** read zero for air and hydraulics.
5. **Return the controls to OFF or neutral** after the try-out.

<Pitfall title="Leaving the Start Button On After Try-Out">

**Common mistake:** the tech presses Start during verification, nothing happens (good), and they go to work. The start selector is left in RUN.

When the locks come off later, the machine starts instantly, possibly while someone is still reaching in. Always **return controls to OFF or neutral** after the try-out.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

Follow the sequence: **prepare → notify → shut down → isolate → lock and tag → control stored energy → verify.** Isolate **every** source at the **device**, put **your own** lock on each, release or block **stored energy**, and **try to start** and **test** before work begins. Return controls to **OFF** after trying.

- A. Press Start to confirm nothing moves, then return the switch to OFF
- B. Tell the operator the machine is going down
- C. Bleed air at the dump valve and pin the raised sealing head
- D. Read the machine-specific procedure
- E. Apply a personal lock to the disconnect and the air dump valve
- F. Stop the machine with the cycle-stop button
- G. Turn the disconnect OFF and close the air dump valve

Answer: **D, B, F, G, E, C, A**
