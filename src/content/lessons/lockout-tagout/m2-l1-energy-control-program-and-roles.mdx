A binder of procedures nobody follows is not a program. All three pillars have to work together.

## Who Does What: The Three Roles

| Role | Who | What they must know |
|---|---|---|
| **Authorized employee** | The person who **locks or tags out** equipment to service it | Recognize hazardous energy sources, the type and magnitude of energy, and the methods to isolate and control it |
| **Affected employee** | Someone who **operates or uses** the equipment, or works in the area where servicing happens | The purpose and use of energy control procedures; **never** try to restart locked or tagged equipment |
| **Other employee** | Anyone whose work may be in an area where LOTO is used | The procedure exists, and they must **not** remove locks or restart equipment |

An affected employee **becomes** an authorized employee when they perform servicing that requires LOTO. They need that training first.

## Machine-Specific Procedures

General "how to lock out" instructions aren't enough for most equipment. OSHA requires documented procedures that clearly spell out, for the equipment involved:

1. The **intended use** of the procedure (which machine, which tasks)
2. Steps for **shutting down, isolating, blocking, and securing** the machine
3. Steps for **placing, removing, and transferring** lockout/tagout devices, and who is responsible
4. Requirements for **testing** the machine to **verify** the effectiveness of the lockout

### What Good Procedures Look Like

Most plants use one-page **visual procedures** posted at the machine:

- **Photos** of each isolation point, numbered
- **Energy type and magnitude** (e.g., "480 VAC," "90 psi air," "2,000 psi hydraulic accumulator")
- **Device location** and the **method** (e.g., "Turn handle OFF, apply lock")
- **Stored energy** steps (bleed, block, discharge)
- **Verification** method for each source
- Release steps

### When Documentation Isn't Required

OSHA's standard includes a narrow exception: a written procedure isn't needed for a specific machine if **all** of a list of conditions are met. These include that the machine has **no potential for stored or residual energy** after shutdown, has a **single energy source** that can be readily identified and isolated, isolation fully **de-energizes** it, it's locked by a **single lockout device** under the exclusive control of the authorized employee, and there have been **no prior accidents** involving unexpected activation during servicing. If **any** condition isn't met, write the procedure. Most industrial machines don't qualify.

## Training

| When | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Initial** | Before an employee performs or is affected by LOTO, appropriate to their role |
| **Retraining** | When job assignments change, machines or processes change and create new hazards, procedures change, or a periodic inspection (or other reason) shows deviations or gaps in knowledge |
| **Documentation** | The employer must **certify** that training was done, with names and dates |

If your plant uses **tags only** for some equipment, training must also cover the **limitations of tags** (Lesson 2.2).

<Pitfall title="The Procedure Written in the Office">

**Common mistake:** procedures are written from drawings by someone who never walked the machine.

The result: a missed second disconnect, a valve that's actually behind the machine, an accumulator nobody listed. Write and validate every procedure **at the machine**, with the people who service it, and **test** it by actually performing the lockout.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaway

An energy control program has **procedures, training, and periodic inspections**. **Authorized** employees apply locks; **affected** employees operate the equipment and must never restart it while locked out; **other** employees must leave it alone. Procedures must cover **shutdown, isolation, device placement and removal, stored energy, and verification**, and should be **visual** and **validated at the machine**.

<TryIt title="Assign the Roles">

Classify each person during a gearbox replacement on a palletizer:

1. The millwright who locks out the palletizer and removes the gearbox. *(Authorized.)*
2. The palletizer operator who is told the machine is down. *(Affected.)*
3. A forklift driver who passes through the area. *(Other.)*
4. The operator who later helps lock out to adjust a sensor. *(Becomes authorized. Must be trained as authorized first.)*
