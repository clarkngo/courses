## Energy Comes in Many Forms

Most people think of LOTO as "turning off the breaker." But electricity is only one form of hazardous energy. A single packaging machine may have five.

| Energy type | Examples | How it hurts |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrical** | Motors, heaters, control circuits, capacitors, VFDs | Shock, arc flash, burns, unexpected motion |
| **Mechanical (kinetic)** | Flywheels, coasting rolls, fans, saw blades | Caught-in, cuts, amputation |
| **Mechanical (potential)** | Compressed springs, tensioned belts, bent material | Sudden release, struck-by |
| **Hydraulic** | Pumps, cylinders, accumulators, pressurized lines | Crushing, fluid injection, falling loads |
| **Pneumatic** | Compressors, receivers, cylinders, trapped air | Unexpected motion, hose whip, flying debris |
| **Gravity** | Raised platens, forks, rams, counterweights, stored parts | Crushing from falling or lowering loads |
| **Chemical** | Process lines, tanks, reactive materials | Burns, toxic exposure, fire |
| **Thermal** | Steam, hot oil, heated rolls, cryogenics | Burns, scalds, frostbite |
| **Other** | Radiation, lasers, water pressure | Varies |

**Stored (residual) energy** deserves special attention. It remains after the main source is isolated. Module 3 covers releasing it in detail.

## What Counts as an Energy-Isolating Device?

OSHA defines an **energy-isolating device** as a mechanical device that **physically prevents** the transmission or release of energy. Examples:

| Is an energy-isolating device | Is NOT an energy-isolating device |
|---|---|
| Manually operated electrical **disconnect switch** | **Push buttons**, selector switches |
| Manually operated **circuit breaker** | **Control circuit** devices, PLC outputs |
| **Line valve** (manually operated) | **E-stop** buttons |
| **Block** (e.g., a mechanical block or pin) | **Interlock** switches on guards |
| **Slip blind** or **blank** in a pipe | **Software** "maintenance modes" |
| Any similar device that **physically** blocks energy | Anything that relies on logic or a signal |

### Disconnects

A good electrical disconnect:

- **Opens all ungrounded conductors**
- Can't be operated independently on one pole
- Has a **visible indication** of position
- Accepts a **lock** in the OFF position

**Don't** pull fuses or rely on a motor starter. A starter is a control device; its contacts can weld closed.

### Valves

Hydraulic, pneumatic, steam, and process line valves must be **lockable** in the closed position, using built-in lockout features or valve lockout covers. For pneumatic systems, a **3/2 dump valve** blocks supply **and** vents downstream air (see the Pneumatics course).

For hazardous process lines (chemicals, steam), isolation may require **double block and bleed** or a **blind** rather than one valve.

### Capable of Being Locked Out

When equipment is installed, replaced, or majorly repaired or renovated, OSHA requires energy-isolating devices **to be designed to accept a lockout device**. Older equipment that can't accept a lock may use tagout under strict conditions (Lesson 2.2), but the goal is always lockable isolation.

<Pitfall title="Locking the Wrong Disconnect">

**Common mistake:** a tech locks out the disconnect labeled "Conveyor 3," but the label is wrong. It actually feeds Conveyor 4.

Labels drift as equipment is added and rewired. This is exactly why **verification** (trying to start the machine and testing for absence of voltage) is a required step, and why machine-specific procedures should be **checked on the floor**, not just written in an office.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaway

Hazardous energy includes **electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravity, chemical, and thermal** forms, plus **stored** energy. Only **physical** devices such as **disconnects, breakers, line valves, blocks, and blinds** isolate energy. **Buttons, E-stops, interlocks, PLCs, and starters do not.** New and modified equipment must accept locks.
