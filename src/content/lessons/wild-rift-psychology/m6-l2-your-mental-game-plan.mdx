## From Knowing to Doing

You now have a toolkit from real psychology: motivation, reinforcement, tilt, emotion regulation, decision biases, attention, team dynamics, toxicity, identity, healthy play, and deliberate practice. Knowing all of that doesn't change anything until it becomes a **routine** you run without thinking.

Athletes in sports psychology use **pre-performance routines**: consistent sequences before a free throw, a serve, or a race. Research suggests routines can help performance by settling arousal, focusing attention, and cutting down on overthinking. This lesson builds yours across four time scales.

---

## 1. Pre-Game Check (30 seconds)

Before you tap **Play**, run a quick self-check. One version is **H.A.L.T.**, borrowed from recovery and wellbeing circles:

- **Hungry?**
- **Angry?** (or already frustrated from something else)
- **Lonely?** (playing to fill a gap you might fill another way)
- **Tired?**

If two or more are yes, consider a normal or ARAM instead of ranked, or doing something else first. That's **situation selection** from Module 2.

Then set **one process goal** for the game, from your deliberate-practice focus: "Minimap glance every last hit," "Ward river before 3:00."

---

## 2. In-Game Reset (during the death timer)

When something goes wrong (you die, a teammate flames, an objective is lost), run a short reset:

1. **Breathe:** one slow exhale, longer than the inhale.
2. **Release:** loosen your grip and shoulders.
3. **Label:** silently name it. "Frustrated."
4. **Refocus:** look at the minimap and find the **next** useful action: an objective timer, a wave to catch, a ward to place.

That covers response modulation, attention deployment, and reappraisal in about ten seconds.

If chat is a problem, **mute** first. Ask yourself whether the next 30 seconds would be better with or without it.

---

## 3. Post-Game Review (1–2 minutes)

Before you queue again:

- **Rate your tilt** 1–5. If it's 4 or 5, take a break. That's your stop rule.
- **Process goal:** did you stick to it? Yes, partly, no.
- **One decision:** name one good decision regardless of the outcome, and one decision you'd change.
- **Next game:** keep the same process goal or adjust.

Notice what's missing: no blaming teammates, no checking their profiles. Your review is about what **you** can control.

---

## 4. Weekly Reflection (10 minutes)

Once a week, step back:

- What did my process metric (CS, early deaths, vision) do this week?
- How many sessions did I end with my stop rule, and how many did I play past it?
- How did my sleep go on gaming nights?
- Did the game feel more **harmonious** or more **obsessive** this week?
- What's my focus for next week?

Over time this log becomes your own data. You'll start to see patterns, like "I tilt more on weeknights after 10 p.m." or "my early deaths dropped when I warded river," that no generic guide could tell you.

## Run It as an Experiment

Treat your plan as a **two-week experiment**, not a set of rules you have to obey perfectly.

1. **Week 1:** Use the plan. Don't change it. Just record what happens.
2. **Midpoint:** What part was easy? What part did you skip every time?
3. **Week 2:** Keep what helped, drop or simplify what didn't, and add one thing.

Expect to forget parts of it. Habit-formation research (for example, Phillippa Lally's study of everyday habits) found that new habits took on average around two months to become automatic, with a huge range between people and behaviors. Missing a day didn't ruin the process. Consistency over time matters more than perfection.

<Pitfall title="Making the Plan Too Big">

**Common mistake:** writing a 30-step routine you'll never follow. If your plan takes longer than a minute before each game, cut it down. A routine you actually use with three steps beats a perfect one you ignore. Start with the stop rule and the in-game reset; add the rest once those stick.

</Pitfall>

## Course Wrap-Up

Across this course you've seen that the psychology of Wild Rift players is just the psychology of people, under fast, competitive, anonymous, and highly rewarding conditions:

- We play because the game meets real **needs**, and reward schedules make stopping hard.
- **Tilt** is a predictable chain you can interrupt.
- Fast decisions are shaped by **biases** and limited **attention**.
- Strangers on a team fall into **attribution errors**, **loafing**, and **disinhibition**.
- **Rank** can become identity, and healthy play is about **control** and **harm**, not hours.
- Improvement comes from **deliberate practice** and a **routine** you run every game.

The same tools work outside the game, in exams, sports, work presentations, and any moment where pressure meets a decision. Wild Rift just gives you a lot of chances to practice.
