## When a Badge Becomes "Who I Am"

"I'm Diamond." "I'm hard stuck Gold." Notice the wording: not *my account is* Diamond, but *I am*. For many players, rank stops being a number about recent games and becomes part of their **identity**.

That shift changes how wins and losses feel. A loss isn't just a loss; it's a threat to who you are.

---

## Contingent Self-Worth

Jennifer Crocker's research on **contingencies of self-worth** found that people base their self-esteem on different domains: grades, appearance, approval, competition. When your self-worth is **contingent** on a domain, success there brings a spike of good feeling, and failure brings a sharper drop. Self-esteem becomes unstable, tied to the latest result.

If your self-worth is contingent on rank:

- Climbing feels euphoric, but briefly.
- Demotion feels like a personal failure.
- You may avoid playing ranked at all to "protect" your rank.
- Other people's ranks feel like judgments of you.

None of this means you shouldn't care about rank. Caring is fine. The problem is when rank is the **main** pillar holding up how you feel about yourself.

---

## Social Comparison

Leon Festinger's **social comparison theory** says we evaluate ourselves by comparing to others.

- **Upward comparison** (to people above you) can inspire, or can make you feel inadequate.
- **Downward comparison** (to people below you) can boost your mood, but often by looking down on others ("Silver players are trash").

Wild Rift and its community serve up comparisons constantly: rank borders in loading screens, friends' ranks on your list, streamers in Challenger, win-rate sites. Comparison isn't avoidable, but you can choose **who** and **how**: comparing your current self to your self from three months ago tends to be more useful than comparing yourself to a pro.

## Ladder Anxiety

**Ladder anxiety** (or "ranked anxiety") is a common term for stress and avoidance around playing ranked. Signs include:

- Opening the app, looking at the ranked button, and playing a normal instead.
- Feeling sick or tense during champion select.
- Dodging lobbies frequently to avoid risk.
- Playing many unranked games "to warm up" and never getting to ranked.

From a psychology view, this is a classic **avoidance** pattern. Avoiding the feared situation reduces anxiety short-term, which reinforces avoiding it again. Over time the fear grows.

What tends to help (with anxiety in general, not just games):

- **Gradual exposure:** play one ranked game, then stop. Next session, two. Let your brain learn the outcome is survivable.
- **Shift the goal from outcome to process:** "this game I'll focus on warding" instead of "I need to win."
- **Lower the identity stakes:** remind yourself that rank is a sample of recent games, not a verdict on you.

## "Elo Hell" Thinking

"Elo hell" is the belief that you're stuck at a rank because your teammates are holding you back. Statistically, you are the one constant across all your games; your teammates and opponents change every match. Over enough games, if you're truly better than your rank, the math tends to move you up. (The *Rift Analytics* course works through why large samples matter.)

Psychologically, elo-hell thinking is the **self-serving bias** from Module 2 at a season-long scale. It protects self-esteem, but it **blocks improvement**, because the problem is always someone else.

That doesn't mean variance isn't real. Short-term streaks are often noise. The point is that across hundreds of games, your own play is the biggest lever you control.

---

## Mindsets About Ability

Carol Dweck's work on **fixed vs. growth mindset** suggests that people who believe ability can grow respond better to setbacks than those who believe ability is fixed.

- Fixed: "I'm just not good at mechanics." "Some people are naturally Diamond."
- Growth: "My mechanics aren't there *yet*." "Diamond players practiced things I haven't."

A note of caution: large meta-analyses (for example, by Victoria Sisk and colleagues) have found that growth-mindset **interventions** in schools have small average effects, bigger for some groups than others. So mindset isn't magic. But for gamers, the practical version holds up well: **treat rank as something you build through practice, not something you are.**

<TryIt title="Self-Worth Pie Chart">

If rank takes up a large slice, pick one other area you'd like to grow and write one small action for it this week. A broader base makes losses on the ladder hurt less.

<Pitfall title="Protecting Your Rank by Not Playing">

**Common mistake:** stopping ranked at a "good" rank to avoid losing it. Short-term this feels safe. Long-term it locks rank into your identity even more tightly and makes the eventual first loss feel bigger. If you enjoy ranked, play it. If you'd rather not, that's fine too, but make it a choice, not an anxiety-driven avoidance.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaways

- Rank can become part of **identity**, which makes losses feel like threats to the self.
- **Contingent self-worth** on rank makes self-esteem unstable.
- **Social comparison** is constant in-game; compare to your past self more than to others.
- **Ladder anxiety** is an avoidance cycle; gradual exposure and process goals help.
- **Elo-hell** thinking is the self-serving bias at scale; you are the constant across games.
- **Growth mindset** effects are modest in research, but treating rank as built, not fixed, is a useful frame.
