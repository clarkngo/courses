## The Question Behind Every "One More Game"

It's 11:40 p.m. You just lost a 22-minute game where your Baron got stolen. You know you have class or work tomorrow. You tap **Play** again anyway.

Psychology doesn't treat that as a lack of willpower. It asks a better question: **what is this game giving you that makes the next match feel worth it?**

The answer matters because the same need can be met in healthy or unhealthy ways. If you know which need is driving you, you can decide on purpose instead of on autopilot.

---

## Self-Determination Theory

**Self-determination theory (SDT)**, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, says people have three basic psychological needs. When an activity meets them, we feel motivated and energized. When it frustrates them, we feel drained.

| Need | What it means | How Wild Rift meets it |
|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Feeling effective, improving, mastering challenges | Landing a skillshot, winning lane, climbing a tier, seeing your KDA go up |
| **Autonomy** | Feeling that your choices are your own | Picking your champion, rune page, build path, and when to rotate |
| **Relatedness** | Feeling connected to others | Duo-queueing with a friend, a clutch save for a teammate, a club or guild |

Research by Andrew Przybylski, Scott Rigby, and Richard Ryan applied SDT to video games and found that games meeting these needs were the ones people enjoyed most and wanted to keep playing. It wasn't graphics or violence that predicted enjoyment. It was **need satisfaction**.

### The Flip Side: Need Frustration

The same game can **frustrate** each need:

- **Competence frustrated:** a losing streak, getting dove in lane every game, dropping a division.
- **Autonomy frustrated:** being forced into a role you hate, a teammate demanding you play a specific champion, feeling the matchmaker decided the game before it started.
- **Relatedness frustrated:** being flamed, ignored pings, a duo partner who blames you.

A lot of what we'll study in this course, from tilt to toxicity, is what happens when these needs are frustrated in real time.

## Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

SDT also separates two kinds of motivation:

- **Intrinsic motivation:** you play because the play itself is satisfying. The thrill of outplaying someone. Figuring out a matchup.
- **Extrinsic motivation:** you play for something outside the play. A rank badge, a skin from the event pass, a daily login reward, a friend's approval.

Most players have both. The problem is when extrinsic rewards **crowd out** intrinsic ones. If you only queue to finish an event mission, a loss doesn't just cost you a game; it costs you progress toward the reward. Games stop being fun and start being chores.

> Ask yourself: if ranked badges and event rewards disappeared tomorrow, would you still play? How much?

## What Different Players Want

Nick Yee's research on online-game players found that motivations cluster into broad groups:

1. **Achievement:** advancement, mastery of mechanics, competition.
2. **Social:** chatting, building relationships, teamwork.
3. **Immersion:** discovering the world, lore, role-playing, customization.

You can see all three in Wild Rift:

- The **achiever** grinds ranked and studies matchup data.
- The **socializer** mostly plays normals or ARAM with friends and cares more about the voice chat than the result.
- The **immersion** player collects skins, reads champion lore, and follows the Runeterra stories.

None of these is the "right" way to play. But mismatches cause friction: an achiever and a socializer in the same duo can have very different ideas of what a "good night" looks like.

## Why a Mobile MOBA Is Extra Sticky

Wild Rift has features that lower the friction of starting another game:

- **Short matches.** Games often wrap up in under 20 minutes, so "just one more" feels cheap.
- **The phone is always with you.** No need to be at a desk. Any spare 15 minutes is a possible game.
- **Fast feedback.** Every match ends with stats, rank changes, and rewards, a quick hit of competence information.

None of this is sinister on its own. It's good design. But it means the natural **stopping points** that a PC or console gives you (getting up from the desk, turning off the TV) are weaker. You have to build your own.

<Pitfall title="Calling It 'No Willpower'">

**Common mistake:** deciding you "just have no self-control" because you keep queuing. That framing doesn't help you change anything. A more useful question is: *which need is unmet right now, and is the next game likely to meet it?* After three losses in a row, competence is frustrated, and a fourth game while tired is more likely to frustrate it further. Knowing that makes it easier to stop.

</Pitfall>

## Key Takeaways

- **Self-determination theory** says we're drawn to activities that meet our needs for **competence, autonomy, and relatedness**.
- Wild Rift can **satisfy** and **frustrate** each of those needs, often in the same match.
- **Intrinsic** motivation is enjoying the play itself; **extrinsic** is playing for rewards. Too much extrinsic can crowd out fun.
- Players differ: **achievement, social, and immersion** motivations all show up on the Rift.
- Short mobile matches weaken natural stopping points, so you need to build your own.
