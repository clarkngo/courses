## You Can't Not Feel It, But You Can Steer It

Telling yourself "don't be mad" rarely works. Psychologist James Gross's **process model of emotion regulation** offers a better map. There are several points where you can shape an emotion:

1. **Situation selection:** choosing whether to be in the situation at all (Do I queue ranked tonight?)
2. **Situation modification:** changing the situation (muting a flamer)
3. **Attention deployment:** choosing where to look (the minimap, not the chat)
4. **Cognitive change (reappraisal):** changing what the situation *means*
5. **Response modulation:** changing your response once the emotion is already up (breathing, relaxing your grip)

Earlier points tend to be more effective and cost less effort. Let's go through tools at each stage.

---

## Before the Game: Situation Selection

The cheapest tilt to handle is the one you never queue into.

- Don't start ranked when you're already stressed from something else, hungry, or exhausted.
- If your last two games were losses and you felt your tilt signature, switch to a normal, ARAM, or a different activity.

This isn't quitting. It's choosing the conditions you perform best in, like an athlete who doesn't train hard on an injury.

---

## During the Game: Modify the Situation

### Mute Is a Tool, Not a Defeat

If a teammate is flaming, you can mute them. Some players feel this is "giving up information." In practice, a flaming player is rarely giving useful information, and reading their messages raises your arousal. Muting is **situation modification**: you change what reaches you.

Many experienced players mute all chat by default and rely on **pings**, which carry the useful signals (enemy missing, on my way, danger) without the emotional noise.

### Point Your Attention

When you notice yourself fixating on a teammate's mistake, give your attention a job: check the minimap, count the enemy's summoner cooldowns, track the next objective timer. Attention is limited. Filling it with useful tasks crowds out rumination.

---

## Reappraisal: Change the Story

**Reappraisal** means reinterpreting a situation so it has a different emotional meaning. Studies consistently find it works better over time than **suppression** (bottling it up and pretending you're fine), which tends to keep arousal high and use up mental resources.

| Hot appraisal | Reappraisal |
|---|---|
| "Our jungler is trolling." | "Our jungler is probably having a rough game. What can I do on my side?" |
| "I'm terrible, I died again." | "That death shows me their jungler pathed top. I'll ward the river." |
| "This game is lost." | "Their comp falls off. If we stall to late game, we have a chance." |
| "This is ruining my night." | "This is one game out of hundreds this season." |

Reappraisal isn't fake positivity. The new story should be **true**. It just picks a frame that keeps you useful.

---

## In-the-Moment Body Tools

When arousal is already high, calm the body directly.

- **Slow exhale breathing.** A few breaths where the exhale is longer than the inhale (for example, in for 4, out for 6). Slow breathing with long exhales is associated with lower physiological arousal. Do it while respawning or walking back to lane.
- **Release your grip.** Tension in the hands feeds back into feeling tense. Deliberately loosen your thumbs and shoulders during the death timer.
- **Name it.** Silently labeling the feeling ("I'm frustrated") has been linked in brain-imaging studies to reduced emotional reactivity. It also creates a small gap between you and the feeling.

The **death timer** is your built-in reset window. Use it for one breath and one label instead of typing in chat.

---

## If-Then Plans

Peter Gollwitzer's research on **implementation intentions** shows that specific if-then plans are much more likely to be followed than vague goals. "I'll stay calm" is vague. These are specific:

- *If* a teammate flames me, *then* I mute them immediately and ping my next objective.
- *If* I die twice to the same gank, *then* I ward before going back to lane.
- *If* I feel my grip tighten, *then* I take one long exhale on the next death or recall.

The plan does the thinking ahead of time, so hot cognition doesn't have to.

---

## Between Games: Stop Rules

A **stop rule** is a pre-commitment you make before your session starts. Common ones:

- **Two-loss rule:** after two ranked losses in a row, stop ranked for the session.
- **Tilt-signature rule:** if you notice your tilt signature after a game, take at least a 10-minute break off-screen.
- **Time cap:** set a timer; when it goes off, finish the current game and stop.

Make the rule **while you're calm**, and write it somewhere you'll see it. Rules made mid-tilt are almost always "one more."

<Pitfall title="Venting in Chat to 'Let It Out'">

**Common mistake:** thinking that typing angry messages releases the pressure. Research on venting suggests it usually doesn't reduce anger and can keep you stewing on it. It also lowers your team's performance and invites flame back. If you need to vent, do it *after* the game, out loud, away from teammates, or better yet, write a one-line note about what you'd do differently.

</Pitfall>

---

## Key Takeaways

- Emotion can be shaped at several stages: **situation, attention, meaning, and response**. Earlier is usually easier.
- **Muting** and **pings** are situation-modification tools.
- **Reappraisal** generally works better than suppression, as long as the new frame is true.
- Use the **death timer** for slow exhales, grip release, and labeling the emotion.
- **If-then plans** and **stop rules** do the thinking for you before tilt arrives.
